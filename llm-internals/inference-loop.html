Part Four — Inference at Scale

The Inference Loop

One choice is now fully specified — scale, squash, cut, draw. A reply is that choice run in a loop, and the loop has a shape you can price. After this page you can say where a reply's waiting time goes: which phase eats it, what sets the pace of each, and why you wait for the first piece, then watch the rest flow.

The loop, end to end

Start from the sampling page's end. The model has produced a probability distribution for the next piece; you draw one. Now what? The drawn piece is appended to the text, and everything runs again — that is the whole trick. Decode is the name for this loop: turning a prompt into generated pieces, one draw and one forward pass at a time [inference].

Walk one reply end to end [inference]. The prompt arrives as text and is tokenized into pieces. One forward pass runs over all of them — every piece of the question processed together. The distribution at the last position is taken, one piece is sampled from it, and the piece is appended. Then the loop proper: a forward pass — at minimum over the new piece alone — produces the next distribution, the next piece is drawn and appended, and so on.

Two things end the loop. The model can draw eos — the end-of-sequence piece, a special token that means "this reply is finished" — or the host can cut it at a length cap [inference]. Until one of those happens, the loop has no reason to stop.

Notice what is absent: a plan. No outline is drafted first, no sentence sketched and revised. Each step is one forward pass conditioned on everything so far — the question, and every piece the model itself has already produced. Every generated piece becomes part of the input for the next step [inference]. The loop is also bounded behind itself: it cannot condition on more than the context window, the maximum number of pieces the model can see at once — GPT-3's was 2,048 pieces; Llama 3 ships at 8K and extends to 128K [inference].

What arrives at your screen

What does the user actually see? Not the loop — its output, delivered as it runs. Pieces are byte strings; detokenization turns each into text as it arrives, so a long reply can begin rendering before it finishes generating [inference].

One wrinkle: pieces do not respect character boundaries. A multi-byte character — an accented letter, most non-Latin scripts — can be split across two piece ids, its bytes divided between them. A streaming renderer must buffer until a complete character arrives, or the user sees half a glyph flash before its other half [inference].

The delivery mechanism is ordinary web plumbing: one long-lived response in the event-stream style, the server pushing small chunks as they are ready, each chunk carrying the text of roughly one piece. The app glues the deltas together as they land [inference].

Two kinds of work

Now price the loop. Its work comes in two shapes, so different that they get separate names. Prefill is the opening forward pass over the whole prompt — every piece of the question processed in one go [inference]. Everything after it is the decode phase: one piece per forward pass, repeated until the reply ends [inference].

Part Two's GPU page named the two regimes, and they map exactly. Prefill is compute-bound: it reads the weights once and does arithmetic for every prompt position on that single read, so the arithmetic units set the pace. Decode is memory-bound: it reads all the weights again for every single piece, so the memory system sets the pace [gpus].

The asymmetry is extreme at real sizes. A prefill of a thousand pieces takes tens of milliseconds on 7B-class hardware; each decode step costs at least 6.87 ms — the bandwidth floor for a lone sequence [inference]. A two-hundred-piece reply on that setup: roughly 1.4 s of decode against roughly fifty milliseconds of prefill — about ninety-seven percent of the wait is the decode phase [inference]. The asymmetry even shows up in price lists: commercial APIs bill a completed piece at several times the rate of a prompt piece, because each one costs a full weight sweep [inference].

The two timers

Users feel the loop through two numbers. TTFT — time to first token; the field says token, this site says piece, same thing — is how long the user waits before the first piece appears [inference]. TPOT — time per output token — is the pace of every piece after the first [inference].

TTFT is dominated by prefill; TPOT is the decode step time [gpus]. With streaming, latency — the felt delay between asking and seeing anything — is the wait for the first byte: TTFT plus the network [inference]. After that, a healthy decode pace outruns reading; the reply feels like it is being typed by something faster than you.

Both timers move with the request's shape. A longer prompt stretches TTFT, because prefill arithmetic grows with the prompt; a longer reply multiplies the decode phase, because every extra piece is another full weight sweep [gpus]. Ask a book-length question and the wait before the first piece grows; ask for a book-length answer and the trickle after it does.

The weight sweep, and a hidden cost

Memory-bound was a claim from the GPU page; here is the arithmetic under it. Each decode step must read every active weight at least once — two bytes per parameter in the half-precision format the model was trained in. The time for one step is therefore at least the weight bytes divided by the card's memory bandwidth, and the ceiling pace is the bandwidth divided by the bytes [gpus]. Run it for the standard cards [gpus]:

Worked example — the pace ceiling

the pace ceiling: one weight sweep per piece
(research/gpus.md — verified arithmetic):

  7B model, fp16:  13.04 GiB = 14e9 bytes
  A100:  14e9 / 2.039e12 B/s = 6.87 ms per piece
         -> 146 pieces/s ceiling
  H100:  14e9 / 3.35e12 B/s  = 4.18 ms per piece
         -> 239 pieces/s ceiling
  70B model, fp16: 130.4 GiB
         -> does not fit one 80 GB card at all

  (real deployments reach ~60-80% of these)

Real deployments reach sixty to eighty percent of those ceilings — framework overhead, attention bookkeeping, everything that is not the weight read itself. Measured systems serve a 7B model at fifty to one hundred fifty pieces per second on A100-class hardware [gpus]. And the arithmetic units — the ones the spec sheet brags about — sit nearly idle: a 7B forward pass is about 1.4e10 FLOPs riding on a 14 GB read, roughly one operation per byte against a machine whose balance point sits hundreds of times higher [gpus].

One reply, priced to the millisecond. For a thousand-piece prompt and a two-hundred-piece reply on 7B-class hardware [inference]:

Worked example — the bill for one reply

the bill for one reply
(7B model on an A100; 1,000-piece prompt,
200-piece reply):

  prefill:  one pass over 1,000 pieces    ~ 50 ms
  decode:   200 steps x 6.87 ms          ~ 1.4 s
                                        --------
  total                                 ~ 1.45 s

  decode's share: ~97% of the wait

This accounting still hides a cost. Attention: each new piece must look at every earlier piece, and looking requires the keys and values those positions produced — work the forward pass already did, once, during prefill and during every decode step since. The naive loop throws that work away and redoes it, every step, for everything so far [inference]. The fix is not to compute faster but to remember: keep what the earlier passes produced, and let each new step consult it. That memory — what it holds, what it costs, and why it grows with every piece — is the next page.

Takeaways

  • A reply is the sampling decision looped: forward pass, take the last position's distribution, draw, append, repeat until eos or a length cap — no plan, every step conditioned on everything so far.
  • Generation has two shapes of work: prefill — one forward pass over the whole prompt, compute-bound — and the decode phase — one piece per pass, memory-bound.
  • TTFT is dominated by prefill; TPOT is the decode pace; latency to first byte is TTFT plus network. A longer prompt stretches the first, a longer reply multiplies the second.
  • Decode's floor is the weight sweep: every active weight read once per piece, so the ceiling pace is memory bandwidth divided by weight bytes — 146 pieces per second for a 7B model on an A100.
  • At a lone sequence the arithmetic units sit nearly idle; the memory system is the whole game — which is why the next page's memory, the KV cache, is worth a page of its own.
