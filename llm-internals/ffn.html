The floor's other half

The counting at the end of the last page landed unevenly, and the imbalance is the pointer. The gathering half of one 8B floor holds about forty-two million entries; the other half of the same floor holds over four times more. This page is about that heavier half.

It gets a plain name for a plain job. The FFN — the feed-forward network — is the floor's per-position half: a short stack of learned table multiplies applied to each position's vector on its own — widen to a bigger middle, bend, narrow back [components]. The name is inherited from the older network designs this architecture replaced; all it means here is that the transform runs straight through each position, with nothing looping back.

Per-position means exactly what it says. If the text is a hundred pieces long, the gathering half computes a hundred intertwined gathers — every score grid couples the pieces. This half runs the same multiplies on each of the hundred vectors, one at a time, blind to the others. Shuffle the pieces before this half and every output vector shuffles with them, unchanged.

Plain, but not redundant. The gathering half moves information between positions; this half decides what to make of it, position by position. In the finished machine, every cross-piece structure lives in the gather — and every per-position transformation of any depth lives here [components].

Widen, bend, narrow

The original recipe, from the paper that introduced the architecture, is two learned tables with a fixed bend between them: FFN(x) = max(0, xW1 + b1) W2 + b2, with the middle width set to four times the working width [components]. In the original model itself: working width 512, middle width 2048 [model-configs].

The bend is the activation function: a fixed, unlearned rule applied to every entry of the widened vector between the two multiplies — max(0, x) in the original, called ReLU: negatives go to zero, positives pass through [components].

The middle width is the design's main dial. The expansion ratio is how many times wider the middle is than the working width — four in the original design [components].

Why is the bend load-bearing? Arithmetic:

Worked example — what the bend prevents

two table multiplies with nothing between them:

  (x @ W1) @ W2   ==   x @ (W1 @ W2)

  whatever the two tables learn separately, the pair acts
  exactly like the single combined table (W1 @ W2) — one
  multiply. depth buys nothing unless something nonlinear
  sits between the multiplies

Without it, any run of table multiplies collapses into a single table, and depth would buy nothing. The bend is small — one fixed curve per entry — but it is the only thing keeping two tables from becoming one. It is also where this half's nonlinearity lives. (The gather has its own nonlinear step — the softmax — but that one acts across positions.)

And why widen at all? Because the middle is where the floor keeps its per-position capacity: more entries there means more distinct transformations available before the results are squeezed back down. How much width is worth buying is a budget question with an empirical answer — it waits in Part Two.

The modern recipe: gate, up, down

Modern models replace the fixed bend with a learned gate. SwiGLU is the three-table recipe: SwiGLU(x) = (Swish(x W_gate) ⊙ x W_up) W_down, where ⊙ multiplies entry-by-entry and Swish(x) = x · sigmoid(βx) is a smooth ReLU with β usually one [components].

Read it as three moves. The up table widens, exactly as in the original design. The gate table also widens, then passes through Swish, which leaves each entry as a multiplier between roughly zero and one. The entry-by-entry multiply combines the two wide rows: the gate decides, per entry, how much of the up-projection gets through. Then the down table narrows back to the working width.

The difference from the original bend is worth stating plainly. ReLU's shape was the same for every input. The gate's shape depends on the input — the model computes half of its own bend. "How much of this entry passes" stops being a fixed rule and becomes a learned, input-dependent question.

Three tables where the original had two means the width budget shifts to keep the entry count comparable: an expansion ratio of eight-thirds in the Llama 2 family — rounded to a multiple of 256, which lands at 11008 for the 7B — and three and a half in Llama 3's 8B: an exact 14336 [components].

Worked example — middle widths, three designs

middle widths at working width 4096:

  original recipe (2 tables, ratio 4):     d_ff = 16384
  Llama 2 7B  (3 tables, ratio 8/3):       d_ff = 11008
  Llama 3 8B  (3 tables, ratio 3.5):       d_ff = 14336

  entry check: 3 * (8/3) = 8 = 2 * 4 — at matched budget,
  three tables at eight-thirds cost what two cost at four

The arithmetic behind those ratios: three tables at eight-thirds cost what two tables cost at four — the three-table recipe did not buy a bigger floor, it bought a smarter bend at the same price.

Roughly four fifths of every floor's entries sit in the per-position half. The model's numbers live here. That also explains a choice from the last page in reverse: sharing key-value heads trimmed the gathering half precisely because it was the cheap half — the expensive half was never on the table.

How the floors stack, and what the whole model totals once thirty-two of them are counted alongside the embedding and the output table, is the next page's arithmetic. One position at a time

Close on the independence, because it is the architectural dividing line [components]: the same tables apply to every position's vector — separately, identically. The middle row computed for position zero and the one computed for position nine hundred come from the same tables. No cross-position anything happens in this half; all of it lives in the gather.

So a floor is two halves with two jobs: a cross-position gather, then a per-position transform. What is still missing is the wiring — the scaling steps before each half, the add-backs after each. How those connect, and why they are what let floors stack deep, is the next page. After that, the machine is assembled — and Part Two can turn it on: what number says it is wrong, and how that number reaches every table.

Takeaways

  • The FFN is the floor's per-position half: table multiplies applied to each position independently; all cross-position work lives in the gather.
  • Its shape is widen, bend, narrow. Without the bend, a run of multiplies collapses into one table — the activation function is what makes depth count.
  • The modern bend is learned and input-dependent: SwiGLU's gate row decides, per entry, how much of the up-projection passes; then the down table narrows.
  • The expansion ratio sets the middle width: four in the 2017 design, eight-thirds rounded in Llama 2, three and a half — 14336 at width 4096 — in Llama 3's 8B.
  • About four fifths of a floor's entries live in this half — roughly 176 million of 218 million in the 8B model. Next: the wiring that stacks the floors.
