Part Two — Training

Scaling Laws and Chinchilla

After this page you can price a training run in one multiplication, state the law the loss obeys as budgets grow, and say why the most-served models deliberately train past the point that law calls optimal.

Counting the run

The last page left the machine specified — chips, speeds, a cost a multiplication away. Do the multiplication. A forward pass over n pieces of text costs about 2nN operations — roughly nN multiply-adds at two operations apiece, with N the entry count; the lookups and the softmax are rounding errors next to the tables. The backward walk roughly doubles the arithmetic, so one training step on a batch of B pieces costs about 6BN, and a whole run that processes D pieces in total costs C = 6ND: entries, text, six [training].

The formula is worth having because it checks against published runs [training]:

Worked example — pricing three real runs

the arithmetic, three published runs
(total compute C = 6 x N x D):

  GPT-3:
    6 x 175B entries x 300B pieces    = 3.15e23 ops
                                         (paper: 3.14e23)

  Chinchilla:
    6 x  70B entries x 1.4T pieces    =  5.9e23 ops

  Llama 3 405B:
    6 x 405B entries x 15.6T pieces   = 3.79e25 ops
                                         (paper:  3.8e25)

Three multiplications, checkable with a pocket calculator — that is what it means for training cost to be knowable before a single step runs. The total spend is fixed by two choices made in advance: how many entries, and how much text.

One more reading of the formula, because it hides two honest approximations. The six counts arithmetic only — the table multiplies of the forward pass plus the doubled bill of the backward walk — and treats the lookups and the softmax as rounding errors, which at these sizes they are. And it counts arithmetic at the chips' theoretical pace, which no real run sustains: the efficiency shave from scheduling, communication, and the memory bill is a separate factor, and it lands where the last section of this page measures it — well under half of peak in practice.

The law

So budget is a dial. What does a dial buy? The field's first empirical answer is a law. Scaling laws: across many orders of magnitude, the loss falls as a power law in entries, in text, and in compute — every equal multiplicative step in budget buys the same fractional drop in loss [training]. The original finding, published in 2020 from hundreds of training runs, also carried practical guidance: grow the entry count faster than the text [training].

Hold the law's shape in mind — a straight, gently falling line on log-log axes. It says two things at once. The good news: spending works, predictably, far past where intuition would guess saturation; no wall appeared at any scale measured. The sobering news: the line is straight, not plunging. The fractional drop per budget step is constant, so each further halving of loss costs more than the last one did. Improvement is smooth — and it never becomes free.

Chinchilla: the even split

The law prices spending, but it did not at first say how to split a fixed budget between entries and text — a bigger model on less text, or a smaller model on more? The 2022 answer that reset the field's economics: for a fixed compute budget, the loss-minimizing split grows entries and text in equal proportion. Chinchilla-optimal is the name for training at that balance, and the working rule of thumb is about twenty pieces of text per entry [training].

How was the balance found? Not by theory — by survey. Train many models at many splits of one budget, read each one's loss, and the floor of the family traces the answer. Too few entries and the text is wasted: the model lacks the room to store what it reads. Too little text and the entries are wasted: room with nothing learned to put in it. The balance point, where neither waste dominates, is the equal split the survey found.

The demonstration that made the name famous: Chinchilla, seventy billion entries trained on one point four trillion pieces, set against Gopher, four times larger, at the same total budget — and the smaller, better-read model won every benchmark in the report [training]:

Worked example — one budget, two shapes

same training budget, two shapes:

  Gopher:      280B entries
  Chinchilla:   70B entries, 1.4T pieces of text
                (a quarter of the entries,
                 twenty pieces of text per entry)

  result: the smaller, better-read model wins
  every benchmark in the report
  (MMLU: 67.5 vs 60.0)

By this measure GPT-3 itself had been under-read: roughly three hundred billion pieces against a hundred seventy-five billion entries — under two pieces per entry [training]. The era's lesson was that the field had been building oversized models and starving them of text.

Deliberately past the optimum

Then practice diverged from the optimum — on purpose. The 8B model followed through Part One read roughly fifteen trillion pieces: about eighteen hundred pieces per entry, nearly a hundred times past the twenty-per-entry rule [training]. Not an accident. The Chinchilla balance minimizes the training bill and ignores the running bill. A widely used model is read far more often than it is trained: once trained, thousands of chips run it for years, and the cost of generating text shrinks with the entry count. Overtrain a small model on abundant text, and every later read is cheaper [training].

One more idea completes the economics. A model's knowledge is parametric knowledge — stored in the entries as compressed statistics of the corpus, retrieved from nowhere at run time. Part Two established this from the objective's side: nothing is looked up; the entries are the memory. From the budget's side, the same fact reads as a compression ratio: how much corpus each entry must carry. Reading an entry-dense model on far more text packs more into every entry — which is why overtraining buys quality per serving dollar even past the training-optimal point.

Worked example — text per entry, three eras

pieces of text per entry:

  GPT-3:              300B / 175B  =     1.7
  the balanced rule:                ~    20
  the 8B model:       15T  /   8B  =  1,875

What the money buys

What does a modern run cost, concretely? The largest run with published accounting: DeepSeek-V3, at 2.788 million chip-hours on H800s for 14.8 trillion pieces of text [training]. The same accounting yields the efficiency figure: the chips ran at roughly thirty-seven percent of their theoretical peak across the whole run — the unavoidable shave that real scheduling, communication between split tables, and the backward walk's memory bill take out [training].

And what the money buys is stranger than a smooth return. The loss falls smoothly — that is the law — but capabilities arrive in jumps: behaviors absent at one scale and present at another, with nothing in the smooth loss curve announcing them. The original GPT-3 paper's own example was in-context learning — the ability to follow examples placed in the text itself, with no update to the entries — arriving as a threshold effect of scale [training]. Budget on the smooth curve; measure on the jumpy one.

That closes Part Two's economics. The machine is built (Part One), charged, moved, and run on silicon at a knowable price (this part). What exists at the end of the run is still a raw text-predictor — brilliant at continuing a page, useless as an assistant. Ask it a question and it may well answer with more questions, because that is what a corpus looks like where questions are usually followed by more questions. Turning the predictor into the assistant is the first page of Part Three.

Takeaways

  • One multiplication prices a run: C = 6ND — entries times pieces times six — and it checks against published runs to within rounding (GPT-3, Chinchilla, Llama 3 405B).
  • Scaling laws: the loss falls as a power law in budget — smooth and predictable over orders of magnitude, but each fractional drop costs more than the last.
  • Chinchilla: for a fixed budget, grow entries and text together, about twenty pieces per entry — the quarter-size, better-read model beat Gopher on every benchmark reported.
  • Serving flips the optimum: a widely used model is read more than it is trained, so the 8B model overtrains to ~1,875 pieces per entry and wins on total cost.
  • Knowledge is parametric — compressed corpus statistics in the entries, nothing retrieved — and the loss falls smoothly while capabilities arrive in jumps.
