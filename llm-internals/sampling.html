Sampling: Choosing the Next Token

After this page you can say exactly what happens between the machine's final scores and the piece that appears on your screen — what temperature does to the numbers, why the always-best choice loops, and how the tail of the distribution gets cut.

One distribution, one decision

Post-training shaped the distribution; it never chose from it. Name the choosing. Sampling is the rule that turns the model's probability distribution at one position into the single piece that comes out — the draw from the distribution [inference].

The raw material first. The forward pass ends, at every position, in a score for each piece of the vocabulary — the output table's row for that position, unbounded numbers. The softmax turns scores into probabilities: exponentiate each, divide by the sum. One mechanical detail matters for correctness: subtract the largest score before exponentiating — exponentials of large numbers overflow, and the subtraction changes nothing, because the same factor divides out of every term [inference].

With probabilities in hand, the choice is a decision with knobs, and the knobs are the whole of this page. Every assistant runs the same pipeline — scale, squash, cut, draw:

How is a "draw" actually made? Not with a spinning wheel. The probabilities partition the stretch between zero and one: the first piece owns the opening slice, the next owns the slice that follows, and so on until the whole interval is spent. The machine draws one uniform random number inside that interval — every point equally likely — and takes whichever piece owns the slice where the number landed. A piece holding sixty percent of the mass owns sixty percent of the interval, so it comes up about six times in ten. That is all "random" means here: one uniform number, looked up in a table of slices.

The certain choice and its failure

The first rule to try is the natural one: always take the most likely piece. Greedy decoding — the argmax at every position, no chance involved [inference]. It is fast, it is deterministic, and it fails in a characteristic way.

It repeats. Take the best piece, and the text just built makes the same piece the best again: "I am a robot. I am a robot." — the documented loop of best-choice generation on open-ended text [inference]. The companion script shows the same failure in miniature: its greedy run on the toy emits the</w> c the</w> c . ate</w> . ate</w> ate</w> . — ten pieces, cycling through four distinct ones; the certain choice is a repeating pattern [inference].

That failure teaches this page's real lesson: sampling is not a creativity feature bolted on for variety. It is an anti-degeneracy mechanism. The deterministic rule collapses into repetition precisely because it is deterministic — the second "I am a robot" is the most likely continuation of the first. Chance is what breaks the loop [inference].

The temperature dial

Temperature is the first knob: divide the scores by a number T before the softmax [inference]. Below one, the distribution sharpens — the biggest score grows relatively bigger. Above one, it flattens — the mass spreads toward even. Pushed toward zero, temperature becomes the certain choice; pushed large, the coin-flip chorus. Nothing about the model changes — the same scores, rescaled; the dial only changes how much of the learned shape survives the softmax.

  T = 0.5:  [0.842, 0.114, 0.042, 0.002]   sharper

  T = 2.0:  [0.434, 0.263, 0.205, 0.097]   flatter

  (T toward zero: the top piece takes all.
   T large: near-even.)

Read the direction, not the exact numbers: halving the temperature moved the top piece from about sixty percent to about eighty-four; doubling it spread the mass until the top piece held less than half [inference]. When an assistant product exposes a "creativity" setting, this is the dial being turned.

Cutting the tail

Even a well-shaped distribution has a tail worth fearing. At temperature one, a trained model assigns some probability to every piece of a fifty-thousand-piece vocabulary — including pieces no fluent writer would place next. Draw often enough and the tail will speak: the wrong-but-plausible word, the broken half-piece, the pivot into nonsense [inference].

Two ways to cut. Top-k: keep the k most likely pieces, renormalize, and draw from those — a fixed count, whatever the distribution looks like [inference]. Top-p — usually called nucleus sampling — keeps the smallest set of pieces whose probabilities add up to at least p, renormalizes, and draws — a fixed mass, whatever the count [inference].

The same distribution, cut both ways:

Worked example — one distribution, cut two ways

the same distribution, cut two ways
(start: [0.609, 0.224, 0.136, 0.030]):

  top-k, k = 2:
    keep [0.609, 0.224], renormalize:
    -> [0.731, 0.269]          (a fixed count)

  top-p, p = 0.9:
    smallest set reaching 0.9:
    0.609 + 0.224 + 0.136 = 0.969
    -> [0.628, 0.231, 0.140]   (a fixed mass)

The empirical finding behind the field's preference: on natural text the mass concentrates — the set carrying ninety percent of the probability is often only a few hundred pieces of the whole vocabulary, so cutting below it removes the incoherent tail while leaving the real choices intact [inference]. GPT-3 shipped with top-p at 0.9 as its default for generation [inference].

In practice the knobs compose in one order. Temperature first, on the score scale; then the cut; then the draw. Top-k could run on either scale — the softmax preserves order, so the ranking of pieces is the same before and after — but top-p is meaningless before the squash: "the set holding ninety percent of the mass" presupposes a mass. The knobs also interact predictably: a cold temperature already empties much of the tail, so the cut matters less; a hot one refills it, so the cut does more work. A conservative assistant runs a temperature below one with a modest cut; a playful one runs nearer one and cuts harder. None of this is learned — the scores are fixed at this point; the knobs belong to the operator, not the trainer.

The road not taken

One more rule belongs here, because it is the one a programmer invents first. Beam search: keep not the single best piece but the b best partial texts; extend every one of them at each step; drop the least promising; finish with the highest-probability whole text. A search of fixed width for the best sequence — the natural way to make the certain choice less shortsighted [inference].

It is the right rule for the wrong objective. Beam search optimizes the probability of the whole sequence — the criterion of tasks that have one best answer, like translation. Open-ended generation has no single best answer, and the sequences it finds are the repetitive, safest ones: the same degeneration as the certain choice, arrived at more thoroughly [inference]. The working recipe for open-ended text is the simpler one — a temperature, a cut, a draw.

The choice is now fully specified. But one choice is not a reply. A reply is this decision repeated — piece after piece, each new piece fed back in as input, the distribution re-formed at every step — and inside that repetition live two very different kinds of work, with different costs. The loop itself, and its two phases, are the next page.

Takeaways

  • Sampling is the rule that turns one position's probability distribution into the single piece that appears; the pipeline is scale, squash, cut, draw.
  • Greedy decoding — the argmax every step — loops, because the repeated text is its own most likely continuation; chance is an anti-degeneracy mechanism, not a creativity extra.
  • Temperature divides the scores before the softmax: below one sharpens, above one flattens, zero is the certain choice — the same model, a different shape.
  • Top-k keeps a fixed count, top-p (the nucleus) a fixed mass; on natural text the ninety-percent set is often a few hundred pieces, so the cut removes the incoherent tail and keeps the real choices.
  • Beam search hunts the highest-probability whole text — right when one best answer exists, degenerate on open-ended generation; the working recipe is temperature, a cut, a draw.
