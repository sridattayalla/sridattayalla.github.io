Part Two — Training

GPUs: Where the Math Lives

After this page you can say what a GPU is in two sentences, why fetching a number costs far more than multiplying it, and why that one asymmetry explains both training's memory bill and the speed limits of generating text.

The chip the math runs on

The training step is now fully specified — charge, walk, move — and it is enormous: hundreds of quadrillions of multiply-adds per step, for millions of steps, on tables that must be fetched from somewhere. The somewhere, and the machine doing the multiplying, is this page. This is not a detour into hardware for its own sake: the physical facts here are what every later choice — batch size, model size, serving speed — bends around.

Strip a GPU to what matters for this work. It is a chip with thousands of small arithmetic units running in lockstep, and its whole design bets on one shape of work: the same operation, applied to enormous piles of numbers at once. Learned-table multiplies are exactly that shape — every entry of an input vector meets every column of a table — which is why the machine built for graphics became the machine this field runs on. The peak rates are quoted for the chip's tensor core units, the ones that perform matrix products rather than isolated multiplies [gpus].

Around the arithmetic sits memory in two tiers, and the tier distinction will explain most of this page. HBM — high-bandwidth memory — is the large store: tens of gigabytes stacked beside the processing units on the same package, where a model's entries live while work runs (80 GB on an A100, the same on an H100) [gpus]. SRAM is the other tier: the small, much faster memory embedded right beside the arithmetic units — far too small to hold a model, fast enough to keep the units fed once data reaches it.

The asymmetry that rules everything

Now the two speeds, and the ratio between them that rules everything downstream. Moving bytes from HBM to the arithmetic happens at a fixed rate — the chip's memory bandwidth: about 2,039 gigabytes per second on an A100, about 3,350 on an H100 [gpus]. Arithmetic is counted in FLOPs — floating-point operations, one multiply or one add each — and the same chips perform, on table-shaped work, roughly 312 trillion and 990 trillion of those per second respectively [gpus].

Put the two together and the asymmetry appears: for every byte the chip fetches, it can perform on the order of a couple hundred operations — by the datasheets' arithmetic, computing on a number is roughly one hundred fifty to six hundred times cheaper than fetching it [gpus]. Read that as a rule of economics: fetched bytes are the scarce currency; arithmetic is nearly free by comparison. Work that reuses each fetched entry many times runs at the arithmetic speed. Work that fetches every entry to use it once runs at the memory speed — and the memory speed is the slow one.

Two regimes of work

The two situations have names. Work is compute-bound when its time is set by the number of operations — which happens when each fetched entry feeds many operations. Work is memory-bound when its time is set by fetching — which happens when each fetched entry feeds few. The two shapes of running a language model land one in each regime, and the split is stark.

Reading a text in — hundreds or thousands of positions at once — is compute-bound: every table is fetched once and then applied at every position, so one fetch serves the whole text, and the arithmetic units set the pace. Generating text — one piece at a time — is memory-bound: to produce a single piece, every table in the machine must be fetched in full, used once, and thrown away; the pipe sets the pace and the arithmetic units sit idle. Part Four builds the entire serving story on this split; the speed numbers wait there.

The asymmetry also names the two escape hatches, which is why they work: shrink the bytes per entry — fewer bytes per fetch means more entries per second through the same pipe — and share each fetch across many sequences at once, so one fetch serves many users. Both attack the scarce resource directly, and both get their own page in Part Four.

Training's memory bill

Back to training, whose bill is dominated by the same hierarchy. The last page established that the backward walk must hold every intermediate value; add the update rule's own needs, and the full cost per entry comes into view. Training runs in mixed precision: the forward and backward walks compute in two-byte numbers, while the master copy of every entry — and the two running averages the update keeps for it — sit in four-byte precision [gpus]:

Worked example — the bill for one entry

the memory bill for one entry, in training
(mixed precision, AdamW-family update):

  the entry itself, two bytes          2
  its slope, two bytes                 2
  master copy, four bytes              4
  running average of its slope         4
  running average of squared size      4
                                      ---
  total                               16 bytes

  a 7B-entry model:   ~14 GB of entries alone,
                      ~112 GB to train on

Sixteen bytes per entry [gpus]. The 7B model — fourteen billion bytes of entries in two-byte dress [gpus] — occupies about a hundred twelve billion bytes in training: the entries alone are one-eighth of the bill. An eighty-gigabyte chip cannot hold it before any work begins.

One trade exists between the two currencies. Gradient checkpointing: do not store every intermediate value from the forward pass — recompute them during the backward walk instead. The trade costs roughly a third more arithmetic and buys a large cut in the memory the walk needs [gpus]. When the memory bill, not the operation count, is what caps the run, this is the standard lever.

One model, many chips

And when one chip cannot hold the model at all, the model is split — along the three natural axes. Tensor parallel splits each table multiply across chips: every chip holds a slice of every table, and the slices' partial results are combined after each floor, which demands a fast interconnect between chips. Pipeline parallel splits the floors: one chip owns the first run of floors, the next chip owns the following run, and work flows through the machine in stages — kept busy by feeding many small batches one after another. Data parallel splits the batches: several identical copies of the whole model each work a different batch, and the copies combine their slopes after every step; the modern sharded variants go further, each chip holding a slice of the entries rather than a full copy [gpus].

The largest runs use every axis at once. The biggest of the Llama 3 family — over four hundred billion entries — trained on sixteen thousand H100s, with the batches, the floors, the tables, and even the long sequences themselves split across the machine [gpus].

So the machine exists, its speeds are datasheet facts, and its cost is a multiplication away: chips times hours. Given the machine, the question that remains is the oldest one in engineering — how big to build, and how long to run, before the next dollar is better spent elsewhere. The field's empirical answer to that question, and the budget philosophy that followed it, is the next page.

Takeaways

  • A GPU is thousands of lockstep arithmetic units plus a two-tier memory: small fast storage embedded beside the units, and tens of gigabytes of HBM where the model's entries live.
  • The ruling asymmetry: per byte fetched, the chip can perform on the order of a couple hundred operations — fetched bytes are the scarce currency, arithmetic nearly free.
  • Two regimes: reading a whole text at once is compute-bound (one fetch serves every position); generating one piece at a time is memory-bound (every table fetched per piece).
  • Training's bill is sixteen bytes per entry in mixed precision — a 7B model's ~14 GB of entries becomes ~112 GB in training dress; gradient checkpointing trades a third more compute for a large memory cut.
  • Beyond one chip: split the tables (tensor parallel), the floors (pipeline parallel), or the batches (data parallel) — the largest runs use all three, on sixteen-thousand-chip clusters.
