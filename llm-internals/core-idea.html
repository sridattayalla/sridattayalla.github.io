One job, no exceptions

The previous page ended on a promise: the model scores every possible next chunk. Take that seriously, because it is not a simplification — it is the specification. At every moment of its existence, a trained model does exactly one thing: it looks at the pieces of text it has been given and produces a score for every piece in its fixed list. Not a plan for the sentence. Not an opinion. Scores — one per candidate — and then something outside the model picks one and asks again.

One subtlety before going further: the list of candidate pieces is the same list the input was cut into. The model reads pieces and writes pieces, drawing from one shared menu. A consequence you can predict already: a language model can never emit a word that its list cannot represent — not "cannot think of it", but structurally cannot print it. Everything you have ever seen one of these models output was assembled from that menu, one entry at a time.

There is no other goal in the box. When the output looks like reasoning, that is the score list doing the reasoning's job; when it looks confident, the scores were lopsided. Hold onto that austerity. Everything in the next seven pages exists to make this one scoring operation good enough to be mistaken for thought.

The model is a pile of numbers

But what is the scorer? Strip the mystique: a model is numbers. A parameter is one learned number — a single entry in one of the model's many grids of them. The weights are the pile as a whole: every grid, every entry. Models are named by how many they carry: GPT-3 holds 175 billion parameters, arranged among other things into 96 identical floors [model-configs].

Each floor is called a layer: the same small computation, repeated, each pass refining what the previous one wrote. The overall design — repeated floors plus the machinery for reading text in and scores out — is called a transformer. The name is historical; the page that assembles the floors tells that story.

These numbers are not written by hand and stored in no one's spreadsheet. They start random and are nudged, over months of compute, until the scoring gets good. That nudging process is Part Two of this manual. Until then, treat the weights as a given: a huge, fixed pile of numbers you are allowed to multiply with.

One run: the forward pass

Now the operation itself. A forward pass is one complete run: the pieces so far go in, and the score list comes out. Inside, it is only multiplication and addition — trillions of multiply-adds in a large model, but nothing more exotic. The scores arrive in a raw form called logits: one number per candidate piece, unbounded, meaningless alone. A logit is not a percentage and has no units; only the comparison between logits carries information — one candidate beating another by a wide margin means the model strongly prefers the first.

The final step turns logits into shares. Softmax is the function that does it: raise e (the calculus constant) to each logit, then divide each result by their total. Watch it happen on three candidates for the blank:

Worked example — softmax by hand

context: "the cat sat on the ___"

candidate       mat      sofa     moon
logit           2.0      1.0      0.5
e^logit         7.39     2.72     1.65     (e = 2.71828...; e^0.5 = 1.64872...)
total                    11.76
share           0.63     0.23     0.14     (each e^logit divided by the total)

The output list — one share per candidate, every share between zero and one, all shares adding to exactly one — is a probability distribution. It is the model's full opinion, hedged: shares almost never reach zero, so every nonsense candidate keeps a sliver. Softmax is monotone: the bigger logit always gets the bigger share, so the ranking survives the conversion. What changes is the spacing — near-equal logits come out near-equal, and a large gap becomes a landslide.

Here is a prediction you can make right now: what does the distribution look like with random weights? Near-uniform. Every candidate gets almost the same share, because nothing in the pile of numbers has learned to prefer anything. Training is the process that turns that flat hedging into lopsided opinion — the entire art of Part Two is measuring and shaping the tilt. The tilt is all that separates a random number generator in a transformer's clothes from a system that finishes your sentence.

Watch out

The classic misread is treating logits as probabilities. They are not: they can be negative, they can be huge, and their differences are not odds. The distribution — after softmax — is the only place percentages live.

The score list is long — one entry per candidate, 50,257 for GPT-3 [model-configs] — but every entry is produced by the same arithmetic.

The max subtraction is numerical hygiene: e raised to a large logit overflows, and shifting every logit by the same amount leaves the shares unchanged.

The loop that writes

One forward pass produces a distribution. To write more than one piece, the machinery outside the model does something almost comically simple: it picks a piece from the distribution, appends it to the text, and runs another forward pass on the whole thing. Self-feeding — the model consuming its own output — is what autoregressive means. There is no draft, no plan, no lookahead: piece forty-one is chosen with zero knowledge of what piece forty-two will be.

Running this loop against a finished model is called inference. The word contrasts with training — the process that tuned the weights — and the two regimes could not feel more different: training is months of offline arithmetic; inference is you, waiting. Part Two covers the first; Part Four covers the second at industrial scale.

The loop ends when the pick is a designated stop piece — a reserved entry in the fixed list whose entire job is "done". GPT-2 reserves exactly one such entry, printed like <|endoftext|> [model-configs].

Note

Each cycle recomputes the whole text from scratch — every piece, every floor. That sounds wasteful, and it is; Part Four's caching pages exist because of it.

Why a guessing game looks intelligent

None of this explains why the output is any good. The explanation fits in a paragraph. To predict the next piece well on hard text, you must have absorbed whatever produced that text. After "the capital of France is", honest continuation requires knowing that Paris sits in France — stored not as a sentence but as a tilt in the scores. Multiply that by an internet's worth of evidence, and the pile of numbers ends up encoding grammar, facts in aggregate, styles, even shapes of argument — because every one of those things leaves fingerprints on which piece comes next.

The mystery is not that this works; it is how far it scales. The next seven pages build the machinery that makes a single guess this good: how text is cut (next page), how the pieces become numbers worth multiplying, and how the numbers reach back through the sentence to find each other.

Takeaways

  • The model computes one thing: a score for every possible next piece, every time, with no other goal.
  • Logits are raw ranking strengths; softmax converts them to shares that sum to one — the model's full, hedged opinion.
  • A forward pass is deterministic arithmetic — multiplication and addition, at scale — nothing more exotic.
  • Autoregressive means self-feeding: each pick rejoins the input for the next pass; there is no plan beyond the next piece.
  • Inference (running the loop) and training (tuning the numbers) are the two regimes. Next: the pieces themselves — the real answer is messier than "words".
