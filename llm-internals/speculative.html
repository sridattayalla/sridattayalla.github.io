Part Four — Inference at Scale

Speculative Decoding

The last page made each sweep carry more pieces by shrinking the bytes. This page makes one sweep carry more pieces by making them free to propose: a small model guesses several, the big model checks them all at once — and the check is arranged so the output is provably identical to the big model working alone.

Propose, then verify

The floor has survived every attack so far: one sweep of the big model's weights per piece. Speculative decoding does not touch the sweep — it changes how many pieces one sweep buys [speculative]. Two players: a draft model — small, cheap to run, a hundredth the size in the classic setup — and the target model — the real one, whose weights are the expensive sweep [speculative].

One round runs in two moves. The draft generates a handful of pieces — four to eight, say — the ordinary way, one at a time; each of its steps is small and cheap [speculative]. Then the target runs one forward pass over the prompt plus every drafted piece, and produces its own probability distribution at every position — the same parallel scoring that prefill does over a prompt, turned onto the draft's proposals [speculative].

Read the shape of that twice, because it is the whole trick: the big model can score many positions in one sweep at almost no extra cost, because decode at a lone request is memory-bound — the weights stream past whether the step computes one piece's arithmetic or five. The draft spends cheap serial steps; the target spends one sweep that was going to happen anyway [speculative].

The check that keeps it honest

The target's distributions could be used to simply overrule the draft — take the best piece at every position and keep whatever matches. That would be fast and wrong: the output would no longer be a sample from the target, and the sampling page showed why sampling matters. The real rule is finer [speculative].

Accept pieces left to right. Piece i, which the draft proposed with probability q, is accepted with probability p over q, where p is the target's probability for the same piece. If the target likes the piece at least as much as the draft did, the ratio is at least one and the piece is always kept; if the target likes it less, the piece survives only sometimes — exactly in the ratio [speculative].

Make the rule concrete. Suppose the draft proposed a piece with probability one half, and the target likes the same piece at four fifths: the ratio is well past one, and the piece is always kept. Flip it — the draft was confident at nine tenths, the target lukewarm at one half: the ratio sits below one, and the piece survives five times in nine. Overconfidence is punished in exact proportion; humility is never paid more than certainty.

The first rejection ends the streak, and the repair is precise: resample that position from the normalized positive part of p minus q — the target's preference left over after the draft's claim is subtracted [speculative].

Why this shape? It is rejection sampling, a classical statistics device, dressed for sequences: draw from the cheap proposal, accept by the ratio of true to proposed, repair from the remainder. The composite provably emits exactly the target's distribution — the paper's first theorem — so nothing about the output changes, only its speed [speculative].

The number to watch is the acceptance rate — how often the draft's pieces survive the target's check, call it alpha [speculative].

When it pays

Price a round: a few cheap draft steps plus one target sweep, with an expected yield that grows with the acceptance rate [speculative]:

Worked example — one round, priced

one round, priced
(gamma = 4 drafted pieces, alpha = 0.75):

  draft:    4 x T_draft     (serial, small model)
  verify:   1 x T_target    (one pass, all pieces)

  expected pieces out per round:
    (1 - 0.75^5) / (1 - 0.75) = 3.05

  win condition: four cheap steps plus one sweep
  must beat 3.05 full sweeps

At an acceptance rate near three quarters — what the original paper measured with a small draft on an eleven-billion-parameter model — four drafted pieces yield about three pieces out per target sweep. End to end: up to 3.4x faster at temperature zero, where the draft's certainty is highest, and 2.6x at temperature one [speculative].

See where the formula comes from. The round always nets at least one piece — the resampled repair after a rejection, or a bonus piece drawn from the target itself when every proposal survives — and each accepted proposal adds one more, with the odds of surviving k checks in a row falling as alpha to the k. The yield stacks as one plus alpha plus alpha squared, capped at the drafted count plus one terms, which is exactly the panel's quotient. The cap is also why drafting more pieces stops helping: when alpha is low the stack's tail is nearly empty, and every extra proposal still costs a serial step.

The reason one verification pass is nearly free: arithmetic intensity. At a lone request, decode runs at about one unit of arithmetic per byte read — the starving arithmetic units of the GPU page. Scoring five positions in one pass multiplies the arithmetic by five on the same weight read: intensity five, still far below the machine's balance point. The card had compute going spare; speculation feeds it [speculative].

Where it stops, and what came after

The spare compute is not infinite, and the trick fails where it was already spent. At high request counts the card is compute-bound — the verification arithmetic now competes with real traffic, and speculation costs more than it saves; it is a low-load optimization [speculative]. At high temperature the acceptance rate drops — a diverse target is harder to predict — and every rejection wastes a draft step. And a draft that cannot track the target accepts too little to pay for itself [speculative].

The lineage refined the draft. Self-speculation reuses the target itself — exiting early, or skipping layers — as its own small model. Medusa bolts extra heads onto the model that predict several future pieces in one pass, verified as a tree of candidates. EAGLE drafts at the feature level: a light head predicts the target's hidden states rather than its pieces, and reads pieces off those, reusing the target's own representations; acceptance is high enough for three to six-and-a-half times speedups, and it ships as a config flag in the modern serving engines [speculative].

Part Four, stacked

Stand back and the part is one campaign against a single floor: every piece costs one sweep of the weights. The loop page priced the sweep and split it into phases. The cache page stopped the sweep from redoing attention's work. The serving page amortized the sweep across requests. The quantization page shrank the bytes the sweep must move. This page bundled several pieces into one sweep. Five moves, one division — bytes over bandwidth — made friendly five different ways.

Part Five turns from the cost of a piece to the reach of the machine: what happens when the window must stretch far past what training prepared — and what the machine actually does with a million pieces of room.

Takeaways

  • Speculative decoding splits the work: a small draft model proposes several pieces serially; the target model verifies them all in one parallel pass — the same scoring prefill does.
  • The acceptance rule — accept with probability p/q, resample the first rejection from the leftover p minus q — makes the output provably identical to sampling the target alone. Rejection sampling, dressed for sequences.
  • Expected pieces out per round: (1 - alpha^(gamma+1)) / (1 - alpha) — about three per target sweep at alpha 0.75 with four drafted.
  • It pays because decode starves the arithmetic units: one verification pass over gamma+1 pieces multiplies intensity on the same weight read — compute the card had going spare.
  • It stops paying when the card is already compute-bound, at high temperature, or with a weak draft; EAGLE-style feature-level drafts are the shipping refinement (3-6.5x).
