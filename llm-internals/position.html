Position: Order Without Coordinates

After this page you can say exactly why shuffling a sentence changes nothing for the machine built so far — and how modern models stamp each piece with its slot, as angles inside the vector itself.

The bag problem

The last page ended on a defect, so start by making it precise. Look up the pieces of a sentence — any sentence — and you get one vector per piece. Swap two pieces and look again: the same vectors come back, attached to the same pieces, and nothing anywhere in the machinery — not the lookup, not a dot product, not a table multiply — can tell the two inputs apart. A function with that property has permutation invariance: permute the inputs and the output does not move.

For language that is fatal, because so much of meaning lives in arrangement. "The dog bit the man" and "the man bit the dog" are the same bag of pieces with opposite meanings. A bag-processor asked what comes next after "the man bit the dog" has no way to feel that this time the dog is the victim — victim and aggressor are told apart by order, and order is what the bag throws away.

The fix cannot be a later module that reads slot numbers off to the side, because of how the next mechanism works. Attention — the gathering machinery of the next page, and the reason the architecture carries its name — lets pieces exchange information by taking dot products between their vectors. A dot product sees only the two vectors it is handed. If order is to survive to that point, it must already be inside those vectors: the slot has to ride on the piece. That is this page's whole job.

What a good slot-mark looks like

Suppose the mark had to be designed from scratch. Four requirements would shape it.

First, distinct: pieces at different slots must carry different marks, or nothing downstream can tell slot three from slot seven.

Second, made of vector stuff: the mark lives inside entries that get added, multiplied, and compared, so it must be numbers in those entries — not a side-channel the rest of the machinery cannot see.

Third, and subtlest: comparisons should feel gaps, not addresses. The piece three slots back is a meaningful relation; the piece at slot four thousand ninety-six is not. A phrase contributes the same meaning at the top of a page and at the bottom, so the agreement between two pieces should ideally depend on how far apart they sit — not on where.

Fourth, stretchable: text keeps arriving, and the scheme should keep distinguishing slots well past any length it was tuned around.

Practice settled on two answers. The original transformer added a fixed pattern to every vector. Modern models rotate the vectors instead. Both turn slot into geometry, and both lean on the same trick — many rhythms at different speeds — which is worth meeting slowly, once.

The 2017 answer: add a clock

A positional encoding is any scheme that bakes the slot number into each piece's vector. The first one, from the 2017 paper that introduced the transformer [components], is a sinusoidal encoding: a fixed, unlearned pattern of sines and cosines — one pair of entries per rhythm — added on top of the embedding row.

The pattern for width d is built pair by pair: pair i oscillates as the sine and cosine of the slot divided by 10000^(2i/d) — formally, PE(pos, 2i) = sin(pos / 10000^(2i/d)) for an even entry and the cosine of the same angle for the odd entry after it [components]. Nothing in the pattern is learned; it is the same fixed table for every model that uses it, and the paper reports that letting a model learn its own pattern worked no better [components].

Read each pair as a clock hand. As the slot advances by one, the hand turns by a fixed small angle; each pair turns at its own constant speed. The fast pairs whirl — they pin down neighboring slots precisely. The slow pairs creep — like hour hands, they mark coarse position without wrapping ambiguously over any realistic length. Read together, a modest number of pairs gives every slot a different combined reading: a fingerprint of where.

Worked example — the clocks at four slots

width 4 = two pairs: pair 0 runs at speed 1, pair 1 at speed 0.01
PE(pos) = [ sin(pos)  cos(pos)  sin(0.01*pos)  cos(0.01*pos) ]

  pos  0    0.000   1.000    0.000   1.000
  pos  1    0.841   0.540    0.010   1.000
  pos  7    0.657   0.754    0.070   0.998
  pos 50   -0.262   0.965    0.479   0.878
            -- fast pair --    -- slow pair --

fast pair: full turn every ~6.28 slots (about 8 turns by slot 50)
slow pair: full turn every ~628 slots (under a tenth of one turn by then)

Every entry above is one sine or cosine call away — the panel is checkable with a calculator [components]. Two readings worth pausing on. The fast pair has wrapped around about eight times by slot fifty — nearly useless for where-in-the-document, excellent for how-far-from-the-previous-piece. The slow pair has completed less than a tenth of one turn in the same span — it could keep distinguishing slots for hundreds of positions before finishing its first cycle. Fast for resolution, slow for range, with the speeds between them spread wide.

Because the pattern is added to the embedding row, every hidden state from the ground floor up carries two things at once: what the piece is, from the row, and where it sits, from the pattern. The scheme works. But notice which requirement it misses: the marks are absolute. How two pieces' patterns relate depends on both slots, not just on the gap between them. The paper points out a regularity that softens this — the pattern at slot m plus a fixed gap is a fixed linear transformation of the pattern at slot m — but comparisons that feel only the gap are not built in [components]. The modern scheme builds them in.

The modern answer: rotate by slot

Modern models do not add a pattern to the vector; they turn the vector itself. RoPE — rotary position embedding — treats the vector's entries as pairs and rotates pair i of the piece at slot m by the angle m * theta_i, where theta_i = 10000^(-2i/d) [components]. The speeds come from the same ten-thousand spread as the clocks above. The difference is what gets marked: instead of adding a where-signal on top of the meaning, the scheme rotates coordinates so that where is baked into the angles themselves.

Why rotation, of all things? Because of how dot products behave under it. Turn two vectors by the same angle and their dot product is unchanged. Turn them by different angles and the dot product shifts — but only by the difference of the two angles. So if every piece's coordinates are rotated by its own slot, then any dot product between two pieces depends on the gap between their slots and on nothing else. No addresses anywhere. The third requirement, solved by geometry.

Watch it happen with one pair turning one radian per slot. Every number below is printed by the companion script and recorded in the research sheet [components]:

Worked example — equal gaps, equal dot products

toy setup: width 2 = one pair, theta = 1 radian per slot
u = [ 1  0 ]      v = [ 0.5  1 ]      u . v = 0.5

rotate u to slot m, v to slot n, then take the dot product:

  (m, n) = ( 0,  0)    dot = 0.500  \
  (m, n) = ( 3,  3)    dot = 0.500   }  gap 0 -> always 0.500
  (m, n) = (17, 17)    dot = 0.500  /

  (m, n) = ( 1,  0)    dot = 1.112  \
  (m, n) = ( 5,  4)    dot = 1.112   }  gap 1 -> always 1.112
  (m, n) = (40, 39)    dot = 1.112  /

  (m, n) = ( 2,  0)    dot = 0.701  \
  (m, n) = ( 9,  7)    dot = 0.701   }  gap 2 -> always 0.701

one rotation spelled out: rope([1, 0], slot 3) = [cos 3, sin 3] = [-0.990, 0.141]

Same gap, same dot product — whether the two pieces sit at the front of the text or deep inside it. Different gap, different dot product. And one limit the panel cannot show: a single pair at a single speed eventually wraps and repeats, so far-apart gaps can collide. Real vectors carry many pairs whose speeds span a factor of ten thousand, and their joint pattern separates gaps that any single pair would confuse — the same fast-for-resolution, slow-for-range spread as before, doing a new job.

One honest detail about placement: the rotation is not applied to the piece's working vector itself. It is applied to two small derived copies of it — the pair of copies that the next page's machinery compares against each other — right before their dot product. The geometry above is the whole idea; the copies get their names on the next page.

Two details in the code: the pairs are formed by matching the first half of the entries with the second half, and the speeds come from the ten-thousand spread — visible in the theta line.

Two clocks, one idea

Both schemes answer the same question with the same shape of answer: turn slot into geometry inside the vectors, using many rhythms at different speeds. The additive version marks slots absolutely and lets the machinery learn around it. The rotation version makes every comparison feel the gap directly.

And with the marks in place, the order-blindness is gone. Shuffle the input now and the rotations misalign — dot products that came out equal come out different, and the outputs move with them. The dog-bites-man pair finally enters as genuinely different input.

What the marks are for arrives on the next page: the machinery that lets every piece gather information from the pieces before it, whose comparisons these rotations were designed to serve.

Takeaways

  • Everything before this page is permutation-invariant: shuffle the input and the outputs do not move. Word order carries meaning, and a bag throws exactly that away.
  • A positional encoding bakes the slot into the vector itself; the sinusoidal recipe adds a fixed sine-and-cosine pattern — fast pairs resolve neighbors, slow pairs cover range.
  • RoPE rotates coordinate pairs by an angle that grows with the slot, so rotated dot products depend only on the gap between slots, never on the addresses.
  • One pair at one speed repeats; many pairs spanning a factor of ten thousand in speed give each gap a distinct joint fingerprint.
  • Next: the gathering machinery that turns these marked vectors into information flow between pieces — the mechanism that gives the architecture its name.
