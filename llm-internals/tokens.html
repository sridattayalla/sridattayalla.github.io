Tokens: The Model's Alphabet

After this page you can say exactly what a language model sees when you send it text — and why that explains its most famous blunders.

Why not letters? Why not words?

The previous page left a debt: "pieces of text" is doing a lot of work in that sentence. Time to pay it. The model's reach is finite — it can only process so many pieces at once, and everything downstream scales with the piece count. Cut text into letters and a page becomes thousands of pieces of the dullest kind. Cut it into whole words and the list of possible pieces explodes: every name, every typo, every conjugation needs its own entry, and the list still meets words it has never seen, with no way to spell them.

The compromise every modern system uses: cut text into frequent fragments, somewhere between letters and words. Common words become single pieces; rare words shatter into reusable shards. The vocabulary is the fixed list of pieces the model reads and writes — set before training, frozen after. The sizes vary by family: the list GPT-3 reads holds 50,257 entries [model-configs]; Llama 2 used 32,000 and Llama 3 uses 128,000, packing more of each rare word into a single piece [tokenization].

Both budgets explain the strange middle size of the pieces. Whole words would waste the menu on the rare — every surname, typo, and conjugation demanding its own entry. Single characters would waste the reach on the common — every short, frequent word re-spelled from scratch, slot after slot. Fragments split the difference: the frequent words of a language are few, so they fuse into single pieces, while everything rare shatters into shards the menu already owns. The menu stays modest, the reach stays long, and the same fragments serve both jobs.

Building the list: merge what is frequent

Where does the list come from? BPE — byte pair encoding — is the standard recipe, and it is delightfully dumb. Start from single characters. Count every pair of adjacent characters across a large body of training text. Glue the most frequent pair into one new entry — that rule is called a merge — apply it everywhere, recount, and repeat until the list reaches its budget. Frequent patterns fuse early ("th", "the"), rare ones never fuse at all.

The companion script trains one on a toy body of text so you can watch every step [tokenization]:

Worked example — twelve merges, start to finish

training text: "the cat sat on the mat. the cat ate the rat.
                the rat ate the cat."     (16 words, 11 distinct letters)

merge  1: 'a'  + 't'   -> 'at'
merge  2: 'e'  + '</w>' -> 'e</w>'        ('</w>' marks a word end)
merge  3: 't'  + 'h'   -> 'th'
merge  4: 'th' + 'e</w>' -> 'the</w>'
merge  5: 'at' + '</w>' -> 'at</w>'
merge  6: 'at' + '.'   -> 'at.'
merge  7: 'at.'+ '</w>' -> 'at.</w>'
merge  8: 'c'  + 'at</w>' -> 'cat</w>'
merge  9: 'at' + 'e</w>' -> 'ate</w>'
merge 10: 's'  + 'at</w>' -> 'sat</w>'
merge 11: 'o'  + 'n'   -> 'on'
merge 12: 'on' + '</w>' -> 'on</w>'

final vocabulary: 24 entries = 12 starting symbols + 12 merges

Encoding a sentence means replaying the merges in learned order and printing row numbers:

Worked example — encoding

text:    "the cat ate the rat."
pieces   the</w>   cat</w>   ate</w>   the</w>   r   at.</w>
ids      15        19        20        15        9   18

Notice the last word. "the" is one piece, but "rat." shatters into "r" plus "at." — because merge six glued "at" to the period before "rat" ever had a chance to form. The gluing is greedy and local, and its accidents are permanent: this vocabulary can never print "rat" as one piece [tokenization].

Real systems refine the recipe in one big way. Byte-level BPE starts from the 256 raw bytes instead of characters, so any input is representable — any language, any emoji, any mangled typo — and there is no unknown symbol, ever. GPT-2 learned roughly 50,000 merges this way over web text [tokenization].

One consequence deserves emphasis before moving on: the list is part of the model, not an industry convention. Train on different text, or set a different budget of entries, and the merges come out different — the same sentence then shatters at different joints. That is why switching between model families moves the seams, and why a system built around one model cannot hand its piece lists to another: everything must be re-cut from the raw text.

What the model actually sees

After cutting, the input is a list of integers. A token id is simply the piece's row number in the vocabulary — no more, no less. The model never sees letters, words, or punctuation; it sees these numbers, and only these numbers.

That single fact explains the most viral failure in the field. Ask a model how many times the letter r appears in "strawberry" and it will happily get it wrong. Look at what it actually received [tokenization]:

Worked example — the strawberry input

text:      "strawberry"
pieces     st        raw       berry
ids        301       1831      8396
r count    0         1         2      (total 3 — but no letter is in the input)

The question "how many r's" is a question about letters. The letters were destroyed at the door. What the model holds are three row numbers whose pieces happen to contain zero, one, and two r's — and to answer correctly it would have to have memorized, during training, the letter count of every vocabulary entry. Sometimes it has; often it has not. This is a boundary problem, not a reasoning problem — like asking someone to count a building's windows from a photograph of its door.

The same boundary cuts in surprising places. A leading space typically travels with the word that follows it, so " cat" and "cat" are different pieces — and "The cat sat on the" is five pieces, four of which start with a space [tokenization]. Numbers split at unpredictable joints: "15,000" arrives as three pieces — "15", ",", "000" — which is why arithmetic on large numbers goes wrong [tokenization]. English averages about four characters per piece on modern lists [tokenization], so a thousand-word essay is roughly thirteen hundred pieces of input.

The reserved entries

Some entries in the list are not text at all. A special token is a reserved piece that acts as a control signal. You have already met one: the stop piece from two pages back, the one that ends the writing loop. GPT-2's vocabulary is 256 byte entries, plus 50,000 merged entries, plus exactly one reserved stop piece — 50,257 in all [model-configs]. Later systems reserve more: markers that separate the user's text from the system's instructions, markers for tools and documents. Part Three shows what those buy.

Watch out

Because reserved pieces look like ordinary text positions, a malicious input containing something that resembles one can shift what the model believes about the conversation. Production systems filter for this at the boundary; the vulnerability exists precisely because text and control share one list.

Turning pieces back into text

Output runs the mirror operation. Detokenization is concatenating the pieces' text together — the model emits a row number, the system prints its piece, done. There is one wrinkle worth knowing: pieces are byte strings, and a single character can straddle two pieces, so a system that streams text as pieces arrive must hold back a partial character until its tail shows up [inference]. That is why streamed replies occasionally pause for a beat mid-word: the machinery is waiting for the rest of a character, not thinking harder.

So the full picture of the boundary: text in, integers out; integers in, text out. Everything between is the model. But an integer is a label, not a meaning — row 15 and row 16 are as unrelated as jersey numbers. The next page gives every row its meaning, as numbers the model can actually multiply.

Takeaways

  • The vocabulary is a fixed menu of fragments; the model reads and writes from the same list, and can never print what the list cannot spell.
  • BPE builds the menu by gluing the most frequent adjacent pairs — greedy, local, and permanent in its accidents.
  • A token id is a row number; letters are destroyed at the door and never reach the model.
  • Strawberry-style blunders are boundary artifacts — the model was never shown the letters it is asked to count.
