A field manual

How LLMs Actually Work

A trained language model does exactly one thing — predicts the next chunk of text — and this manual takes you through every gear inside that one thing, down to numbers you can check by hand.

The whole trick in one sentence

You have watched a language model finish a sentence. Here is everything that happened, minus the branding. The text you wrote was cut into numbered chunks. The chunks went into the model, and out came a score for every possible next chunk. The model picked one, attached it, and ran the whole procedure again on the slightly longer text. Repeat until it emits a designated stop piece. That loop is the entire product — the chat, the essay, the code — and this manual walks through it gear by gear.

Those chunks are the atoms of everything that follows. A token is one entry from a fixed list of text fragments: often a word, a piece of a word, or a punctuation mark. Common words are usually single pieces; rare ones shatter into fragments. A model's list is fixed before training begins and never grows — bigger lists simply pack more of a rare word into each piece, and the guessing game itself is unchanged.

And what is doing the scoring? A model is the finished artifact: an enormous collection of numbers arranged in shapes, plus the recipe for turning chunks into scores. Nothing is looked up at run time — no database, no index — every fact and habit the model has lives inside those numbers. GPT-3, the 2020 system that made this design famous, contains 175 billion of them [model-configs].

A language model is a model trained on human-written text to play this one game well: guess the next piece. That is the entire definition. The rest of this manual is about what it takes to make the guess good — and what falls out of trying.

What you will be able to do

This manual is written so that, by the end, you can do the following without hand-waving:

  • Point at any part of the stack and say what it computes, and what would break without it.
  • Explain why a model that aces professional exams still miscounts the letters in strawberry — a boundary problem, not a reasoning problem.
  • Check any number the text asserts: each one is either computed in front of you or carries a bracketed citation.
  • Read deployment decisions — machine size, users per chip, caching — as engineering rather than folklore.

The route

Five parts, in dependency order — each page assumes only what came before.

  • Part One — The Machine. How text becomes numbers a chip can multiply, how order is marked, and how earlier pieces get mixed into later ones — ending with the full assembly, floor by floor.
  • Part Two — Training. What "guessing well" means as arithmetic, how billions of numbers get tuned by their own mistakes, and the hardware that pays for it.
  • Part Three — The Assistant. The extra training pass that turns a text-continuer into something that takes instructions.
  • Part Four — Running at Scale. Why the first piece of a reply is slow, why the rest stream, and why moving numbers — not arithmetic — sets the price.
  • Part Five — The Frontier. How the model's reach gets stretched from thousands to millions of pieces, and the newest trade the field is making.

A companion program, code/tiny_llm.py, implements the whole machine in miniature — cutting text into pieces, mixing, one training step, and generation with and without the trick that makes it fast. Every mechanism in these pages has a line number there.

How to read this manual

Three conventions hold throughout. Every number is either worked in front of you or carries a citation mark like the bracketed one earlier on this page; the marks resolve on the Sources page. Every term is defined at first use — the dotted underline on token, model, and language model marks a definition, and the Glossary collects them all. And the worked examples are genuinely hand-checkable: if a table says a row adds to one, you are invited to add it.

That is the contract. Turn the page.

Takeaways

  • A language model cuts text into fixed pieces, scores every possible next piece, picks one, and repeats — that loop is the whole product.
  • Everything the model "knows" is baked into its numbers at training time; nothing is looked up when you chat.
  • The rest of the field — assistant behavior, speed, price — is engineering wrapped around that loop.
