Part Three — The Assistant

Post-Training: From Autocompleter to Assistant

After this page you can say what a base model is and why it is not an assistant, what the stages of post-training do to it, why hallucination is structural rather than a bug, and what the wrapper around the model looks like at the piece level.

On this page

  1. The raw predictor's problem
  2. Show it what you want
  3. Judge it, then optimize the judgment
  4. What tuning cannot do
  5. The wrapper you never see

The raw predictor's problem

Part Two ended honestly: what pretraining produces is a brilliant text-predictor and nothing more. Give that thing its name before fixing it. The base model is the raw output of pretraining — the text-predictor as trained, before any stage that shapes it toward being useful [post-training].

Its problem is not stupidity; it is job description. Ask "What is the capital of France?" and the base model continues in the voice of the corpus: on the web, a question is often followed by another question, or a list of questions, or a page header — and so it continues, plausibly and uselessly [post-training]. It completes documents; it does not hold conversations, because nothing in the objective ever asked it to.

Show it what you want

The first fix is the oldest one in learning: examples. Instruction tuning — usually called SFT, supervised fine-tuning — continues training the base model on a small set of written demonstrations: a prompt (the text on the user's side, given to the model) followed by the reply that should have come [post-training]. The recipe that named the genre used about thirteen thousand such pairs, written by hired humans, to turn GPT-3 the predictor into something assistant-shaped [post-training].

Notice what this stage can teach: shape, not knowledge. The demonstrations number in the thousands; the corpus numbered in the trillions. Tuning at this scale moves behavior — format, tone, refusal habits, question-meets-answer — because behavior is shallow and learnable from few examples. Knowledge is not: a 2024 study found that fine-tuning on facts the model does not already hold mostly teaches it to produce those facts fluently whether or not they are true — known facts get absorbed in about fifty steps, while unknown ones keep their charge high and push the model toward fabrication [post-training]. The edge of that lesson: post-training steers what the model already knows; it is close to useless for adding to it [post-training].

Where new knowledge must come from: retrieval — put the needed text into the model's input — or continued pretraining at corpus scale. The context window is the only memory the model actually reads [post-training].

Judge it, then optimize the judgment

The second fix goes past examples, because examples of what is good are thin evidence of what is better. The recipe: build a judge, then optimize against it. A reward model is a learned scoring function — a copy of the model, tuned on human preference comparisons, that outputs one number: how much a human would like a given reply [post-training]. Then RLHF — reinforcement learning from human feedback — runs a loop: the model answers, the judge scores the answer, and the model's entries move to raise the expected score [post-training].

One detail keeps the loop safe: the score is not the only pressure. Each update is leashed — penalized for drifting far from the stage-one version of the model — because a learned judge is itself a guess at taste, and an unconstrained optimizer will find its blind spots: replies engineered to score high while saying nothing, the exploits of any judge. The leash keeps the pursuit of the score from wrecking the machine being tuned [post-training].

Worked example — the three-stage recipe

the recipe that named the genre
(InstructGPT, on GPT-3):

  stage 1 — supervised tuning:
    ~13,000 written question-and-answer
    pairs teach the shape of a reply

  stage 2 — build the judge:
    ~33,000 questions; humans compare
    pairs of replies; train a scalar
    "how much would a human like this"

  stage 3 — optimize against the judge:
    ~31,000 questions; reinforcement
    learning toward the judge's score,
    leashed to stay near stage 1's model

  headline: the 1.3B tuned assistant was
  preferred by humans over the 175B raw
  predictor — a hundred times smaller

The result that pivoted the field: the small assistant tuned this way was preferred by human raters over the raw predictor a hundred times its size, and the full-size tuned model's replies were preferred about eighty-five percent of the time against its own base [post-training]. Behavior turned out to be cheap; scale was not the only lever.

The modern simplification: DPO — direct preference optimization — skips the judge and the loop. It derives, from the same preference pairs, a direct update rule with the same mathematical target as the leashed loop: no separate scoring model, no rollouts, simpler and more stable. The 8B model's published recipe is supervised tuning plus DPO, with variants [post-training]. A newer branch of the same idea swaps the learned judge for checkable rewards — tests that pass, answers that match — and powers the reasoning models; that story belongs to Part Five.

What tuning cannot do

Set the limits plainly, because the product experience depends on them. Hallucination is the model producing fluent, confident statements that are not true [post-training]. It is structural, not a bug in the tuning: every objective so far rewards plausible continuations, and the machine has no mechanism for telling a pattern remembered from the corpus apart from a pattern merely likely [post-training]. Tuning for helpfulness adds pressure of its own — human raters reward confident answers, which can raise the rate on questions the model cannot answer [post-training].

Worked example — the rates, measured

hallucination rates on questions
(tuned assistant vs raw GPT-3):

  closed-domain (answer documented):
    raw GPT-3:     41%
    tuned:         21%   (roughly halved)

  open-domain (no fixed answer):
    raw GPT-3:     17%
    tuned:         22%   (slightly worse)

The numbers bear the structure out. On questions about a fixed, documented domain, tuning cut the raw predictor's rate roughly in half. On open-ended questions the tuned model was slightly worse — and no amount of tuning removed the class of failure [post-training]. What works is grounding: retrieval that puts true text into the input. Training the model to say "I don't know" helps where the domain is closed, and is the honest habit anyway, but it does not fix the open domain [post-training].

The other limit is loss, not gain. Catastrophic forgetting: tune the model hard on a narrow domain and its general behavior degrades — the new material displacing the old. The standard mitigation is mixing general data back into the tuning set, which the 8B model's recipe does [post-training].

The wrapper you never see

One mechanical fact remains, and it explains half of what an assistant product is. At run time, the assistant is the tuned model wrapped in a fixed format string: the chat template, a piece-level protocol — invisible in the product's interface — that marks where each turn begins, who spoke, and where each reply ends [post-training]. The model was tuned on this exact format; speaking to it outside the format is addressing it in a language it was never taught [post-training].

Worked example — one conversation, serialized

what the model actually reads
(Llama 3 style markers):

  <|start_header_id|>user<|end_header_id|>
  What is the capital of France?<|eot_id|>
  <|start_header_id|>assistant<|end_header_id|>
  The capital of France is Paris.<|eot_id|>
  <|start_header_id|>user<|end_header_id|>
  And its oldest bridge?<|eot_id|>
  <|start_header_id|>assistant<|end_header_id|>
  ...

  one flat sequence of pieces — every turn,
  re-read from the first piece, every time

A system prompt is the same mechanism used for standing instructions: a fixed first turn, present before any user speaks, that sets persona, tools, and constraints [post-training]. And the serialization implies something easy to miss: the model is stateless between requests. At every turn it reads the whole conversation — every previous turn plus the markers — as one flat sequence of pieces, from the first piece onward [post-training]. Whatever memory an assistant product appears to have is either text re-sent with the request, or retrieval injecting text into it; nothing else survives between turns.

With the assistant shaped, one question remains before it can speak at all. At every position of every reply, the machine still ends in a probability distribution over the vocabulary — and tuning has shaped the distribution, not chosen from it. Which piece actually comes out — the certain choice, the weighted gamble, or something between — is a decision with its own engineering, and it is the next page.

Takeaways

  • The base model is pretraining's raw output: a document-completer, not a converser — a question meets a plausible continuation of a question-shaped page.
  • Instruction tuning (SFT) teaches shape from thousands of examples, not knowledge from trillions; facts the model lacks cannot be tutored in — retrieval is the memory that works.
  • RLHF builds a learned judge and optimizes against it on a leash; the tuned 1.3B model beat the raw 175B model with human raters — behavior is cheap, and scale is not the only lever.
  • DPO collapses judge and loop into one direct rule with the same target — the standard modern recipe (the 8B model: SFT plus DPO).
  • Hallucination is structural — the objective rewards plausibility, not truth — and the chat template is the piece-level protocol the product speaks; the model is stateless between turns.
