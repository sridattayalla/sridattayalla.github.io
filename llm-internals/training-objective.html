The machine runs — is it right?

Part One ended with a tower and a count: eight billion entries of multiply-and-add, thirty-two floors tall, and nothing anywhere in it that knows right from wrong. Run the tower on any text and it produces, at every position, a score for every piece of the vocabulary. The machine is equally content whether those scores point at what actually came next or at rubbish.

Training is the outside process that fixes this, and it needs a verdict first: one number, computed after a pass, that says how wrong the machine was. This page pins that number down exactly — what it charges, what it averages over, and how to read its value. The rule that pushes it down is the next page's subject; the number comes first, because without a definition of wrong, no rule for reducing it can be derived.

One number per guess

Every position of every pass ends the same way: a score for each piece of the vocabulary, squeezed through a softmax into a probability distribution — the machine's guess about what comes next. The training text already knows the answer: the piece that actually followed. The guess is graded on a single fact, the probability it put on that piece.

The grading rule is cross-entropy: the charge is minus the natural logarithm of the probability the machine placed on the piece that actually came next. Put probability one on the right answer and the charge is zero — certain and right costs nothing. A coin flip on the right answer costs about 0.69. A one-in-twenty guess costs about 3.0. And an even shrug over every piece of a fifty-thousand-piece vocabulary costs about 10.8 — the price of knowing nothing [training].

The same quantity with its sign flipped has a name of its own: the log probability of the right piece, written ln p. Pushing the charge down and pushing the log probability up are the same act — one number, read in opposite directions.

Why a logarithm and not the raw probability? Two mechanical reasons. Being right across many positions is a product of probabilities — the chance of the whole sequence multiplies — and logarithms turn products into sums, which is what makes an average possible. And the shape of the charge punishes confident wrongness: every halving of the probability adds the same constant, about 0.69, to the charge, and as the probability falls toward zero the charge climbs without bound. A machine that rules out the right answer outright pays without limit [training].

Every position, averaged

One guess per position, one charge per guess — now aggregate. The loss is the average charge over every position of the text the machine was run on: one number per pass that says, on average, how wrong the machine was. Recall the causal mask from Part One — each position only ever sees the pieces before it, so every position makes a legitimate guess at the same time, and every position's charge lands in the same average. One forward pass, one loss, every position graded.

Averaged over what text? The corpus: the training set, assembled before training starts — web crawl, code, books — then filtered for quality. Llama 3 read roughly fifteen trillion pieces; GPT-3 read roughly three hundred billion after filtering [training].

The full run over this text is pretraining: the first and by far the largest stage of training, the one that consumes the corpus and produces the raw text-predicting machine that every later stage refines [training].

Training does not read the corpus one sequence at a time. One update works on a batch: a slab of sequences pulled from the corpus, run together, their charges averaged into one loss. The largest runs of GPT-3 moved 3.2 million pieces per step; Llama 3 in its later phases moved 16 million [training].

One consequence to carry forward, because it explains much of what these machines later do. The corpus is the model's only contact with the world: whatever patterns the text contained, statistically, are what the entries encode. Nothing is retrieved at run time — the machine is a compressed statistical memory of its training text, not a database it consults. That is why a model has a knowledge cutoff, and why it can produce fluent, confident statements about things the corpus never covered. What to do about that is Part Three's problem [training].

Reading the number

A charge of 2.0 is hard to feel. The standard translation is perplexity: raise e to the loss. The result reads as the effective number of choices the machine was deciding among — a machine at loss 3.0 has a perplexity of about twenty, meaning it is on average as unsure as someone choosing among twenty options. A certain prediction is a perplexity of one, a coin flip two, and the even shrug over GPT-3's vocabulary fifty thousand [training].

Worked example — reading a loss in both directions

reading the loss in both directions
(GPT-3's vocabulary: 50,257 pieces)

  p on right piece   charge   perplexity   reads as
  1.000              0.000    1.0          certain
  0.500              0.693    2.0          a coin flip
  0.050              2.996    20.0         a twenty-way guess
  1 / 50,257         10.824   50,257       the even shrug

  reverse: a loss of 2.0 means e^-2 = 13.5%
           on the right piece, on average

Both directions are worth holding. Reading down the table: each row is a tenfold worse guess, and the charge climbs by a constant step each time while the perplexity multiplies by ten — the charge is additive, the perplexity multiplicative. Reading the last line the other way: a machine reporting a loss of 2.0 is putting about thirteen and a half percent on the right piece at every position, on average [training].

Read it top to bottom. The softmax turns the final scores into a probability distribution at every position. The next line picks out, for each position, the probability the machine assigned to the piece that actually followed. Then the logarithm, the sign flip, and the mean: the average charge, the loss. The tiny constant added inside the log is a guard — a machine that put exactly zero on the right answer would make the raw logarithm minus infinity; the guard turns certain wrongness into a very large but finite charge.

From roughly half a percent to roughly twenty percent on the right piece, after one move. Training works, and this is the entire sense in which it works: the same tower, the same arithmetic, entries nudged until the charge falls. What remains is the rule. One number comes out of the top of the machine; eight billion entries sit inside it. How a single charge at the top reaches every entry below — which entry to move, which way, and how far — is a solved problem with a name, and it is the next page.

Takeaways

  • The unit of wrong is cross-entropy: minus the logarithm of the probability placed on the piece that actually came next. Certain right costs zero, a coin flip about 0.69, knowing nothing about 10.8 on a fifty-thousand-piece vocabulary.
  • The loss is the average of that charge over every position — and the causal mask means every position makes a legitimate guess at once, so one pass grades them all.
  • Pretraining reads a filtered corpus — roughly fifteen trillion pieces for Llama 3 — in batches of millions of pieces per step.
  • Perplexity is e to the loss: the effective number of choices. Loss 3.0 is a twenty-way uncertainty; the even shrug over GPT-3's vocabulary is 50,257.
  • One verified step took the toy's loss from 5.198 to 1.629 — half a percent to twenty percent on the right piece. The rule that chose the move is next.
