Part Five — The Frontier

Long Context: Extending the Window

Part Four priced each piece of a reply. Part Five widens the machine's reach: the window — every position from the first piece of the prompt to the last piece of the answer — is a training-time promise, and breaking it has a cost. Here is what actually happens at the edge of the window, and the tricks that move it.

On this page

  2. Zoom out: position interpolation
  3. Stretch the low notes: NTK-aware scaling

The wall at the edge of training

Feed a model trained with an eight-thousand-position window a prompt four times longer and it does not politely refuse — it falls apart: perplexity explodes, because the position frequencies in that far range were never seen in training [context].

Recall what a position is to this machine. The position page showed each one applied as a rotation — a different angle per position per frequency pair, calibrated by nothing but exposure. Training tunes every weight around rotations it has seen; past the edge, the angles are strangers.

The second wall is cost. Attention scores every position against every other, so the score matrix is n by n: doubling the context quadruples the attention arithmetic — the reason the Llama three report cites quadratic growth as the cost of long context [context].

Zoom out: position interpolation

Position interpolation shrinks the addresses instead of growing the machine: rescale every position p to p over s before applying the rotations, where s is the stretch — target window over trained window [context].

The effect is a zoom-out on the position axis. Every position in the longer prompt lands somewhere inside the range training calibrated, at a price: adjacent pieces now sit s times closer together, and the model must relearn to read at the new density [context].

Worked example — one stretch, priced

one stretch, priced
(trained window 8K, target window 32K):

  s = 32768 / 8192 = 4

  position mapping, before and after:
    p = 100    ->   25
    p = 8000   -> 2000
    p = 32000  -> 8000

  fine-tune bill (position interpolation):
    about 1000 steps on long documents,
    a fraction of a percent of pretraining

That relearning is cheap. The original demonstration took a model trained at four thousand positions to thirty-two thousand with about a thousand fine-tune steps on long documents — a fraction of a percent of pretraining compute [context].

Why so cheap? The weights already know how to read positions — every projection and feed-forward was trained on rotations of exactly this kind; only the spacing changed. Fine-tuning adjusts how the model interprets differences between neighbors, not the machinery that produces them. The price side is just as real: with the stretch at four, two pieces that training could tell apart by their angles now arrive four times closer, and pairs that used to be distinct can collide. Interpolation trades resolution for reach, and the fine-tune is the model relearning to squint.

Stretch the low notes: NTK-aware scaling

A uniform squeeze is crude. NTK-aware scaling refuses to move the positions at all — it stretches the schedule instead: the rotation frequency base is raised to the stretch factor to the power of head dimension over head dimension minus two [context].

Worked example — the two edits, side by side

the two edits, side by side
(stretch s = 4, head dimension d = 128):

  position interpolation:
    every position divided by 4; base untouched

  ntk-aware scaling:
    positions untouched; base stretched:
    b' = 10000 * 4^(128/126) = 40889

Why do frequencies split the work this way? A high-frequency pair rotates fast: adjacent positions differ by a large angle, so nearby pieces are sharply separated — but the pattern repeats every few positions and carries nothing about long range. A low-frequency pair rotates slowly: neighbors look nearly identical, yet the small drift accumulates, and after thousands of positions the angle is still turning — the slow hand of the clock, the one that encodes distance. Squeezing all positions uniformly damages both hands at once; stretching the base stretches only the slow one.

The asymmetry is the point. The fast-rotating, high-frequency pairings that separate nearby positions stay nearly intact — local resolution survives — while the slow, low-frequency rotations stretch to cover the new distance. The model keeps its close reading and gains reach with zero fine-tuning: two to four times the window, no additional training at all [context].

YaRN refines both edits at once. The stretch is applied per frequency — extrapolate the highest, interpolate the lowest, smooth the middle — and the attention logits get a temperature factor, because each query now softmaxes over more terms and the distribution flattens; sharpen to compensate [context]. The bill: ten times fewer tokens than interpolation needed, two and a half times fewer steps, and the seven- and thirteen-billion-parameter Llama pair reading to one hundred twenty-eight thousand positions in about four hundred steps [context].

Bound the cache: sliding-window attention

Sliding-window attention attacks the cost wall from the other side: each piece attends only to the W pieces behind it, for a fixed window W — so each layer's attention arithmetic and KV cache stay bounded by W no matter how long the conversation grows [context].

Reach returns with depth. A layer reads W pieces back; the layer below reads W pieces back from each of those; the information hops down the stack, so the top of a deep model draws transitively on roughly layers times W positions [context].

The accounting: a layer's score matrix shrinks from n by n to n by W, and its slice of the KV cache stops growing once the conversation passes W pieces. For a very long prompt the arithmetic per layer stops rising entirely — the layer pays the same whether the prompt is twice the window or a hundred times it.

Mistral's seven-billion model ran a four-thousand-position window inside a thirty-two-thousand-position context this way, and later models interleave windowed layers with full-attention layers — near history cheap, far history occasional [context].

What ships, and what actually works

Modern frontier models are trained long rather than stretched after the fact: the Llama three line climbed to one hundred twenty-eight thousand positions in six stages of continued pretraining on long documents, adjusting the rotation base along the way. The bases crept across the configs as the windows grew: ten thousand originally, five hundred thousand for Llama three, a million for its code variant [context].

The newest edge removes position from some layers entirely: Llama four interleaves layers with no positional information at all among rotated layers — order comes from the causal mask alone — plus the attention temperature at inference, and claims ten million positions for its smallest model; the largest Gemini reads more than a million [context].

Length is not comprehension. Retrieval from the middle of a long prompt is measurably worse than from either end — the lost in the middle curve, a U shape in accuracy by position [context]. A giant window is a promise about capacity, not about attention.

Takeaways

  • A model fed past its trained window does not fail politely — perplexity explodes on unseen position frequencies, and attention cost is quadratic in length.
  • Position interpolation divides every position by the stretch before rotation: the whole target range folds into the trained range, s-times denser, relearned in ~1000 fine-tune steps.
  • NTK-aware scaling stretches the frequency base instead — high frequencies intact, low frequencies extended — buying 2-4x with zero fine-tuning; YaRN adds a per-frequency ramp plus attention temperature, reaching 128K in ~400 steps.
  • Sliding-window attention bounds each layer's cost and cache at W (Mistral: 4096 window in a 32K context), with reach restored by depth (~layers × W).
  • What ships: staged long-context pretraining (Llama 3: 8K→128K, bases crept 10,000→500,000→1,000,000), layers with no positional information interleaved (Llama 4), 1M+ inputs (Gemini) — yet retrieval still sags in the middle of long prompts.
