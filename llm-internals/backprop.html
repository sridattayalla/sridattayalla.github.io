Backpropagation and the Training Step

One number, eight billion knobs

The previous page ended with a question: one charge comes out of the top of the machine, and eight billion entries sit below it. Make the question precise. The charge is a function of every entry — nudge any entry and the charge shifts, a little or a lot, up or down. Training wants, for every single entry, the answer to one local question: if this entry rises by a hair, which way does the charge move, and how steeply?

That per-entry slope is this page's whole target, and no calculus ceremony is needed to hold it — the nursery definition is the working one: nudge the entry up by a hair, measure the charge's response, divide response by hair. The gradient is that slope computed for every entry at once: for each learned table, a table of the same shape whose every cell holds that cell's slope. A positive cell — raising that entry raises the charge. A negative cell — raising it lowers the charge. And the cell's size says how strongly.

Why slopes, and not something cleverer? Because a slope is a direction and a step size in one number per knob, and the cheapest improvement rule imaginable is then available: move every knob a small step against its slope. Everything on this page exists to make that one rule computable.

The chain rule, run backward

The obstacle: the charge is not written in terms of the entries. It is written as a long chain — entries feed tables, tables feed the stream, the stream feeds scores, scores pass through a softmax into probabilities, the probability on the right piece sets the charge, and positions average. To price an entry three floors down, a slope must pass through every link between it and the charge. So name the chain: the computational graph is the forward pass written as elementary steps — lookups, multiplies, adds, the softmax — each step consuming the previous steps' outputs. Part One built this graph floor by floor; it was always a graph, and from here on that is how it must be seen.

Slopes travel through chains by an old rule. The chain rule: if a depends on b, and the charge depends on a, then the charge's slope with respect to b is the charge's slope with respect to a, times a's slope with respect to b — dL/db = dL/da * da/db. One multiplication per link. A slope that enters a link from above leaves below, multiplied by that link's own local slope.

Start where the charge is. The topmost links — the softmax and the charge together — have a combined local slope with a famous name: probability minus truth. Take the machine's probability for each piece of the vocabulary and subtract one at the piece that actually came next. If the machine put forty percent on the right piece, its top slope there is minus sixty percent — too low, push it up — while small positive leftovers sit on the wrong pieces and push them down. A right answer the machine was already certain of gets a slope of nearly zero: nothing left to say. The toy's backward walk begins with exactly this subtraction:

Below the top, two local shapes repeat all the way down the tower. A learned table computes y = xW. Its slope with respect to the table itself is the stored input, transposed, times the incoming slope — a gradient the same shape as the table, ready to move that table. Why one walk suffices

Assemble the procedure and give it its name. Backpropagation: run the forward pass and keep every intermediate value; then, starting from the charge, walk the computational graph in reverse — apply the chain rule at each link, pass the result down, and whenever a learned table is passed, record that table's gradient. One walk, and every entry in the machine has its slope — at a cost of roughly two to three times a single forward pass [training].

Why walk backward? A counting argument. Walking from the charge downward, each intermediate slope is computed once and then reused by every link that consumes it — the work of pricing one intermediate is shared by all the entries below. Walking the other way, from entries upward, prices one entry per walk: eight billion walks. Backward is one walk. The machinery that does this automatically has a name: autograd — the system every modern framework ships, which records the forward pass's steps as they run and generates the backward walk from the recording. The toy hand-writes what autograd would generate, which is the point of writing it by hand: the magic turns out to be two lines per link, repeated.

The walk's price is memory. Every multiplier in the chain rule is a value from the forward pass — the stored input in the table rule, the probabilities in the top rule — so every intermediate value must be kept until its link is passed. The stored intermediates have a name: activations — the values a forward pass produces on its way to the charge. Training must hold them all for the whole backward walk, which is why training a model is memory-hungry in a way that generating text is not: generation needs no backward walk and throws the intermediates away as it goes [training].

The move: slopes to new entries

Slopes in hand, the move itself is tiny. Gradient descent: every entry steps against its slope — new value = old value minus step size times slope — because the slope points uphill and the charge must come down. The step size has a name of its own, the learning rate. The toy's whole update is two lines:

Plain descent is stable only at step sizes so small that learning crawls, and the reason is shape: some entries live on persistently tiny slopes, others on huge ones, and one shared step size mistreats both — too large for the twitchy entries, too small for the quiet ones. The standard fix, AdamW, gives each entry its own effective step: keep a running average of the entry's slope, and a running average of its squared size; move by the first divided by the square root of the second, times the learning rate. The division cancels each entry's local scale, so the effective step is roughly uniform across the whole machine — which is what makes deep towers trainable at all. A small per-update shrink of every entry toward zero, decoupled weight decay, completes the recipe [training].

The learning rate itself is a schedule, not one number. Runs begin with a short warmup — a ramp from near zero over the first fraction of a percent of steps — then decay along a slow cosine curve to roughly a tenth of the peak [training]. The magnitudes tell the story: GPT-3, the largest of its era, peaked at 0.00006; the 8B model peaks at 0.0003 and finishes at 0.00003 [training]. The toy moved at 0.2 — a single small floor forgives what a thirty-two-floor tower does not, so real towers take steps hundreds to thousands of times smaller.

One full step, then, is three phases: a forward pass over a batch for the charge, the backward walk for every slope, the update for every entry. Training is that step repeated — millions of times, for months [training].

The sweep, verified

How can the backward walk be trusted? Measure it against its own definition. The nursery slope — nudge an entry, watch the charge — can be computed directly for any single entry: nudge up, nudge down, take the difference of the two charges, divide by the distance between the nudges. The swept slopes are the measured slopes, to less than five parts in a hundred million — the hand-written backward pass is not an approximation of the rule; it is the rule. And the move at the bottom of the panel is the previous page's step seen from inside: every entry in the toy moved once against its measured slope, and the charge fell accordingly.

The rule is now complete — a charge, a walk, a move — and the remaining problem is matter. The toy's verified step runs in milliseconds on a laptop. The same rule, run on the 8B model's sixteen-million-piece batches, is hundreds of quadrillions of multiply-adds per step, repeated millions of times — arithmetic at a scale only one kind of machine does well, and that machine's shape feeds back into every choice that follows, from batch size to model size. Takeaways

  • The gradient is the charge's slope per entry: for every learned table, a same-shaped table of slopes, each cell saying which way to move that entry and how steeply.
  • The chain rule runs slopes backward link by link: at the top it is probability minus truth; at a learned table, stored input times incoming slope; at an add-back, the slope splits over both branches and sums.
  • Backpropagation is one backward walk that prices every entry at roughly two to three times a forward pass — and its price is memory: every intermediate value (the activations) must be kept for the walk.
  • The move is gradient descent — entries step against their slopes at the learning rate. AdamW rescales each entry's step by its own recent history, which is what makes deep towers trainable.
  • The toy's hand-written sweep matched measured slopes to less than one part in a hundred million, then moved its charge from 5.198 to 1.629 in one step.
