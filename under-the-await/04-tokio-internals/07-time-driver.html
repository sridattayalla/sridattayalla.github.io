The time driver

Networks are slow in a way epoll cannot help with. Sometimes the event a task is waiting for is not a packet but a moment: retry in five seconds, give up at noon, tick once per second forever. A runtime that can only sleep on sockets is a runtime without clocks. So Tokio has a second driver, and this chapter is about it. The time driver is the component that owns every sleep in the runtime: it records each deadline, advances with the clock, fires the right wakers at the right instants, and, as you saw on the I/O driver page, caps how long a parked worker may nap.

The requirements sound modest until you scale them. A busy service can hold enormous numbers of armed sleeps at once: every request carrying a deadline, every retry loop, every heartbeat. Inserts and removals happen constantly, deadlines fire thousands of times per second, and all of it must coexist with the scheduler's promise that a worker either runs tasks or sleeps cheaply. The data structure doing that work is the star of this page: the timing wheel.

Why not the obvious designs

Start with the obvious: keep every deadline in one sorted structure.

A sorted list gives you the nearest deadline in a peek, but every insert walks the list, so a burst of sleeps becomes a burst of walks. A binary heap fixes the walk, at the price of logarithmic work on every insert, every removal, and every fire. Neither is wrong; both are more expensive than what Tokio settled on. The stated goal was that all timer operations, insert and remove and fire alike, cost a small constant on average, where a heap pays logarithmic cost.

There is a second, subtler problem: the wake cadence. Whatever holds deadlines must also answer “when is the next one?” every time a worker parks. With a heap that answer is a peek at the root, which is fine; the trouble is that a naive design that re-checks deadlines on a fixed heartbeat burns a core and wrecks the worker's nap. The real design answers “when next?” in constant time and sleeps exactly until then.

The wheel: six levels, sixty-four slots

A timing wheel is a ring of slots, and time walks around the ring: whatever is linked into today's slot fires today. One ring of 64 slots with one slot per millisecond covers only 64 milliseconds of future, which is useless for “retry in five seconds.” A single ring covering years at millisecond precision would need billions of slots. The fix is hierarchy, the same trick a clock face uses: a second hand, a minute hand, an hour hand, each a small ring, together covering an enormous range.

Tokio's wheel is 6 levels of 64 slots each, and the wheel's tick is 1 ms. Level 0 is the finest: each of its slots covers a single millisecond, so the whole level covers 64 ms. Each higher level's slots are 64 times wider than the level below, so the levels stack up like this:

Six small rings of sixty-four slots each cover a maximum sleep of 236 milliseconds, roughly two years. No slot is ever allocated for a sleep that has not been armed, and none is wasted on empty future: a sleep two days out occupies exactly one link, in one slot, on level 4.

Insert: pick a shelf, link one node

When you arm a sleep, the wheel asks which level the deadline belongs to. The rule is a bit trick: find the highest level whose slot width can still represent the deadline exactly enough, then compute the slot index inside that level, then push the entry onto that slot's intrusive linked list. The entry is a small node holding the deadline and the sleeper's waker. No allocation of a ring, no sorting, no heap sift: a handful of integer operations and one link.

Dropping a sleep early is symmetric. A future that gives up before its deadline is simply unlinked from whatever slot holds it, which is the same small constant as the insert. Timers that never fire are almost free, which is what you want, because most sleeps in a real program are defensive deadlines that never trigger.

Tick and cascade

The wheel advances with the clock, one tick per millisecond of elapsed time, and two things happen as it turns.

First, firing. Only level 0 fires. When the wheel's pointer reaches a slot on level 0, the entries linked there have reached their deadlines: their wakers fire, the tasks behind them get queued by the scheduler, and the slot empties. Everything above level 0 is storage, not a firing mechanism.

Second, cascading. When the wheel's pointer wraps around to a slot that is occupied at some higher level, that slot's time has come at its level's granularity, but its entries are not due yet. They are not fired; they are cascaded down exactly one level: each entry is re-linked into a slot at the finer granularity below, where it will sit until it either cascades again or lands in level 0 and fires. A sleep armed for two days out will cascade from level 4 to level 3, and much later from 3 to 2, and so on, one handoff at a time, until it is close enough to live in level 0.

One detail makes the whole thing cheap to drive: each level keeps a 64-bit field with one bit per slot, set when the slot holds any entries. Finding the next occupied slot is a trailing-zeros count on that field, not a scan of the ring, so even a nearly empty wheel answers “anything due?” and “when next?” in a handful of instructions.

sleep, as a future

Here is the user-facing half:

// arm a deadline 250 ms in the future and wait for it
tokio::time::sleep(Duration::from_millis(250)).await;

Underneath, sleep builds a future holding the deadline and a wheel entry wired to the task's waker, links that entry into the right slot, and returns Pending. That is the entire difference from the I/O story of the last chapter: same contract, different bench. A socket parks its waker in a ScheduledIo; a sleep parks its waker in a wheel slot. When the wheel's pointer reaches the slot, the waker fires, the scheduler queues the task, and the next poll sees the deadline reached and returns Ready. The polling side never re-checks the clock in a loop; it sleeps until told.

Interval tickers, retry delays, and every deadline wrapper in the ecosystem reduce to the same arm-and-wait shape. This is also why timer operations had to be cheap: each of those constructs arms and re-arms wheel entries constantly, at program scale.

The wheel sets the pace

Now close the loop with the I/O driver. You learned that a worker with nothing to do parks inside epoll_wait, and that the nap is capped by the nearest pending deadline. Where does that cap come from? The wheel. Parking asks the time driver “when next?”, and the answer is one trailing-zeros peek per level. If the nearest deadline is 30 ms out, the worker hands epoll_wait a maximum wait of 30 ms, and either bytes or the clock will collect it. If nothing is armed anywhere, nothing bounds the nap.

Direction of control matters here, so say it precisely: the time driver owns the clock. Driver turns are what advance the wheel; the wheel does not run on a background thread of its own. A worker parked in the I/O driver is simultaneously the runtime's alarm clock, and busy workers take timer turns between tasks on the same cadence they take I/O turns.

One more structural fact for honesty: the wheel is a single per-runtime structure behind a lock. Timer operations are constant time, but they do serialize on that lock; it is a deliberate trade of simplicity for contention, and an alternative sharded timer exists behind an unstable flag for the multicore-hungry.

With I/O and time both driven, one large piece of the runtime remains unexamined, and it is not machinery at all but the toolbox that tasks use to talk to each other: locks, counters, and pipes. Those are next, and every one of them is a waiter list wearing a different hat.

In this chapter

  • The time driver owns every sleep in the runtime, and its clock advances on driver turns, not on a dedicated thread.
  • Deadlines live in a hierarchical timing wheel: 6 levels of 64 slots, one tick per millisecond, covering sleeps up to about two years.
  • Insert, remove, and fire are all constant-time on average: pick a level, link or unlink one node. No heap, no sorting.
  • Only level 0 fires; higher levels cascade entries down one level at a time as their slots come due.
  • The wheel answers “when next?” with bit tricks, and that answer is the cap on a parked worker's epoll_wait.
