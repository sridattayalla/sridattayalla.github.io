The multi-threaded scheduler

Part 2’s cooperative scheduling page described the general deal: tasks must yield, workers keep run queues, and idle workers steal work from busy ones. This page is that deal implemented, and it is the deepest page of the part. The scheduler is the workers plus the queues they pull from plus the decision procedure every worker runs on every lap of its loop. You already know the workers and the tasks they run; what is left is where a task waits, how a worker finds it, and what stops one greedy task from ruining everyone’s latency.

The three places a task waits

A task that is not running and not finished is always in exactly one of three places: its worker’s LIFO slot, its worker’s local queue, or the global inject queue. They are checked in roughly that order, and they get progressively slower and progressively more shared.

The local queue

Each worker owns a local queue: a fixed ring buffer with capacity for 256 tasks. It is lock-free in the common case because it has exactly one producer, the owning worker itself, and the producer’s end of the ring is written with plain stores. The beautiful part is what the ring contains: not boxes, not handles, but the task headers themselves. The queue is an intrusive linked list threaded through the tasks; a task is its own queue node. Pushing a task costs no allocation at all, just a pointer splice and a bump of the tail index.

When the ring is full, the worker does not block and does not grow anything. It pushes the newest task, plus half of the queue’s current contents (the newer 128 tasks), over to the global inject queue, and alerts its siblings that food is available. Notice it keeps the older half, and the choice is deliberate: tasks a worker adopts out of the inject queue land in the front half of its ring, so by shedding the back half the worker never takes a task out of the inject queue only to hand it straight back. Whatever it adopted keeps its seat until it has been polled at least once.

The global inject queue

The inject queue is the shared front door. Two kinds of traffic use it: tasks submitted from threads that are not workers (the main thread, or the extra threads you will meet on page four), and overflow pushed out of a saturated local ring. It is guarded by a single mutex, which sounds alarming until you remember what it is for: it is the slow path, measured against a lock-free ring that a worker hits thousands of times per second. Correct and boring beats clever here.

The LIFO slot

Each worker also keeps one extra holding cell, the LIFO slot: space for exactly one “run this next” task. The rule is narrow but pays off constantly. When the task a worker is currently polling wakes another task, the woken task goes into this slot, ahead of the whole queue, so it runs immediately after the current poll returns. Message-passing programs turn into ping-pong rallies: a request task wakes the handler, the handler wakes the responder, and each bounce happens with the relevant data still warm in the CPU cache, with no queue traversal in between.

An unbraked fast path like that is a starvation hazard, so it comes with a throttle: the slot only gets priority 3 times per tick, after which the worker ignores it until an ordinary queue task has run. A tick, in this page’s vocabulary, is one lap of a worker’s main loop: pick a task, run it, do the bookkeeping. The counter exists because two tasks that wake each other in a tight loop would otherwise monopolize their worker forever, the same ping-pong that makes the slot valuable making it lethal at full speed. There is a second subtlety worth savoring: a task promoted through the LIFO slot does not get a fresh fairness allowance. It inherits the poll budget of the task that woke it, so a chain of LIFO promotions shares one allowance rather than minting a new one per hop. (Budgets get their own section below.)

The search order

When a worker needs work, it runs the same procedure every time. In order:

  1. The LIFO slot, if the throttle has not disabled it.
  2. The local queue, popping from its own ring.
  3. The global inject queue, but not on every lap: the worker checks it on a cadence, tuned dynamically to land roughly every 10 ms of wall time (the historical hard-coded default was 61 local polls between checks, a number the source comments describe as copied from Go).
  4. Steal: pick a random sibling and try to take half of its local queue. The random start is deliberate; without it, every idle worker would pile onto the same busy victim. And a worker only enters the searching state at all if fewer than half of the workers are already searching, because steal attempts are not free.
  5. Park: nothing to do, anywhere. The worker goes to sleep inside a driver, resting in epoll until the kernel reports an event or the next timer deadline approaches. How a parked worker sleeps, and who wakes it, is page five’s story.

One more cadence knob rounds out the picture: a worker polls the drivers every 61 ticks even while busy, so a worker deep in compute-heavy work still delivers socket events and timer wakes between slices. The searching rule has a mirror image too: when a searching worker finds work, it alerts a parked sibling, because work that just arrived tends to keep arriving.

Stealing, up close

Why half, rather than one task or the whole ring? One task at a time would make stealing a permanent tax: the thief would be back almost immediately, paying the atomic operations again. The whole ring would punish the victim for being productive. Half is the balance point: enough to amortize the cost of the steal across many future polls, little enough that the victim, which is demonstrably the machine’s best source of work, keeps producing. The theft itself is a single atomic multi-word grab against the victim’s ring, the one place where the local queue’s lock-free design has to tolerate foreigners at the door.

The cooperative budget

Everything so far assumed tasks play along: poll, suspend, yield the worker. What if one does not? A tight loop with no await in it never returns control; there is no timer interrupt coming to the rescue, because the kernel does not even know a task exists (Part 1’s preemption does not reach user-space scheduling). Every other task on that worker would wait forever.

The fix is the budget: every task slice carries an allowance of 128 polls. Runtime operations spend from it as the task runs; when the allowance hits zero, the next suspension point returns Pending whether or not the work was ready, and the task is re-filed at the back of a queue with a fresh slice eventually coming. A well-behaved task never notices the budget exists. A badly behaved one is defanged: it still makes progress, poll by poll, but between each slice everyone else gets their turn. This is Part 2’s fairness contract made mechanical, a real number enforced by real code instead of a plea in a style guide.

You can spend the allowance willingly, too, which is the polite thing to do in a long-running loop:

async fn chew_through(mut jobs: Vec<Job>) {
    while let Some(job) = jobs.pop() {
        job.process().await;
        // hand the worker back between jobs
        tokio::task::yield_now().await;
    }
}

yield_now is the explicit version of what the budget does implicitly: suspend here, go to the back of the queue, let a sibling run. Used well, it flattens the latency your longest loops impose on everyone else.

Where a spawn lands

Two cases, from the anatomy page’s wrapping ritual. A task spawned from a worker (from inside other async code) is scheduled the same way a wake is: into that worker’s LIFO slot, or onto the back of its local ring when the throttle has the slot switched off. Same thread, same cache, no coordination. A task spawned from anywhere else lands in the inject queue, because a foreign thread cannot safely touch a worker’s ring:

use tokio::runtime::Runtime;

fn main() {
    let rt = Runtime::new().unwrap();

    // we are on the main thread, not a worker: inject queue
    let handle = rt.spawn(followup_work());

    rt.block_on(handle).unwrap();
}

The inject path costs a mutex and waits for a worker’s adoption cadence, so a burst of work spawned from outside takes slightly longer to start than the same work spawned from inside. In the common shapes of real programs this gap is noise; it is worth knowing it exists, and worth knowing which side of the door you are standing on when you call spawn.

One round trip, start to finish

Here is the whole page compressed into a single story: a file read finishes on one of page four’s extra threads, the follow-up work is spawned, and it crosses the machine to completion.

Walk it once more with the machinery in mind. The outside thread cannot touch any worker’s ring, so step 1 files the new task into the inject queue under its mutex. Worker 0, busy but hitting its global-check cadence, adopts the task (step 2) into its local ring, which is already filling with other woken work (step 3). Worker 1, having found its own slot, ring, and the inject queue empty, picked a sibling at random and stole half of worker 0’s ring (step 4), our task included. It polls the task to completion (step 5), which sets the completion bit and fires the join waker stored in the task’s header (step 6). The task doing the awaiting lives on worker 0, so the wake routes it into worker 0’s LIFO slot (step 7), and on worker 0’s next lap it resumes with the value in hand (step 8). No thread was created, blocked, or harmed in the making of this request.

The one-worker flavor

For completeness: the current-thread runtime runs the same design with the worker count pinned to one. There is no stealing (no siblings), no LIFO slot, and the global queue is checked after 31 consecutive local picks rather than on a time-based cadence. The budget, the queues, and the rules are otherwise identical. The choosing logic is short: if you need work stealing, take multi-thread; if your futures cannot be sent across threads, or you are on one core anyway, the single worker is cheaper and smaller.

In this chapter

  • A waiting task sits in exactly one of three places: its worker’s LIFO slot, its worker’s 256-entry local ring, or the mutex-guarded global inject queue.
  • The local ring is an intrusive list threaded through the task headers; overflow pushes the newer half plus the new task to inject and keeps the older half, so tasks just adopted from inject are not pushed straight back.
  • Workers search slot → ring → inject on a tuned cadence → steal half a random sibling’s ring → park inside a driver.
  • The LIFO slot gives wake-driven ping-pong cache locality but is throttled to 3 uses per tick to prevent starvation, and its passengers inherit the waker’s budget.
  • The budget of 128 polls per slice is the enforcement arm of cooperative fairness: exhaust it and the task is re-queued at the back, ready or not.
