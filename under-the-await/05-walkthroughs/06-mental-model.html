The full-circle mental model

Five parts ago, the question was why one connection per thread dies at ten thousand connections, and what could possibly replace it. You have since built the answer from the kernel up: syscalls, readiness, state machines, wakers, workers, drivers, channels. This page puts the whole thing back together in one diagram and one loop, so that the next time you stare at a hung .await, you can walk it yourself, station by station, and know exactly which piece to suspect.

The loop

The loop, narrated

  1. Your code awaits. The compiler already turned the surrounding async function into a state machine, and the await is the seam where it hands control away: the desugaring made poll out of your straight-line code.
  2. Something polls it. At the very start that something is block_on; after the first spawn it is a worker. Poll runs your code synchronously until it reaches a wait: the Future contract.
  3. The wait is not ready. The future stashes the waker that came with this poll, returns Pending, and control flows back out to the executor: the waker is the whole callback, owned by the caller instead of escaped into.
  4. The task parks. The state machine, boxed and pinned on the heap, sits in no run queue at all; only the driver's waiter lists remember it exists: task anatomy.
  5. The world moves. A packet lands in a socket buffer somewhere in the kernel, or a deadline expires inside the timing wheel; either way, something now knows what your task was waiting to hear.
  6. Epoll reports, the token resolves. The driver's single readiness call returns the ready file descriptor, and the token maps it to the waker stashed in step three.
  7. Wake means queue. The waker pushes the task onto a run queue, a pointer and nothing more; that is all a wake has ever been.
  8. A worker adopts and polls again. Through the LIFO slot, the inject queue, or a steal from a neighboring worker: the runtime closes the loop. This poll returns Ready and your code exits at the top right, or Pending and the lap begins again.

Five parts, one machine

Part 1 was the kernel half. Programs are processes running instructions in user space; threads are the kernel's unit of execution, expensive in stacks and context switches; input and output are syscalls on file descriptors; blocking on them parks a whole thread per waiter; and readiness, EAGAIN plus epoll, is the kernel's escape hatch from that arithmetic.

Part 2 was the strategy half. Thread pools recycle blockers but cannot manufacture concurrency; callbacks and event loops scale but tangle control flow; green threads buy ergonomics with stacks; stackless coroutines keep the ergonomics and drop the stacks; and cooperative scheduling makes yielding the program's job, which is exactly what await became.

Part 3 was the language half. Rust made await a return: a future is a poll-me state machine, the waker is the callback reborn under executor control, Pin keeps self-referential state machines valid across those returns, and fifty lines in a mini executor proved the whole contract runs without any runtime at all.

Part 4 was the industrial half. Tokio wraps epoll in a driver, futures in boxed tasks, and tasks in workers with budgets, LIFO slots, and stealing; gives blocking work a pool of its own; arms sleeps in a wheel; makes channels and locks that park tasks instead of threads; and defines cancellation as an ordinary drop with rules you must respect.

Part 5 was the practice half. The boot sequence showed the machine assembling itself; the echo server showed every await walking into it; the combinators showed concurrency without tasks; the pipelines showed tasks talking through bounded channels; and the pitfalls showed the five ways the machine bites back when its rules are ignored.

Walking a hung await

The loop is not just a summary; it is a debugging protocol. When a task never wakes, walk the circle and ask one question per station. Was the future polled at all: is the task in any queue, and did whoever spawned it keep it alive? Did the poll reach the wait and stash the waker, or did it panic or bail with an error before registering interest anywhere? Did the world move: did the packet actually arrive, the deadline actually pass? Did the wake fire: does the token resolve to the waker that was stashed, or did a losing select branch drop the interest on its way out? Did the queue move: is a worker wedged inside a blocking call, the first sin from the pitfalls page, or starved by a task that refuses to yield? And did the resumed poll finish, or is it looping on Pending because the readiness it wanted was consumed by a racing branch? Every async hang you will ever debug is one of those stations failing, and every station has a chapter in this book.

What you actually saved

Here is the answer to Part 1's question, properly stated. Async did not remove the syscall: epoll still runs, packets still cross the kernel boundary, and a read still ends in a read. What async removed is the waiter. A parked thread costs its full stack, 8 MB of reserved address space by default on Linux, plus a kernel wait queue entry and a context switch to return to it. A parked task costs a few hundred bytes of heap and one waker on a list. Ten thousand waiters stop being ten thousand allocations of a scarce kernel resource and become ten thousand cheap heap objects, invisible until the moment their reason arrives. Concurrency stopped being a resource you allocate and became a property of how you wait; that is the entire dividend, and every page of this book was an inventory of how it gets paid out.

The honesty clause: none of this is free. Every task is a heap allocation, every lap around the circle is a poll, every wake is a queue push and a scheduling decision, and the combinators and channels add their own state machines to yours. The machinery pays its way when waiters outnumber cores by a wide margin; for a handful of long-running blocking jobs, plain threads remain simpler, cheaper to reason about, and fully supported by the same runtime through the blocking pool. The mental model is not a religion; it is a cost table. You now know what each kind of waiting costs, where the cost is paid, and who pays it, which was the point of walking under the await in the first place.

The next time a program of yours serves ten thousand connections on a handful of threads, or times out a thousand operations with one wheel, or shuts down a pipeline by dropping a sender, you will not need this book: you will need the loop. Follow the await into the poll, watch it stash the waker and park, wait for the world to move, and meet it again when a worker picks it up. Under every await you ever write, the machine is doing exactly this, and nothing else, until there is a reason.
