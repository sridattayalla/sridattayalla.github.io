Threads vs events: the tradeoff

Part 1 assembled the machine, and somewhere along the way you may have noticed a fork in the road. One path is chapter 2's: give every concurrent thing a thread, let blocking calls put threads to sleep, and let the kernel's preemptive scheduling juggle them. The other path is chapters 5 and 6's: keep a few threads, make every socket non-blocking, and react, through one sleeping question, to whatever the kernel reports. These are the two classic architectures for concurrency, and programmers have argued about them for thirty years with religious intensity. This chapter refuses the religion. It writes out the ledger, honestly, line by line, and then points at the hybrid that takes the best of both, because that hybrid is where this book is going.

What exactly is being traded

Strip both designs to their skeletons and the same five questions separate them. Who decides when a running computation gives up the CPU? What does a concurrent thing cost in memory? What can go wrong when things share state? What shape does the code take? And which failures do you fear at 3 a.m.? None of these has a right answer. Each is a trade, and the two architectures take opposite sides of every single one.

The ledger

QuestionThread per connection Events on few threads
who yields the CPU, and when the kernel, preemptively, at any instant (chapter 2) your code, voluntarily, between reactions
memory per concurrent thing megabytes of stack reservation per thread, 8 MiB by default on Linux a small blob of saved state per suspended job, often a few hundred bytes
what sharing gets you in trouble data races on shared memory, guarded by mutexes no unintended sharing, but control flow is inverted and fragmented
the shape of the code a linear story per connection; reads like the protocol one reactive loop plus handlers; reads like a state machine
the 3 a.m. failure a deadlock, or a server crushed under ten thousand stacks (chapter 4) one long-running reaction starving every other connection

Two rows deserve expansion, because they carry the most weight in practice. The memory row is arithmetic, and chapter 4 already ran it: a stack costs megabytes before doing anything, while a suspended job's saved state costs roughly what its local variables cost, bytes to a few hundred bytes. That is a factor of ten thousand or more between the two units of concurrency, and it is the single number that decides which designs are even candidates at a million simultaneous things.

The failure row is subtler. The thread design's failures are loud and well understood: deadlocks hang, races corrupt, and both have forty years of tooling aimed at them. The event design's failures are quiet and structural. Because one thread runs every connection, a handler that computes for a second stalls every connection behind it, and nothing crashes, nothing logs, nothing preempts: the machine simply stops serving until the handler returns. Fair sharing between connections is not given by the event design; it has to be built, deliberately, out of the places where the programmer chooses to hand control back. Cooperation must be enforced, because the kernel's preemptive safety net is gone.

Neither one is fast

A tempting frame, and a wrong one, is "events are faster". Faster at what? For latency on a single connection, a thread that sleeps in a blocking read has a beautifully short path: packet arrives, kernel wakes the thread, data flows. For throughput with ten thousand mostly idle connections, events win by default because the thread design cannot even field that many players, as chapter 4's arithmetic showed. Flip to ten thousand busy connections on many cores, and the interesting question becomes how either design divides work across cores, at which point thread designs grow their work queues and event designs grow their per-core loops, and both start resembling each other. The honest summary: blocking-on-threads optimizes for simplicity of each individual story; events optimize for the number of simultaneous stories. Neither speed claim survives without its qualifier.

Latency and throughput deserve one concrete scenario each, because abstractions hide the mechanics. Latency: a single client sends a request and waits. The thread design's path is packet, wake the one sleeping thread, read, reply; the event design's path is packet, wake the one loop, run the handler, reply. Both are microseconds of machinery; what dominates is whether anything else was queued ahead of you. Throughput: ten thousand clients each deliver a request, once a minute. The thread design spends nearly all of its life swapping between sleepers and paying stack memory for the privilege; the event design spends its life asleep inside the big question, waking per packet, with waiters costing bytes. The designs do not merely score differently on these scenarios. They score differently on the axis you are usually not measuring when you pick one by feel.

The hybrid both sides drift toward

Here is the punchline toward which all of Part 1 has been walking. Once you accept that stacks are expensive and that preemptive sharing between many thousands of things is unaffordable, but you refuse to give up either multicore parallelism or comfortable, story-shaped code, a specific architecture suggests itself. Take a small number of OS threads, typically one per core. On each, run a reactive loop in the style of chapter 6, sleeping in the one big question whenever nothing is ready. And represent each connection not by a thread but by a cheap suspended computation: a frozen stack's worth of meaning, stored as a small object, resumed exactly at the points where it asked to wait.

That is the destination. A few kernel threads supply parallelism and the preemptive safety net between loops. Millions of cheap suspended jobs supply the concurrency. The kernel's ready list decides which job to resume, and the machinery that manages all of this, the queues, the waking, the fair sharing between jobs, is ordinary user-space code, built from exactly the primitives you have now met: file descriptors, non-blocking sockets, one sleeping question, and frozen computations that resume where they left off. It is threads' unit of waiting with events' unit of cost.

Building that machinery, or rather meeting the design that won in the Rust ecosystem, is what the rest of this book does. But there is a gap between "small object that resumes where it left off" and code you would actually want to write, and humanity has invented five distinct answers to bridging it: reused threads, functions handed to loops, user-space stacks, compiler-suspended functions, and disciplined hand-offs between them. Those five answers, with their real names and their real costs, are Part 2.

In this chapter

  • Threads and events take opposite sides of every real trade: preemptive versus voluntary yielding, megabytes versus bytes per concurrent thing, races versus inverted control.
  • Neither design is "faster" without a qualifier: threads give each connection a short, simple path; events give scale by making waiters nearly free.
  • The event design's characteristic failure is a long reaction starving every other connection; fair sharing must be built in, because no timer interrupt will rescue you.
  • The hybrid destination: a few OS threads, one reactive loop each, and millions of cheap suspended jobs resumed by the kernel's ready list.
  • Part 2 presents the five classic strategies for expressing those suspended jobs before this book commits to one.
