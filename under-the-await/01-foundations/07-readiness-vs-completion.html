Readiness vs completion

Chapter 6 gave you the readiness model and called it the foundation of everything to come. That is true, but it is not the whole truth: it is one of two contracts the world's kernels offer, and the second one answers a question you may not have thought to ask. Why should the kernel merely tell you that data arrived? Why not have the kernel do the read for you, into a buffer you hand over in advance, and tell you when it is done? That is the completion model, and it powers Windows I/O and a bold, relatively new Linux interface called io_uring. This chapter puts the two contracts side by side, because the choice between them shapes every layer built on top, including the machinery this book is walking toward.

The contract you already know

Recall the shape of readiness. You register a socket with an epoll instance; the kernel reports it as ready; then, and this is the part to look at closely, you call read, you supply the buffer, and the kernel fills that buffer during your call, on your thread, before returning. The kernel's role is a doorman: it tells you which doors are unlocked. Walking through them is your job, one door at a time, each walk a separate trip through the syscall door from chapter 3.

That division of labor has a quiet virtue. Between the moment the kernel says "socket B is ready" and the moment you act on it, the buffer does not exist yet as far as the kernel is concerned. You allocate it when you are about to read, the kernel touches it for the duration of one call, and then it is yours again. Nothing of yours is in the kernel's hands while you wait, because waiting already happened before the buffer existed.

The other contract: the kernel finishes the job

The completion model flips the roles. You describe the entire operation up front, which descriptor, what operation, into which buffer, how many bytes, and hand it to the kernel. The kernel takes the job away, performs it whenever the data actually arrives, writes the result directly into your buffer, and later hands you a small record saying the operation finished: a completion. Your thread never calls read for that data at all. It asked once, walked away, and came back to find the work done.

Windows has worked this way for a long time. The I/O Completion Ports mechanism lets a program start an overlapped operation, say a network receive, by handing the kernel a buffer and a small bookkeeping structure. The kernel fills the buffer on its own schedule and posts a completion record to a queue; any of your threads can harvest that queue at its convenience. Linux grew its own version much later, and it is interesting enough to deserve its own section.

The buffer question

Look at what completion demands of you. The buffer you hand over must stay valid, and stay untouched by you, for as long as the operation is in flight, because the kernel is writing into it while you do other things. Lend out the buffer, forget, and use it for something else, and you have two writers and one victim. io_uring's documentation states the rule plainly: buffer pointers used for reads and writes must remain valid until the operation finishes.

This is the deepest structural difference between the models, deeper than any performance claim. Readiness lets your buffers be short-lived locals, born and dying inside one call on your own thread. Completion forces buffers to be long-lived resources with rules about who holds them and when they come home. Every programming model built on completion ends up with an answer to "who owns the buffer while the kernel works", sometimes an elaborate one, and we will see the echoes of that question much later in this book. For now, hold the asymmetry: under readiness, waiting is over before the buffer exists; under completion, the buffer exists precisely because waiting has been delegated.

io_uring: Linux grows a completion interface

io_uring, introduced around 2019 and matured since, is Linux's completion interface, and its design has one trick that makes it unlike anything before it. The name is literal: there are two rings, a submission queue and a completion queue, and both are regions of memory shared between your program and the kernel. To start an operation you write a submission entry into the shared ring, describing the operation with a small structure: operation code, descriptor, buffer pointer, length, offset. No trip through the syscall door is required to submit, and none to check for completions either, because both queues are plain memory you can read. The kernel consumes submissions, does the work asynchronously, and pushes completion entries onto the other ring for you to harvest.

Why go to this trouble? Syscalls themselves were the cost left on the table. The maintainers of a heavily tuned TCP proxy built on the readiness model measured it spending 70% to 80% of its CPU cycles inside the kernel, mostly on the very door-knocking that readiness still requires, and io_uring was motivated largely by eliminating those trips. It also fixes a blind spot epoll never covered: regular files. Disk reads do not become "ready" in a way epoll can report, so the readiness model has never applied to them; a completion interface simply performs the read whenever the disk delivers it. That is why, when you meet the machinery of Part 4, its experimental io_uring support covers file operations first.

Which contract does the winner use?

Given all that, you might expect every modern system to have moved to completion. The reality is more conservative, and the machinery this book dissects in Part 4 is a good example of why. On Linux it answers "readiness": its central I/O layer is a thin portability wrapper that picks epoll on Linux, kqueue on the BSDs and macOS, and the completion-based Windows mechanism, and presents one interface to everything above. Its io_uring support is real but switched off by default, marked experimental, and mostly covers file operations, where readiness cannot help; the networking path still rides epoll.

The reasons are the reasons that usually win in systems software. Maturity: epoll has two decades of hardening, bug fixes, and operational scar tissue, and io_uring's API churned through several redesigns while growing up. Portability: the readiness model maps onto every mainstream kernel with one wrapper, while a completion-first design has to fake completion where the kernel offers none. And fit: the ecosystem of higher-level code, including everything you will meet in Parts 2 and 3, grew up assuming readiness semantics. A completion model is not an upgrade you can bolt on; it is a different architecture, most visibly in that buffer question. So readiness remains the default, io_uring is the frontier, and you now hold the concepts to understand both, plus the tradeoff that separates them.

That closes the inventory of the machine. You have threads and their price, the kernel door and its integer handles, blocking and its wait queues, non-blocking sockets and readiness, one sleeping question for thousands of descriptors, and now the two contracts a kernel can offer about I/O. What remains is to weigh the two great strategies, many threads or few threads plus many small waiters, against each other honestly. That weighing is the last chapter of Part 1.

In this chapter

  • Under readiness (epoll, kqueue), the kernel grants permission and you perform the read yourself, on your thread, with your buffer.
  • Under completion (Windows I/O completion ports, Linux io_uring), you describe the operation up front and the kernel performs it, writing results into a buffer you surrendered.
  • The buffer rule is the structural difference: completion requires buffers that stay valid and untouched until the operation finishes, which reshapes every layer above.
  • io_uring's two shared-memory rings, submission and completion, remove even the syscall from the common path, motivated by measurements showing 70% to 80% of cycles spent in the kernel under the readiness model.
  • The machinery of Part 4 uses readiness on Linux today, epoll under a portability wrapper, with io_uring support experimental and file-focused: maturity and portability beat raw syscall counts.
