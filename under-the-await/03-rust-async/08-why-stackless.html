Why Rust chose stackless

You have now built the entire machinery by hand: a future, a waker, a desugaring, a memory rule, an engine. This last page of Part 3 steps back from the workbench and asks the design question: of all the shapes Part 2 surveyed, why did Rust bet its language on this one, the stackless coroutine compiled into a state machine? The honest answer is a ledger, not a slogan: a column of costs, a column of payoffs, and a philosophical wager about what a standard library is for. Read the ledger and you will also understand why every cost you paid in the last seven pages, the pinning rules, the colored functions, the hand-built engine, was accepted with eyes open.

The two candidates, one last time

On the green side: green threads, the Go and Java answer. Each concurrent thing gets its own real stack, managed by the engine, and suspension can happen anywhere, because the engine just saves the whole stack and switches to another one. On the Rust side: the thing you just built. Each concurrent thing is a compiler-generated state machine, suspension happens only at the marked points you wrote yourself (.await), and there is no per-thing stack at all: the "stack" degenerates into the struct's live fields, the locals that straddle an await.

The memory difference is the heart of it. A suspended green thread drags a full stack behind it, kilobytes at the very best and megabytes at default settings, most of it untouched. A suspended future is exactly as large as the data it is actually holding at that await point, often a few dozen bytes: the connection handle, the loop counter, the buffer. At ten thousand concurrent things both designs work. At a million, only one of them fits in memory, and it is the one where a paused computation costs about as much as a small struct.

The philosophy: std ships the primitive, not the engine

Rust's standard library is small by the standards of managed languages, and that is a policy, not an accident. When async was designed, in RFC 2592, the decision was explicit: the language and std would define the contract, the Future trait, Poll, the waker machinery, and nothing that schedules or drives anything. No queues, no worker threads, no readiness integration in std. The RFC draws the line in one sentence: it defines the interaction between futures and engines, and includes no engine. Everything you built on the previous two pages, the executor, the ready queue, the park loop, is deliberately not the standard library's job.

Green threads could not live by that rule. An engine that swaps stacks is load-bearing machinery: it must own thread creation, sizing, growth, and the points where switching happens, and every program linked against the language pays for it whether it starts one green thread or none. The stackless design lets Rust keep its promise: a program that never writes async links no engine at all, and a program that does picks its own. The result was an ecosystem competition, several engines fighting it out in the wild, and Tokio emerging as the de facto standard that Part 4 dissects. Fragmentation was the price of that freedom; a winning engine was the outcome.

The costs, paid in full

Be honest about what the ledger charged Rust, because you paid each line by hand in this part.

Pin. The heaviest item. Letting a paused computation be a movable value while it may hold a pointer into itself forced the entire pinning apparatus into the language: a wrapper type, an auto trait, a contract in every poll signature. No stackless design in a memory-safe language escapes this; Go escapes it only by never letting you hold such a value at all.

Function coloring. Sync cannot call async. The two worlds share a type system but not a call graph, and the seam shows: block_on at the boundary, carefully chosen sync alternatives inside, and whole ecosystems split along the line. This is the classic coloring problem from Part 2, and Rust chose it with open eyes because the alternative, making every function suspendable, is exactly the green-thread bet.

Language friction. The state-machine transform interacted badly with traits for a decade: async functions in traits were only stabilized in Rust 1.75, released in December 2023, and they still carry caveats, since such traits cannot be used as trait objects, and multi-threaded engines demand explicit Send annotations the compiler cannot infer for you. Async closures took until Rust 1.85, in early 2025. Each item worked out; the decade of workarounds was a real cost, paid in ecosystem time.

Ecosystem churn. With the engine outside std, every library that touched async had to choose which engine abstractions to build on, and the early years shipped several incompatible answers. The consolidation around Tokio ended the churn, but the scar tissue, adapter crates, re-exports, and "which engine is this for" folklore, is still part of the daily experience.

The payoff, banked

Against the column of costs, three returns, all of which you have personally verified in this part.

Scale. A million paused computations is a mundane workload, not a hero feat, because each one costs its live locals and nothing more. You ran three timers on one thread with zero extra stacks; Tokio runs the same shape with work stealing across a pool the size of your cores. The C10K arithmetic that opened this book dissolves when the unit of suspension is a struct.

Composition without tax. Awaiting a future is a function call into its poll: no allocation, no thread handoff, no lock. You stacked a hand-written future inside a compiler-generated one inside your own engine, and the only machinery that moved was one waker per level. Compare the cost of the same composition under thread pools, one kernel object and one stack per unit, or under callbacks, one heap-allocated continuation per step.

Visible suspension. Every point where control can leave your function is written .await, in the source, where you can see it. When a function holds a lock across a suspension point, or yields between a read and a write, the bug is on the page, not buried in an engine's switching policy. The cooperation contract from Part 2 is enforced by grammar: no .await, no yield, and no hidden preemption exists to save you from a long computation, which is precisely why Part 4 will be so strict about where blocking work goes.

Through the engine-room door

You now hold the complete mental model that Part 4 assumes: the workhorse object there is a pinned future with scheduling metadata bolted on; the engine loop you wrote is the skeleton of Tokio's workers; the waker you fired from a thread is the same object Tokio's epoll machinery fires from kernel readiness. Every remaining mystery is a matter of scale and engineering: run queues with stealing, a separate pool of ordinary threads for work that must sleep in the kernel, timing machinery that replaces your thread-per-timer, and the whole apparatus assembled behind one attribute. Part 4 opens that door. Everything behind it is made of parts you have already held in your hands.

In this chapter

  • Rust chose stackless futures over green threads on memory (live locals versus a full stack per paused thing), on explicitness (visible .await points), and on standard-library philosophy (ship the contract, not the engine).
  • The costs were real and are now familiar: the pinning apparatus, function coloring, a decade of trait friction, and ecosystem fragmentation before Tokio became the de facto standard.
  • The payoff is scale and composition: a million suspended computations at tens of bytes each, and awaits that compile to plain calls with no hidden switching.
  • Std's async surface is deliberately minimal: Future, Poll, the waker machinery; every queue, worker, and readiness integration belongs to the engine you choose.
