Four Words · 07

Yoga: The Yoke

Yoga in the Gita is not postures — it is a yoke: any discipline that harnesses you to a path, and the poem offers three.

The previous page left you holding three words in one: karma the deed, karma the consequence, karma the cycle the consequences drive. It left you holding the poem's move against that cycle — not inaction but detached action, give up the claim on the fruits, not the act. One question survives the move. If action cannot stop, and the fruits were never yours, how are you meant to hold the acting itself? The poem has a word for the how, and it is the third of the four.

From yoke to discipline

The word is yoga. It grows from the Sanskrit root yuj: to yoke, to join, to harness an animal to a load. The English word yoke is its cousin, descended from the same ancient root by a different road, and the family resemblance is the meaning. A yoke is not decoration. It is the curved wooden collar that binds an ox to the plough and to the ploughman's aim, and it works by holding together two things that would otherwise pull apart: animal and load, strength and direction. Yoga, in this poem's mouth, is that collar. A discipline, in the old sense, is not a hobby or a mood. It is a method that binds you to something and aims your strength.

Notice how wide the word runs once you hear it. In the Gita, yoga names whole ways of life and specific techniques, and — this is the part a first-time reader does not expect — it names states of being. Krishna will say at one point that evenness itself, sameness of mind in gain and loss, is called yoga: the road and the arrival share one word. The poem is not using the term narrowly. It is using it the way a farmer does, for the whole apparatus of harness in a single syllable.

What the word does not mean, in this poem, is what a modern gym means by it. No postures, no mats, no studio. The familiar poses and breathing drills arrive centuries later, in other texts, aimed at bodies and energies rather than at a chariot parked between two armies. The most physical the Gita ever gets is chapter 6, a meditation manual with a seat, a rule against too much or too little food and sleep, and a mind held steady like a flame in a windless place — and even there the harness is the point. The poem trusts the word enough to describe itself with it: every chapter closes with the colophon you met on the map page, sealing the dialogue as a scripture of yoga, a yogaśāstra. What you are reading calls itself a book about harnessing.

The three paths

Harnessed to what, exactly? The poem answers with three compounds, each welding yoga to the fuel it runs on. The first is karma-yoga, the discipline of action: the previous page's detached action, given a name and a training. You act, because you cannot help acting. You release the claim on the fruits, because they were never yours to bank. The doing itself becomes the discipline. The second is jñāna-yoga, the discipline of knowledge — and the knowledge at stake has its own name, jñāna, which the next section takes apart. The third is bhakti-yoga, the discipline of devotion: the same yoke, buckled to a person rather than a principle, with Krishna himself in the traces. Action, knowledge, devotion. Three teams, one collar.

Watch what each harness does with the problem the last page left standing. Karma-yoga takes it head-on: you act, and the discipline is how you hold the acting. Jñāna-yoga digs under it: what you need is not a better technique for acting but a knowledge that changes the actor. Bhakti-yoga transposes it: act, but as an offering, the fruits handed over in the moment of doing. None of the three tells you to stop. They differ about where the grip goes — on the deed, on the truth, or on the beloved.

And the poem does not keep the three in separate rooms. A chapter devoted to acting invokes knowledge midway through; a teaching on knowledge lands in devotion at its close. What the poem never posts is a scoreboard. Which discipline is supreme is a question the text opens and readers never closed — and the argument over the answer starts inside the poem itself, which is the next section's business.

Jñāna

Jñāna is knowledge — but start with what it is not, because the negation is the fastest way in. It is not information. You can hold a thousand facts about the sea and still drown; you can recite a map of a city and be lost in it at night. Information is knowledge about. Jñāna is knowledge of: direct, lived, and it changes the one who has it.

Specifically, it is the knowledge the Upaniṣads pursue. The forest dialogues you met under the poem's floor are after the self — the deathless watching beneath the changing body and mind — and that watcher gets its own page next; here, take the kind of knowing rather than its content. Jñāna is not gathered the way facts are gathered, a shelf at a time. It is arrived at, the way you arrive at knowing a person after years at close quarters, except that what you come to know is the one doing the knowing. The poem will later claim that nothing purifies like this knowledge, that it burns the residue of action the way fire turns fuel to ash (chapter 4) — a claim this book will walk through slowly when it gets there.

On the knowledge path, jñāna is the engine, not the cargo. The discipline exists to deliver you to this knowledge and to hold you steady inside it once it comes, which is why the compound is welded the way it is: jñāna-yoga is not knowledge plus some unrelated practice, it is the harness whose whole aim is that seeing. Shankara, whom you met on the dharma page, will make this path the summit of the entire poem. He is not the last word on the question. Hold him for one more section.

The fork previewed

The dispute over which path is supreme does not wait for commentators; it starts inside the poem. At the hinge of chapter 2, Krishna says in a single verse that he has taught the wisdom and will now teach the discipline of action (2.39) — two paths, side by side, delivered to a man still sitting with his bow cast aside. Arjuna hears the fork and asks the natural question: if you hold knowledge the stronger, why do you urge me to this terrible act? Chapter 3 exists to answer him, and its answer defends action — for the world's upkeep, for the actor's own steadiness. Then the poem spends its middle chapters crowning a third path: the devotional chapters put love on the throne, one after another, and chapter 6 has already ended by calling the worshipper the best of the disciplined. The ranking argument is not imported into the Gita by later readers. It is the poem's own furniture.

Even the poem's architecture got drafted into the argument. You remember the map page's three blocks of six chapters — response, divine, self. A thousand years back, a reader named Yāmunācārya sorted those blocks under the three disciplines: the first six under action, the middle six under devotion, the last six under knowledge. Madhusudana Sarasvati, writing in sixteenth-century Benares, systematized the same cut, and both readers add that every block holds all three disciplines inside itself — the sorting is a lens, not a fence. Read their way, the poem moves from acting, through loving, to knowing.

Then the readers inherit the fork, and they divide exactly along it. Shankara crowns knowledge: works purify the mind, but freedom comes through knowing, and the discipline of action is a stepping stone, not the summit. Ramanuja crowns surrender: the poem's true climax is the verse that tells you to drop every duty and take refuge (18.66) — you watched him plant that flag on the dharma page — and disciplined works become the body of that refuge rather than its rival. Madhva crowns devotion: the goal is eternal, distinct, loving service to a sovereign God, with action and knowledge both in service of it. Three readers, three crowns, one poem.

This book will not referee. The walk ahead will hand you the fork each time the poem raises it, show you the readings, and leave the crown where the poem left it: offered, argued, unassigned. But notice what the three disciplines agree on, because it is the door into the last of the four words. Every harness assumes a neck. Every discipline assumes someone wearing it — someone to act, or to know, or to love. Who is that one, and what does it stand on? The final two words of the four are about exactly that: who is acting, and what the self stands on.

Takeaways

  1. Yoga is a yoke: a discipline that binds you to a path — the Gita uses the word for methods, not stretches.
  2. The poem names three great disciplines: action, knowledge, devotion.
  3. Jñāna is the knowledge of the self the Upaniṣads pursue — one of yoga's engines.
  4. Which path is supreme is a fork the Gita itself opens and readers never closed.
