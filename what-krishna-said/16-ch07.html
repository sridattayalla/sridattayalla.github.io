Chapter 7 — The Two Natures of God

The second hexad opens with an inventory: God's lower nature is the world's matter, and it is a maze only refuge exits.

Chapter 6 closed its manual with a quiet promotion: of all yogis, the highest is the one who worships with faith. Repetition and dispassion could hold the mind, but trust outranked mastery — and the manual never said what the trust was aimed at. Chapter 7 opens the second hexad, the six chapters the map at the front of this book calls the divine, and lets the worshipped one answer for himself. The word for him is īśvara, the lord — an owner's word, built on the Sanskrit root for ruling and possessing — and this chapter is where the poem's God begins to itemize himself.

Eightfold and higher

The chapter opens with a boast dressed as an offer. Fasten your mind on me, take refuge in me, Krishna says, and I will tell you in full what I am — knowledge joined with discernment, than which nothing else remains to be known (verses 7.1 to 7.2). The poem has made large promises before: the deathless self, the way through action. This one is larger — total knowledge, offered in a single sitting, between two armies. Then, having raised the stakes, the chapter delivers a parts list.

Earth, water, fire, wind, ether, mind, discernment, egoity. Eight items, named in verse 7.4 as my lower nature — a prakṛti, the word chapter 3 gave you for nature's whole moving order, here divided eightfold. Watch the list climb. It starts where a battlefield is most literal — the earth under the chariot wheels, the water, the fire, the wind on the plain — passes through ether, and then keeps going past matter entirely: mind, then discernment, then ahaṅkāra, the I-maker, egoity itself. The inventory includes you. Your thinking and your sense of being someone are shelved beside mud and wind. And all eight are called lower.

The second nature gets one line, and the line hangs on a verb. Jīva-bhūtā, the life-element — the living thread — and what it does is dhāryate, holds fast: by this the world is carried. The lower nature is what beings are made of; the higher is what carries beings. The image sharpens two verses on: there is nothing higher than me, and all this is strung on me as pearls on a thread (verse 7.7). Lower nature, beads — you among them; higher nature, the string running through them. Notice the care of the doctrine. Matter is not dismissed: both natures are called wombs of beings (verse 7.6), both are his. But one is named lower, and it is held up by something alive. What the pair adds up to — one lord in two aspects, or two orders of thing in one owner — is a question the later lenses of this book will fight over. The poem here only insists on the count: two, and the second one carries.

One number before the chapter moves. Among thousands of humans, verse 7.3 says, one strives toward perfection; among the perfected, one knows me as I am. The chapter knows it is describing a rare object, and it warns you before it begins. Keep the arithmetic in mind. Verse 7.19 will return it with interest.

The maze

Then the inventory becomes a hiding place. Through verses 7.8 to 7.12 the lord catalogs himself inside ordinary things — the taste in water, the light in sun and moon, the syllable Om in the Vedas, the seed of all beings — presence threaded through the everyday and labeled as none of it. And nobody notices. Verse 7.13 gives the reason: deluded by the strands — the three threads of nature's weave you met in chapter 3 — the world does not know him. The next verse names the trap, and the chapter leans its weight on it.

Four words do the work. Daivī, divine: the power that hides him is his own, not a rival's. Guṇamayī, strand-woven: the maze is made of the machinery of nature itself, which is why the maze is competent. Mama, mine: the possessive again — the lord whose name means owner keeps saying my. Duratyayā, hard to cross. Put together: you are inside a fabric, the fabric hides the one who wove it, and the weave was done well. Then the exit clause, and its shape is the chapter's real surprise. Not those who understand it cross. Not those who map the strands. Those who take refuge in me alone — mām eva, me only — cross it. The chapter's own colophon names it jñānavijñāna, knowledge joined to realization, and the chapter then declines to let you think your way out. The exit is not taken by analysis; it is taken by refuge. You do not solve the weave. You are carried out of it by the one who wove it. The verse after is colder — those who do not take refuge are fools, it says, lost to the worst of natures — so the maze chapter does not pretend everyone finds the door.

Four kinds of seeker

So who does take the exit? The chapter turns from the trap to its refugees, and what it produces is a field guide to why people pray.

Four motivations, listed without a sneer. Ārta, the distressed — pain comes first because pain is the most common door into a temple. Jijñāsu, the knowledge-seeker — curiosity, the arrival wanting answers. Arthārthī, the wealth-seeker — the list is honest enough to include the transactional worshipper, the one praying for outcomes. And the jñānī, the knower. Before any ranking happens, all four are called sukṛtinaḥ, people of good deed. The poem has watched the whole spectrum of human motive arrive at one door, and it describes what it sees like a naturalist, not a bouncer.

Then the ranking (verses 7.17 to 7.18): of the four, the best is the knower, the one constantly at it — I am supremely dear to him, the verse runs, and he to me. But watch what the ranking does not do. It does not close the gate. All four are called noble; the knower's superiority is stated as intimacy, not as admission. The chapter could have said that only the knower truly worships and the rest are wasting incense. It says the opposite. The distressed and the wealth-seeker stand inside the temple, called virtuous, ranked lower. Chapter 7 orders motives and refuses to shame the people holding them.

The rare arrival

Now the long road. What does the knower's arrival look like, walked all the way out?

Bahūnāṃ janmanām ante — at the end of many births. The arithmetic promised at the chapter's opening is kept at its center: the wise one walks the whole road, birth after birth of saṃsāra, the round you met in Part 1, and arrives where the distressed arrived in an afternoon — at refuge. The Sanskrit verb is the same verb the maze verse used for its exit, prapadyate. The circuit closes with an almost comic economy: knowledge, walked far enough, lands exactly where pain begins, at the feet of the same one.

And the content of the arrival is a single sentence, the Sanskrit under the gloss: vāsudevaḥ sarvam — Vasudeva is everything. Vasudeva is Krishna's own name, the family name he carries onto this field; the line turns a patronymic into a metaphysics, and later Vaishnava theology will build on exactly this verse. The poem's own comment is colder. Sa mahātmā sudurlabhaḥ — such a great soul is very rare. Mahātman, great-souled, is the word a later century will pin on Gandhi; here it marks an arrival the text itself calls nearly unfindable. For a poem that keeps insisting the goal is open to anyone who will practice, verse 7.19 is a cold splash of honesty: the door stands open and the arrival is rare, and both are asserted in the same breath.

Steadying every faith

Then the chapter's widest opening — set up first by a fence. Verse 7.20 describes worshippers whose discernment desire has carried off: they take refuge in other gods, each following their own nature, under whatever rules they keep. Everything is now staged for exclusion — the rare knower on one side, the deluded and the departing on the other. What the next verses do instead is the poem's pluralist moment.

Read the grammar before the sentiment. Yo yo yāṃ yāṃ — whichever, whichever: the relative pronouns are doubled because no form is excluded in advance. Whatever form any devotee wishes to worship, that very faith — tasya tasya, of that one, of that one — I make firm, unshaken. The īśvara of this chapter does not merely tolerate worship aimed elsewhere. He underwrites it. Faith itself, wherever it points, is steadied from behind by the one it does not name.

Then the small print, and the small print is one clause: the desires such a worshipper gains are arranged by me alone. Both readings are live, and the pair of verses does not settle between them. Read one way, this is radical generosity: every altar works, every god is honored, the divine economy has room for all temples — the translation note calls 7.21 the poem's most pluralism-friendly verse. Read the other way, it is quiet sovereignty: the pluralism is real, but every path's fruits route through a single arranger, and the verse that follows caps the account — the worshippers of the gods go to the gods, while those who worship him come to him (verse 7.23). Steadying every faith, and taking a fee on all of them. Generous, and never innocent.

So the inventory closes: two natures, a maze with one exit, four seekers, one rare arrival, and a lord who holds up even the worship he does not receive. The first hexad ended by ranking the devotee above the adept; the second opens by saying what the devotion lands on. Where the exit leads is the next chapter's question. Chapter 8 asks how one leaves the world at death — and answers that the leaving turns on the last thought held.

Takeaways

  1. God's nature is twofold: an eightfold matter, and a living thread that carries beings.
  2. The strands' maze is crossed by refuge, not analysis.
  3. Four motivations bring seekers — distress, curiosity, wealth, knowledge — and none is turned away.
  4. Worship aimed elsewhere is steadied by the one worshipped: the poem's pluralist moment.
