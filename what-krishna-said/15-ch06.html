Chapter 6 — The Meditation Manual

Chapter 6 is the poem's manual: posture, diet, sleep — and the restless mind that Arjuna says cannot be held.

Chapter 5 ended in friendship. The courtroom had closed — both paths reach the goal, disciplined action the surer road — and Krishna finished his argument with three titles: enjoyer of the sacrifice, lord of the worlds, friend of all beings. Chapter 6 does not reopen the case. It hands the newly befriended reader a practice: where to sit, what to eat, how long to sleep, and what to do about the one thing no seat and no schedule can fix — the mind itself.

Lift yourself

The register changes without warning. Chapters two through five were argument — a claim about the deathless self, a case for action, a courtroom over renunciation. Chapter six is instruction. Its own colophon titles it dhyāna, meditation. The instructions arrive promptly: body, neck and head erect in one line, eyes on the nose's tip, thought gathered onto a single object; a clean spot, a firm seat neither too high nor too low, kuśa grass under skin under cloth (verses 6.11 to 6.14). Between two armies, the poem pauses to tell you how to arrange a room. Then, before the room, it names who is responsible.

Read the grammar before the sentiment. Uddhared, let him lift; ātmanā, by the self; ātmānam, the self. The same noun stands twice in one clause, instrument and object: the self lifts the self. The lifting is done with equipment you already own, by an agent who is also the patient. Then the line: the self alone is the self's friend, the self alone its enemy. No third party appears on the page. The next verse makes the condition explicit — for one who has mastered the self, the self is a friend; for one who has not, it stands as an enemy — but the pairing was already in force.

Notice the rhyme with the chapter you just left. Chapter five closed with a friendship declared from above: Krishna naming himself the friend of all beings. Chapter six opens with a friendship conducted from within, the self's relation to itself, won or lost by conduct. Which is why this verse stands at the door of the manual: nobody sits for you. No one else can still your mind on your behalf, and the poem does not offer to do it for you. The manual begins by naming its only operator.

The anti-extreme rule

Then comes the surprise. You have the seat, the posture, the gaze, the gathered thought. In the ascetic manuals of the ancient world this is where the fasting begins and the vigils start — where the body is billed as the obstacle and the invoice comes due. The Gita refuses the invoice, and it refuses it in both directions.

Four failures, two axes, paired with care. Too much food and no food at all disqualify identically; so do oversleeping and sleeplessness. The verse does not rank them. The glutton and the faster, the sluggard and the sleepless stand outside the same door, shut there by the same sentence. The shape is familiar by now: action cannot be abandoned, renunciation and action both arrive at the goal, and now the body itself may be neither indulged nor punished. The poem distrusts extremes the way a beam distrusts cracks.

The next verse turns refusal into recipe.

Measured in eating, measured in recreation, measured in effort, measured in sleeping and waking — yoga becomes the destroyer of sorrow. The Sanskrit under the gloss does quiet work: the word for measured here is yukta, from the same root as yoga, the yoke. To be measured is to be yoked. Moderation is not hygiene bolted on from outside; it is the discipline itself, worn at every meal. The translation note hears Buddhist phrasing here — a middle way between indulgence and mortification — and the echo suits a poem that borrowed the word nirvāṇa from those same rivals in chapter two. What differs is register: not a doctrine but dinner and bedtime, delivered to a soldier in a chariot.

The lamp

What does success look like? The chapter answers with an image, not a feeling or a milestone.

A lamp in a windless place. Notice what the image does with blame. A flame in moving air is not defective; nothing is wrong with it. It flickers because of where it stands. The restless mind receives the same re-description: turbulence as exposure, not damage. And notice what the image does not claim. The flame is not out. The lamp still burns. Steadiness is not blankness, not trance, not the extinguishing the English word meditation sometimes drifts toward — it is fire without flutter, which means the goal is not a different mind but this mind, unexposed.

As ever with this poem's images, the simile shows how the claim could hang together; it does not establish that the claim is true. The garment image of chapter two did not prove there is a wearer, and the windless lamp does not prove the wind can be removed. Whether any mind reaches a windless place — and where one might be found between two armies — is exactly what the next speaker doubts, in the strongest terms he has.

The realist's objection

Then Arjuna breaks the manual's calm, and it is the most useful interruption in the poem.

He is not theorizing. This is testimony from the one man on the field with data: in chapter one he reported his own mind whirling, bhramatīva ca me manaḥ, as the Gandiva slipped from his hand. Having listened to a chapter that promises a lamp-still mind to whoever eats and sleeps in measure, he files his doubt about the merchandise. His dossier runs four words — cañcala, restless; pramāthin, turbulent; balavat, strong; dṛḍham, obstinate. And the comparison is chosen with a sufferer's precision: not a storm, which passes, but wind itself, everywhere, and you cannot close your hand on it. You cannot even see it, only what it moves, which is also a fair description of attention.

Note the honesty of the verb: to curb it seems to me, he says, as hard as curbing the wind. An estimate flagged as an estimate. And note what the objection is not. It does not quarrel with the goal; Arjuna wanted the settled mind the moment he heard it described. He doubts the manual's premise — that this mind can be held at all. It is the question every reader of an instruction book silently asks, and the poem has the decency to put it in the mouth of its hero.

The answer is method

Krishna's reply concedes the premise and keeps the practice.

No doubt, he begins. The mind is restless and hard to curb; the estimate stands unrevised. What changes is the category of the answer. The question was whether the thing is possible; the reply is a how. Two words carry it. Abhyāsa, practice: repetition — the chapter has described it as retrieval by degrees, the straying mind restrained and brought back under the self's control (verses 6.25 to 6.26). Vairāgya, non-attachment: the cooling of the wanting that gives the wandering somewhere to go. Practice builds the groove; non-attachment removes the fuel. A mind drilled by repetition but still craving returns to its object as often as it is recalled — willpower against appetite is a wager the appetite usually wins — and a mind emptied of wanting but never drilled has nowhere steady to land. Neither is a mood. You cannot feel your way into either on a given morning; you can only have done them, or not.

Arjuna asks the manual's last honest question: what becomes of the one who believes and still falls short — is he lost, like a cloud torn loose? The answer (verses 6.37 to 6.45) refuses the fear. No destruction waits in either world. The fallen practitioner is reborn into conditions where the work resumes, regaining something of the ground already gained and striving on from there. Failure, in this accounting, is a delay — which is the only honest thing a manual can say to a beginner.

Then the ranking, in two steps. First the yogi is declared greater than the ascetic, greater than the learned, greater than the performers of ritual — therefore, be a yogi (verse 6.46). The manual's practitioner is crowned above every rival specialist in the poem's world. And one verse later, the crown moves.

Of all yogis — after a chapter of seat, diet, sleep, and the retrieval of a straying mind — the highest is the one who worships with faith. The superlative is yuktatama, most yoked, built on yoga's own root: the chapter's final word plays on its own name. Read the move carefully, because it is quiet. The technique is not outbid by better technique. The meditator is ranked below someone who trusts. The translation note calls the verse a hinge toward the devotion chapters, and this book will let the hinge stay quiet for now: the devotional turn begins here, understated, at the bottom of a manual, with love ranked above mastery. What kind of thing the loved one is, the poem's next block exists to say.

And with that, a stretch of the poem closes. Chapters one through six — the collapse, then the response — have walked you from a man seated between two armies into the workings of his own attention. The three-hexad map from the front of this book names what comes next: the second block, the divine, six chapters in which the charioteer stops coaching and starts explaining himself. The one the yogi worships is about to speak in his own voice.

Takeaways

  1. The self is its own friend and its own enemy — practice is self-directed.
  2. The manual is anti-extreme: yoga fails on too much food or sleep, or too little.
  3. Arjuna's realism about the restless mind is answered by method: repetition plus dispassion.
  4. The chapter's last verse quietly ranks the devotee above the adept — the devotional turn begins.
