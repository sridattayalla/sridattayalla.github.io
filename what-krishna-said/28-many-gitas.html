How One Poem Became Many Gitas

There is no single Gita. There is the poem, and there are the centuries of readers who built whole systems on it; this part of the book is the toolkit for hearing them.

Arjuna said yes, the armies moved, and the poem froze, eighteen chapters from a man sitting down in a chariot to a man standing up to fight. The freezing was the beginning of something else. The moment the text closed, its readers began, and what you walked through once they have been walking for more than a thousand years.

A text plus its readers

Start with what the poem became. The Gita is one of the most-commented texts in world literature, and its stream of commentary has never run dry: a reader picks the poem up, finds a system in it, and writes the system down verse by verse. A single modern edition, printed in 1935, gathers eleven full Sanskrit commentaries into seven volumes, and it is a sampler, not a census. The earliest complete commentary that survives in wide circulation is the work of a southern wanderer named Shankara, traditionally dated 788 to 820. After him the stream does not thin for a thousand years. Commentators are still arriving in the eighteenth century: monks, temple theologians, a Kashmiri Shaiva, a Marathi poet-saint who died young.

Why did one battlefield conversation attract this crowd? Partly because of where the poem sits. The great schools that grew from the end of the Veda treat the Gita as one of three starting points, standing beside the Upaniṣads and the Brahma Sutras, and a school that wanted a seat at that table had to plant its commentary on all three. The Gita collected readers the way a capital collects embassies, partly out of love and partly as the price of membership. A school without a Gita commentary could not fully appear in public.

And the commentaries do not agree. That is the fact this part of the book stands on. Read far enough into the tradition and you stop meeting one Gita and start meeting many: the Gita of readers who say brahman and the self are one, the Gita of readers who say they are eternally two, the Gita whose climax is the last verse's call to refuge, and the Gita that reads the same call as knowledge arriving in devotional dress. Same text, different poems. Before you can hear those readers argue, you need three small tools in your hand: a word for what a school is, a word for what a commentary is, and a word for the chain that carries both.

Views

The first tool is the tradition's own word for what its schools are. That word is darśana: a view, a seeing, a sightline. The classical schools of India call themselves darśanas, and the word carries less furniture than the English philosophy. It does not name a doctrine you subscribe to or a system you defend. It names the place you have come to stand and what can be seen from there. A view implies a viewer, a stance, a direction, and above all an object: every darśana is a sightline onto one shared thing.

Hold that image, because it does quiet work on every page that follows. Two schools can stand in different places, look at the same poem, and see genuinely different things, without either lying about what it sees. What one sightline shows full-face, another catches edge-on, and a third has its back turned entirely. The poem you walked invites the division. It teaches action and it teaches saṃnyāsa; it says the self is untouched by weapons and then that a Lord dwells in the hearts of all beings; it climbs to a vision that levels the field and then walks back down to work. The commentary tradition exists because the poem hands its readers a question it never closes for them.

Watch the split happen on a single verse. The last teaching, the one the tradition calls the carama śloka, tells Arjuna to abandon all dharmas and take refuge in the speaker alone. The classical sightlines part three ways exactly there. One reads refuge as knowledge, the moment the self recognizes what it always was. One reads it as surrender, the soul's irrevocable gift of itself to a Lord it will never become. One reads it as devotion to a sovereign who was never anything but sovereign. Three views, one verse, and no commentary in the tradition concedes that the others are looking at nothing.

The running commentary

The second tool is the form those readings arrive in. A bhāṣya is a commentary in a strict, rule-bound genre, closer to jurisprudence than to criticism. Its first rule is coverage. The root text is walked verse by verse, first to last, and every verse must be accounted for: you can't skip the verse that embarrasses your system, drop the one that contradicts it, or stop where the poetry levels off. The poem sets the agenda. The commentary follows it the way a footpath follows a ridge.

The second rule is reconciliation. A bhāṣya treats the text as authoritative, the way a legal tradition treats a code: where two verses collide, the collision is a problem for the commentator, never for the text. And the poem leaves collisions lying around on purpose. You watched chapter 3 and chapter 5 argue over whether the life of action outranks saṃnyāsa, and you watched the poem decline to referee. A modern essayist picks the verses that serve the argument and lets the rest go dark. The commentator doesn't have that freedom, and the discipline is the point: standing at a sightline, you must say what every verse means, including the ones you would rather lose.

A third practice explains why the commentaries disagree so cleanly. Every school reads the poem as a slope with a summit: certain verses state the summit, and the rest are approaches, qualifications, scaffolding for slower climbers. Which verse is the summit is not given by the text. It is elected, defended, and then enforced backward and forward across all eighteen chapters. One tradition crowns a verse of knowledge and reads work as its staircase. Another crowns the carama śloka and reads the whole poem as the walk toward it. The election of the summit is the lens itself; nearly everything else in a commentary is the working out of that one choice.

The lineage

The third tool explains who the commentators thought they were. None of them presented himself as an original mind signing a book. Each stood at the end of a chain of teachers and students, and the chain has a name: paramparā, the lineage, the succession. You have met the structure inside the poem already. In chapter 4, Krishna traces his teaching down a chain of transmitters reaching back to the sun, and reports that the chain wore thin and the teaching was lost. A broken lineage is what the poem's crisis sounds like from inside the tradition: the teaching exists, but nobody stands at the living end of it.

A paramparā transmits more than claims. It transmits a practice: how the text is held, when it is recited, what it may do to a life, who can expound it and to whom. The commentary you read is the visible piece; behind it stands the remembered chain of teachers who carried it forward, each accountable to the one behind. That is why the tradition treats reading as something you do rather than something you own. Knowledge that arrives through a chain arrives with obligations. You received it at someone's feet, and someone will one day receive it at yours.

You will meet the structure again in the pages ahead, and not only among monks and temple scholars. The modern readers have chains too, shorter and messier: an activist inherits his reading from a saint, a jurist inherits his indictment from a movement, a scholar inherits a method from a seminar room. Even the claim to have no chain, to read the poem with fresh eyes and no agenda, has a history. When a reader tells you they are giving you the plain meaning of the text, ask who taught them to call it plain.

The rules of this part

So, the rules of the road. Part 3 gives each lens a page, one reader per page, nine pages in all. The pages share a shape: who the reader is and where they stood; what they did with the text; one verse held up and read through their sightline; what the lens costs, or who rejects it and on what grounds. Each page ends by handing the floor to the next lens. No page ranks its reader against the others, and the book stays silent from here to the end of the part. You will be shown Shankara's Gita, Ramanuja's, Madhva's, Gandhi's, Ambedkar's, and the Gitas of their heirs and critics, and you will not be told which one to keep.

One rule sits above the rest. No lens in the coming chapters is neutral, including the ones that claim to be. Every reader ahead is standing somewhere: a monastery, a temple, a seminar room, a critic's desk. Each will show you a real poem, and each will leave something out, because that is what a sightline is. The classical schools, having named themselves views, were honest about this. The moderns are sometimes shyer, and the shyest page of all is the one near the end that will offer you the poem with no lens at all, nothing but the text and the facts. Read it as carefully as you read the devotional ones. The claim to see without standing anywhere is itself a way of standing.

The first lens is the oldest and the most famous, and it belongs to a wanderer from the south, a monk who crossed the subcontinent picking debates and found in the poem you have now walked one reality wearing the appearance of a world. Where you watched armies, he watched conjuring. He takes the floor on the next page.

Takeaways

  1. Darśana means view: each classical school is a sightline on one shared text.
  2. A commentary is a discipline — every verse accounted for, contradictions reconciled.
  3. A lineage transmits reading as practice, not information.
  4. No lens in the coming chapters is neutral, including the ones that claim to be.
