The Forks I: Four Verses, Many Gitas

Four verses carry the weight of every reading you have met. Here they are again, unchanged, with the readers who fight over them gathered in one room.

The scholars cut the layers apart, and the layers explain a great deal: where the seams sit, which hands added what, why one chapter can argue against another. What the scalpel cannot do is walk the forks for you. The reader still has to do that, and this page sets the four steepest forks side by side, every reader you have met still holding a corner.

The act and its fruits

Start with the verse everyone can quote. You met it on the chapter 2 page as the hinge of karma-yoga, and every lens since has had to file through it. Hear it a last time, then put three readers at one table, pointing at the same words.

Shankara takes it as a rung. Works purify the mind; knowledge frees it; the discipline of renouncing fruits exists to steady a mind not yet ready to see what never acted at all. On this reading the verse is scaffolding, honest about being scaffolding: the claim on results is released because results belong to the appearance the teaching means to see through. The rung is real, load-bearing, and meant to be stepped off.

Tilak takes it as an engine, and he read it where engines get built, a prison cell in Mandalay, drafting the Gītā Rahasya as an activist's manifesto. His Gita preaches action, not renunciation; the fruit is renounced so the work can run at full strength. For a colonial subject serving a sentence for sedition, the politics inside the verse was the whole point: a person with no claim on the result cannot be bought, worn down, or frightened out of acting.

Gandhi takes it as the engine of nonviolence. His reading, Anāsakti Yoga, the discipline of non-attachment, renounces the fruit but keeps the fight, because the battle is the duel that perpetually goes on in the hearts of mankind. Release the claim on results and you release with it the anger and fear that make violence look necessary; a resister with no stake in victory can absorb blows without hating the man swinging them. This is the reading that carried the verse beyond its home language and made it a slogan of selfless public service.

Three readers, three vocabularies, one identical cut: the hand that acts stops reaching for the harvest. What differs is what moves into the space the claim vacates, knowledge for Shankara, work for Tilak, nonviolence for Gandhi. A fourth reader hands the fruits to Krishna instead of dropping them, work as devotional service: Bhaktivedanta's line, now among the most printed readings of the verse on earth. The verse itself releases the claim. It does not say what should fill the emptied space, and that silence is the fork.

The decree and the criteria

No other line in the poem forks harder. You walked it on the chapter 4 page and heard it argued again at Ambedkar's trial; here it stands a third time, with its claimants in the room.

Make the second quarter the operative clause and the verse becomes a sieve. That is the classical criteria reading: the orders sorted by strand and action, what a person is made of and does, never the parent they were born to. Shankara holds the sorting useful for its work and, at the summit, not finally real at all; Ramanuja reads a real order sorted vocationally, work matched to the worker; the devotional line reads the same two words as a door slammed on birth-right, the make and work of a soul fixing its station, never its parentage. One phrase, three schools, all pointing the same way: criteria, not chains.

Make the first quarter the operative clause and stop there, and the verse becomes a decree: the four-varna order, made by me. A hierarchy with a maker. The clause that trails it, know me as the non-maker, is the strangest move in the verse: the god who claims the order also disclaims it, and readers have never settled whether that softens the decree or deepens it, whether an author backing away from his own text licenses you to do the same.

Then the indictment, which you have already heard at full strength: Ambedkar charging the verse as divine sanction of graded rank, the sorting phrase failing its own arithmetic, three strands asked to shelve four orders, and the corroborating verses, do not unsettle the ignorant, duties fixed by each one's own nature, read as the tell. He did not argue the tension away; he read the tension as the point.

So say the thing this page exists to say. The strain between this verse and chapter 9's open door is not a strain between readers; it is inside the text. The same poem decrees a fourfold order and, five chapters on, opens its highest way to the very names that order ranks lowest. The historians hold a fourth answer, that the decree is a later hand inserted into an older poem, which relocates the strain into the poem's compositional history without dissolving it. However you date the layers, both verses sit in one received text and pull opposite directions, and every reader you have met has had to decide what to do with both.

The open door and its hinges

The door verse hangs on two hinges, and both are in the Sanskrit. One is the particle api, even, doubled: even those born of ill wombs, even they. The other is the participle vyapāśritya, having taken refuge. What the door is, who it opens for, and on what terms: all of it is argued through those two words.

The inclusion reading builds on the even. The devotional line hears the verse flinging the door wide: birth bars no one from the highest way, the promise is addressed to the excluded precisely as excluded, the lowest names on the list go the highest road. Bhaktivedanta's Gita, the best-known English rendering of the twentieth century by distribution, stands on this even; his school built its house there. On this reading the verse is the poem's conscience, the place where the ranking elsewhere in the text is answered and outweighed.

The concession reading hears the same word pointing the other way. The verse names its mercy list, women, vaishyas, shudras, and calls the list ill wombs: a settled category, named as such, then admitted by exception. A door that opens by exception confirms the architecture that needed the exception; who appears on the list tells you who stood outside the wall. Ambedkar's indictment prices the even against the rule rather than for it: the comfort of being let in, extended by the same voice that fixed the outside.

A classical third stands between them. Madhva's school reads the verse with neither lift nor insult: souls differ eternally, the door is genuinely open, and the way itself is graded to the soul that walks it. The figure's note compresses the record into one line: read both ways across two millennia. Both readings stand, and the hinges hold them: even decides who the verse is about, refuge decides what it asks.

The refuge

The final fork is the poem's final teaching, spoken in the stretch before the frame closes over the field. The tradition calls it the carama śloka, the final verse, and the fighting over it has never stopped.

Ramanuja hears surrender's charter. For his school this verse is the Gita's destination, the place the whole poem was walking toward: prapatti, self-surrender, śaraṇāgati, the handing over of the means themselves. Letting go all duties means the disciplines have done their work and grace carries the rest; works continue afterward as the expression of a surrender already made, not as its price. The teaching of the whole poem, on this reading, is the long approach to one instruction.

Shankara hears knowledge. Refuge is the knowledge of identity, the self released into what it always was; letting go all duties reads as releasing every partial standpoint, every prop and practice, for the single seeing behind them. And do not grieve is not a promise made to a servant; it is the sound of a freedom already true, stated in the register of comfort.

Gandhi hears fearless action. What the verse abandons is the ego's claim on outcomes, not the works; duties let go are duties unclutched, and do not grieve becomes permission to act without the tremor the poem opened with, back in chapter 1. And the reader whose edition carried the verse furthest hears exclusive devotion in it, surrender to Krishna as the Gita's whole teaching compressed into a single command.

Four verses, four rooms of arguing readers, and the same shape in every room: the words agreed on, the door disputed. This book has now laid out everything it owns, the chapter-by-chapter walk, the load-bearing words, the lenses in sequence, the scholars' layers, and here the contested lines beside their claimants. It has not tipped a scale, and it does not intend to start now. The reader, not the book, holds the scale; that was the method on the first page and it is the method on this one. What remains are the questions the argument could not close: whether a good person can be an instrument of violence, whether beings mounted on a machine are free, and what it costs to see a scholar and a dog the same. The poem opens all three and closes none of them; that walk is next.

Takeaways

  1. The same verse honestly read supports devotion, knowledge, and action — the readers are the variable.
  2. The four-varna verse and the open-door verse pull opposite directions inside one poem — that tension is the Gita's live wire.
  3. The refuge verse means surrender to one school and clarity to another.
  4. The verdict was never this book's to assign.
