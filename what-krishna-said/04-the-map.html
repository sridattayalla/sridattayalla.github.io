The Map: Eighteen Chapters, Three Hexads

Eighteen chapters fall into three blocks of six: a collapse, a cure, a closing. Every chapter ends the same way, with a receipt that names the dialogue itself.

The cast stands on the field: one family split in two, five brothers against a hundred cousins, and between the armies a chariot carrying the finest archer of his age and a driver who has vowed not to fight. You know who is out there and what they carry. What you have not yet seen is the shape of the conversation about to pass between the driver and the archer, and that shape is the last piece of your orientation.

Three hexads

Open any edition and count the contents: eighteen chapters, seven hundred verses in the standard received count. The count is fixed; the grouping is not. A classical way to group those chapters, and the one this book uses as its map, cuts them into three blocks of six, three hexads. The cut has a pedigree. Madhusudana Sarasvati, a sixteenth-century commentator working in Benares, laid the scheme out in his Gudhārtha-dīpikā and named the hexads for action, devotion, and knowledge; and the scheme was already old when he wrote it, for Yāmunācārya (916–1041 CE) had mapped the same three centuries earlier in his Gītārtha-saṅgraha. The scheme carries its own caveat: each hexad holds all three threads, so the blocks mark emphasis, not walls.

On this book's plain-English telling, the hexads run like this. Chapters 1–6 answer the collapse: the poem opens with a warrior who cannot lift his bow, and the first six chapters build the response argument by argument, from the deathless self to the act without attachment, until a portrait of the settled practitioner closes the block. Chapters 7–12 put the divine in full view: the speaker stops arguing and starts revealing, name by name and form by form, until the listener asks to see and is shown more than he wanted. Chapters 13–18 settle the self and close the deal: the distinctions grow finer, the field and its knower, the strands that run the field, and a final settlement gathers every thread before the fighting resumes.

This book captions the three rows the response, the divine, and the self, which is one honest set of names; you could as honestly call them the collapse answered, the cure administered, and the deal closed. Hold the grouping loosely. The eighteen-chapter count is the text's own; the three-hexad cut is a reader's map, drawn by named readers, and a map is a tool for finding your way, not a fence. Expect the terrain to disagree with the map now and then; the poem doubles back, and the best readers have always let it.

The receipt at the end of each chapter

After the last verse of the first chapter, the Sanskrit text drops in a line that belongs to neither Krishna nor Sanjaya. It is a colophon, a closing receipt in the habit of scribes: a formula that names the work, claims its lineage, and counts the chapters. Chapter one ends like this: oṃ tatsaditi śrīmadbhagavadgītāsūpaniṣatsu brahmavidyāyāṃ yogaśāstre śrīkṛṣṇārjunasaṃvāde arthaviśādhiyogo nāma prathamo'dhyāyaḥ.

Unpacked, the receipt files the chapter inside a nest of boxes. The outer box is the Bhagavad Gita itself, and the poem carries three titles in apposition: it is a sūpaniṣat, claiming a place among the older teachings; it is a brahmavidyā, a knowledge of what is ultimate; it is a yogaśāstra, a teaching of discipline. The innermost box names the form, a saṃvāda, a dialogue of Krishna and Arjuna. Then comes the chapter's own title, then the count: the first.

Why would a poem interrupt itself with filing information? Because the receipt tells you what kind of thing you are reading, and it tells you at the one moment a listener might forget: the end. The battlefield speech is not a stray fragment of epic dialogue; it is, by its own repeated account, a teaching with a title, a lineage, and a place on a shelf. The colophon is the poem signing its own name.

All eighteen chapters close this way, the same formula with the title and the ordinal swapped, eighteen receipts in all. The chapter titles the receipts supply are compounds built on the Sanskrit word for discipline, every one of them, and that word is one of the four this book will hand you in the coming pages. The effect is a poem that keeps describing itself: a teaching, filed inside a dialogue, filed inside a report, filed inside an epic, with the filing repeated at every seam so you never quite forget the layers. It is also how the text keeps its own arithmetic: when a receipt tells you a chapter is the seventh, it is.

The books under the floor

The colophon's lineage claims hold up. The Gita's vocabulary was not invented on the battlefield; it was assembled from older strata, and two of those strata have names you will meet throughout this book.

A Veda is one of the oldest hymn collections of the tradition: songs to fire and dawn and rain, ritual formulas, verse meant to be recited at a sacrifice, preserved by schools of reciters who memorized the sound and handed it on for centuries. The world of the Vedas is a world of ritual and recitation, precise acts with precise consequences. An upaniṣad is one of the later dialogues of the tradition, quieter and harder to date: teachers and students asking what the self is, what survives the body, whether the ritual images point at something beyond the rite. The Gita quotes both worlds and argues with both. It takes the sacrifice vocabulary and re-aims it at conduct; it takes the upaniṣads' claims about a deathless self and restates them between two armies. When Krishna speaks of a self that does not die, the sentence reaches back through the forest dialogues; you do not need the pedigree to follow the poem, but you will feel it underfoot, and Part 3 of this book will show you readers who built whole readings on that floor.

Under both strata is the floor the poem stands on, the epic itself. A parvan is a book of the Mahabharata, and the epic runs to eighteen of them. The Gita lives in the sixth parvan, the Bhishma Parvan, the Book of Bhishma, named for the grandsire who commands the Kaurava lines on the war's first days. The poem you are reading is a passage inside a book inside an epic, and the colophon is the receipt that keeps its place.

The scale

One more structure belongs on the map, and it is the largest thing on it. The last page gave you the scale in outline; this one gives the arithmetic. An akṣauhiṇī is the epic's unit for counting an army, and the epic counts with unusual precision: one akṣauhiṇī is 21,870 chariots, 21,870 elephants, 65,610 horses, and 109,350 foot soldiers. The ratio is fixed, one to one to three to five, a chariot and an elephant for every three horses and five foot; scale the chariots and the rest follow.

Kurukshetra, the tradition says, drew eighteen of those units onto one field: eleven akṣauhiṇīs for the Kauravas, seven for the Pandavas. The war they were gathered to fight was sized to match, eighteen days of it, and the epic that reports the whole business is eighteen books long. Eighteen is the frame's signature, stamped on every layer, and the tradition noticed the stamp inside the arithmetic itself: add the digits of the chariot count, 2+1+8+7+0, and the sum is eighteen. Read that as numerology rather than census. These numbers are the epic's own bookkeeping, its way of saying an army too large to picture, and the war behind them is unverified by archaeology; scholars treat it as legend layered over possible conflict.

Your map

Here is the whole architecture on one grid. Read it row by row, left to right; the numbers are chapter numbers, the rows are the hexads, and the captions are this book's names for them. The Walk pages of this book, from page nine to page twenty-seven, follow the chapters in exactly this order, the verses in order, the argument of each chapter assembled as you reach it. When you lose your place in the poem, and you will, come back to this page and re-fold the map.

The map is folded, and orientation is finished. You know how the verses will be presented, where the conversation happens, who stands on the field, and how the eighteen chapters lie. What you still need before the walk begins is vocabulary. The poem cannot stop using four words, and the next page takes up the first of them, the load-bearing one, a word from a root that means to hold.

Takeaways

  1. Eighteen chapters in three blocks of six: the response, the divine, the self.
  2. Every chapter ends with the same colophon naming the dialogue itself.
  3. The Gita's vocabulary comes from older strata: the Vedas' ritual world, the Upaniṣads' inquiry into the self.
  4. The Walk pages of this book follow the chapters in order — that grid is your map.
