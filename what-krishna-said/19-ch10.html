Chapter 10 — The Catalogue of Splendor

Chapter 10 is a list poem: the divine catalogs itself in image after image, every entry in the first person, until a single rule throws the net over everything a list cannot reach. Whatever has splendor, anywhere, is a splinter of one abundance.

Chapter 9 ended by opening its royal secret to everyone with ears to hear it. That kind of opening leaves you holding a question: opened to what? Chapter 10 answers with an inventory, and the inventory is inexhaustible. The god who has spent chapters making his case now starts counting himself out loud.

A god who lists

The teaching changes tactics somewhere past the poem's midpoint. The chapters behind you argued. Chapter 7 sorted a god's being into two natures; chapter 8 chased the last thought of a dying man; chapter 9 confided its secret and then threw the secret open. Chapter 10 does none of that. It stops persuading and starts itemizing. By the standard tally, Krishna speaks 574 of the poem's 700 verses and Arjuna 84, so the poem is, in sheer volume, mostly one voice; here that voice turns on its own subject.

The entries all run on one grammar. A class of things is named; one member of the class is picked out; the picked-out member is declared to be the speaker. Of these, I am that. The formula repeats, category after category, without transitions and without apology. An argument has to meet doubt on doubt's own ground, concede what it must, and build toward its conclusion one beam at a time. A list presumes the conclusion and displays range instead. You have met the register outside scripture: a proud host walking you through the treasury with a torch, saying this too, and this, and this. The tour proves nothing about the treasury. You are standing in it.

Before the formula takes over, the chapter sets a frame for receiving it, and the page's first verse carries that frame.

Both of this page's first verses open with the same bare pronoun, ahaṃ: I. Here the I makes a claim of origin. I am the source of all, the verse says, and the Sanskrit word for source is worth holding: prabhava, the place things spring from. From me, it continues, everything moves onward, pravartate, the verb a river or an army uses when it sets out. Then, without pausing for metaphysics, the verse reports what this knowledge does to the people who hold it: the wise, knowing it, worship me, moved by feeling. The verb of their worship, bhajante, grows from the same root as bhakti, the word chapter 9 finally put in your hand. The feeling is in the Sanskrit too: bhāvasamanvitāḥ, the wise accompanied by feeling, devotion with the emotion left in. So the list this chapter recites is not offered as proof of anything. It is offered as material for worship, a stock of things to love the source with.

The formula names the god through things. The page's second verse names where he lives.

The self in every heart

Then the catalogue pauses on an entry of a different intimacy.

The pronoun is the same one that opened the last verse, ahaṃ, but this time it is welded to a heavier noun: ahamātmā, I the self. Sit with the two words, because the poem has been walking toward them since its second chapter. Ātman was the load-bearing claim there: never born, never dying, not killed when the body is killed, the thing Arjuna's grief had mistaken for its object. Chapter 2 announced that self in the register of doctrine, a truth about what you are. Here the announcer claims to be the announcement. The earlier teaching returns wearing a divine face, and the whole return is compressed into two words of Sanskrit.

Where does this self sit? Seated, the verse says, in the hearts of all beings, and the Sanskrit carries the entire phrase as one compound: sarvabhūtāśayasthitaḥ, stationed-in-the-hearts-of-all-beings. Not above the world, not behind it. Inside, at the seat where feeling happens. The verse is addressed to Gudakesha, Arjuna under one of his epic names, and that address gives the sentence a shape you should not miss: the god driving the chariot tells the man standing behind him in it that the self seated in the man's own chest is the driver. Then the verse widens from space into time. I am the beginning and the middle and the end too of beings: every heart, and every heart's full span. The claim does not thin out as it spreads; it takes all of space and all of time in one breath.

Now set the verse down where it is spoken. All beings includes the line Arjuna can see from the chariot: the teachers, the grandsire, the cousins, the men on both sides. The self that weapons cannot cut is seated, by this verse's claim, in every man he was prepared to shoot. Chapter 10 does not stop to work out what that does to the grief that opened the poem, because chapter 10 is not arguing. It sets the identification down like one more entry and moves to the next item. You are left holding what chapter 2 kept apart: the deathless self and the divine speaker, fused, with the battlefield still waiting outside the sentence.

The rule of splinters

A catalogue left as a catalogue is abundance on display. The page's last verse converts the display into a rule, and the rule travels farther than the list does.

Read the verse's shape before its content, because the shape is the rule. The Sanskrit opens on a doubled whatever, yad yad, and answers with a doubled that, tat tat: whatever, whatever has splendor... that very thing, that very thing, know. Between the halves sits a single imperative, avagaccha, know. The verse is not describing the world. It is training an eye. And the property the eye is trained on is named in the opening compound: vibhūtimat, having-splendor. Whatever being has glory, or beauty, or strength, runs the gloss. Three properties, one net, and the net is thrown over everything that owns any of the three.

The verse's last compound says where the property comes from: tejo'ṃśasambhavam, born of a share of tejas. Tejas is the brilliance of fire, radiance as a physical fact; aṃśa is a share, a portion, a splinter's worth. Unpacked, the claim runs: whatever glory you meet anywhere came off one abundance and glows with borrowed fire. Notice the exactness, because the verse is exact. It does not say the glorious thing is the god. It says the thing is born of a spark of the god's splendor: not identity, but derivation. The world's excellence is real, and it is evidence.

That is a rule of reading, and it reaches everywhere the catalogue did not. The list names specimens; the rule covers whatever bears the property, named or not, catalogued or not, anywhere. It also reaches the man hearing it. Strength is one of the rule's three properties, and the hearer is a warrior whose strength is moments from use. About the translation note above, one sentence only: it points ahead to chapter 11 and, beyond it, to Part 3, where this book returns to radiance's modern afterlife. The rule, not the afterlife, is the business here.

Why a catalog

Why would a god list? The poem has other registers on its shelf. It has argued, in chapter 2, that the self cannot die. It has offered incentives, the gate of heaven held open for warriors in that same chapter. It has given a manual, chapter 6's meditation instruction. It has confided a secret, chapter 9's royal one. Chapter 10 chooses inventory, and the choice is itself the teaching. Abundance like this cannot be argued into view; it can only be shown, item after item, until showing becomes a habit of the eye. A catalogue is persuasion by range. Every entry says the same thing: here too. Repetition is not padding in this register. Repetition is the only form inexhaustibility can take in speech.

There is a structural reason for the shift, and your map carries it. The poem's eighteen chapters divide into three hexads, and the sixteenth-century reader Madhusudana Sarasvati assigned the middle one, chapters 7 to 12, to bhakti. The scheme did not begin with him; the same division stands centuries earlier, in Yāmunācārya's reading of the poem. Read chapter 10 inside that hexad and its purpose sharpens. What chapter 9 taught as a secret, chapter 10 hands over as a stockpile: a god issuing the worshiper item after item to love him with. For a devotion that has run out of arguments, the list is the doctrine.

One thing this page owes you plainly. The full catalogue is long, far longer than the three verses shown here, and this book walks the spine, not every entry. That is a choice of method, not a verdict on the text: the entries run on the same formula, and the load-bearing parts are the frame that opens them, the entry that names where the namer lives, and the rule that generalizes the lot. A reader who wants the whole inventory should go to the poem itself. It will still be there. That is the nature of the thing catalogued.

Arjuna's response to the listing is the poem's next turn, and it is not an argument. He has heard the god describe himself through the world's own materials, and he asks for what stands behind the descriptions. Chapter 11 grants the request, and it is the granting, not the asking, that you should dread. A catalogue of splendor can be put down like a book. The vision cannot.

Takeaways

  1. The chapter's grammar is first-person: the divine lists itself in images, not arguments.
  2. The self in every heart — the earlier teaching returns wearing a divine face.
  3. Whatever has splendor anywhere is a splinter of one abundance.
