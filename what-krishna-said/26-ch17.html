Chapter 17 — Faith, Food, Speech

Gray returns: after the two estates, chapter 17 grades faith in three tints, and dinner with it. The chapter sorts by strand, not by side — a taxonomy, offered without contempt.

Chapter 16 closed hard: two lists, three gates, and scripture appointed umpire for what is to be done and what is not. The rule raises a question, and Arjuna asks it in the new chapter's first breath — what of those who sacrifice with faith but cast off the rule of scripture, which estate holds them? Chapter 17 supplies the gradients the two estates lacked: not a middle ground, exactly, but three tints running all the way between the poles.

You become what you trust

Arjuna's question borrows its opening phrase from the umpire verse itself, those who cast off the rule of scripture, and adds one clause: they still sacrifice, and they bring faith to it. Krishna's reply does not assign these worshipers to an estate. It states a law instead, and everything else in the chapter stands on it.

Read the verse once for each of its three clauses. The faith of each follows its strand: whatever you hold to — a god, a creed, a habit, a person — carries the tint of sattva, rajas, or tamas, the strands chapter 14 wove into appetite and action. This person is made of faith: the chapter's word is śraddhā, trust, and it names not an opinion you hold but the material you are built from. Then the closing clause, flat as a ledger line: whatever one's faith, that is what one is. The arrow points one way. You do not believe what you are; you become what you trust.

Notice what this does to the poem's geometry. Chapter 16 asked which estate you were born to and drew a line with no crossing; 17.3 grades the very substance the estates were made of, and it does not rank. The sorting that follows is a naturalist's field guide, not a judge's bench. The chapter does keep one hard edge — austerity that tortures the body for display, set against the rule of scripture, is handed back to the demonic estate — and everywhere else it watches without sneering.

So the question gets a quieter answer than it asked for. Arjuna wanted a verdict, divine or demonic, saved or lost. Krishna gives a physics instead: faith is a substance with three densities, and the work of the chapter is to show what each density does — in worship, in food, in discipline, in giving, in speech. The list of domains is itself the answer. No act is exempt from the sorting, and none is too small for it.

The taxonomy

The sorting starts at the altar. The lucid worship the gods; the passionate worship spirits; the inert worship the dead. Then the chapter turns to what can be watched more easily, announcing that food, austerity, and giving each come in three kinds. Food goes first, because a plate is public. You cannot see another person's faith, but you can see what they reach for.

The sattvic list reads like a nurse's chart, not a rulebook: foods that grow life, strength, health, gladness, cheer. Nothing here is holy or forbidden; the verse describes what nourishes, and who reaches for it. The translation note records this line's afterlife — generations of vegetarian sattvic-diet claims grew from these two lines — but the chapter makes no law of diet. It reads appetite. What a person loves on a plate tells you which strand is doing the loving.

Listen for the strand inside the Sanskrit itself. Each food verse carries a compound naming who the meal is dear to — sāttvikapriyāḥ, rājasasyeṣṭā, tāmasapriyam — dear to the lucid, to the passionate, to the inert. The taxonomy is welded into the grammar: the strand lives inside the adjective that describes the appetite.

The rajasic plate runs hot — bitter, sour, salty, scorching, dry — and the verse does not stop at the menu. It names the harvest: pain, grief, sickness. The mechanism is unglamorous but fair. No external judge, no cosmic sentence: the passionate want intensity, they get intensity, and they pay in the currency the verse lists.

The tamasic list is the unloveliest and the most ordinary: stale, tasteless, putrid, left overnight, leavings, unfit for offering. Not wicked food — neglected food. The inert strand shows up as indifference to what goes in. And the taxonomy keeps running past the kitchen. Giving sorts three ways — with zeal, for return, with contempt. Austerity splits into body, speech, and mind, each with its own run of verses. The chapter is a sorting machine, the same three tints applied domain by domain, and nobody in it is condemned to a column. You read the table, and you find yourself already standing in one.

The speech rule

Of the three austerities, the one aimed at speech is a single sentence, and it is the poem's most portable ethics.

Four tests, and all of them must clear: the words give no offense, they are true, they are pleasing, they are beneficial. Truth is necessary and not sufficient. A true sentence that wounds fails. A kind sentence that lies fails. A pleasant truth that helps no one fails too. The verse sets the bar at true and agreeable and useful at once, which is harder than honesty alone. The rule survives translation because it names tests, not topics; any sentence, anywhere, can be checked against it. The second half widens the frame — the practice of reciting scripture counts as speech-austerity — so the discipline covers not only what leaves your mouth but what you keep feeding it.

Both halves of this rule have been on the field before. Chapter 16's divine list prized truth and non-slander; the demonic estate gave its whole performance in speech, the possessive monologue of mine and mine again. Here the estate's verbal marks compress into a sentence that fits in a pocket. The mind gets its own version two verses later — serenity, gentleness, silence, self-mastery — but the speech rule is the one readers carry out of the chapter, because it can be tested in the next conversation you have.

The syllable that seals

After all the sorting, the chapter ends by marking the territory. Three syllables, held to be the threefold designation of brahman — and by them, the verse says, the brahmins, the Vedas, and the sacrifices were marked of old.

The verses that follow parcel the syllables out, one act at a time. Oṃ is uttered to open the works: sacrifice, gift, austerity. Tat accompanies acts done without aiming at their fruit. Sat names what is and what is good, and the steadiness of the acts is called by it. The effect is a seal rather than a law. An ordinary act — an offering, a donation, a disciplined day — can be stamped with the designation, whatever its tint. Chapter 16's umpire told you to consult the rulebook; the syllable tells you whose name to sign beneath.

You have been reading past this seal the whole book. Every chapter of the poem closes with a formulaic colophon, the line that names the text — a fully Upaniṣadic book, a science of brahman, a scripture of yoga, a dialogue between Krishna and Arjuna — and the formula opens with these three syllables: oṃ tatsad iti. The syllable that seals offerings also seals the poem's own chapter-ends.

Then the chapter closes its ring. The final verse returns to Arjuna's opening question and answers it with one more sorting: whatever is offered, given, or performed without faith is called asat — not false exactly, but nothing, of no account here or beyond. The law said one becomes one's faith; the last verse says what is done without it does not even register as real. The gradients run in both directions.

One chapter remains, and it referees everything: every disputed word the poem has spent — the action and its fruits, the disciplines, the works of the orders, the lord in every heart — returns for one final hearing, and Arjuna ends it. The last verses of the final chapter, the ones everyone quotes, are waiting there.

Takeaways

  1. One becomes one's faith: trust is self-shaping.
  2. Food, worship, austerity, gift, and speech are each sorted into three strands — the chapter is a taxonomy.
  3. The speech rule — true, pleasant, beneficial, non-harming — is the poem's portable ethics.
  4. The threefold syllable seals every act as an offering.
