Chapter 12 — The Beloved Devotee

The shortest chapter of the second hexad is a love poem with a question inside: worship the face, or the formlessness?

The terror closed into a condition — do the work, hold me supreme, harbor no enemy — and the seeing itself was priced at devotion alone, never at study, never at effort. What devotion looks like from the inside, the poem has so far shown only in flashes: a royal secret, a catalog of splinters, a face gentled back into the four-armed form so a human eye could keep it. Chapter 12, which closes the second of the poem's three hexads of six chapters, now sets out devotion's furniture: a question, a preference, a concession, and a portrait.

The question answered by preference

The question comes from Arjuna, and it is quieter than anything he has asked since the collapse. He has just been given back his charioteer — the benign four-armed form a man can look at — along with the condition the vision closed on. Now he asks, in the chapter's opening verse, about worship's two kinds. Some devotees worship you, ever-yoked, with faith: fixing their minds on a form, a face, a name. Others worship the unmanifest, the imperishable, what no eye can take in. Which of them, he asks, are better at this? Notice what the question takes for granted before it asks anything. Both parties are devotees. Both are travelling. The difference between them is not truth but address — one love has a where, and the other refuses one.

Read the answer's grammar before its content. Them I hold the highest — the verb is holding, not proving. The Sanskrit sentence ends on a word built from the root for thinking: in my judgment, as I see it. The chapter's ruling is one vote, and the vote belongs to the god who was asked. What the verse does not say is equally loud. It never calls the other travelers lost, never ranks the formless as a lesser truth, never shuts the door it passes over. It states a preference. And the word the translation renders as highest is, in the Sanskrit, a superlative of yoked — the most-yoked, the most joined-up — so the ranking is spoken inside the discipline's own vocabulary, as if the question were never about two roads, only about two weathers of travel.

Readers divide along the very line the verse draws, and the division is old. Shankara, whose last word is the unmanifest brahman, hears accommodation: for embodied beings the personal is the workable door, and the ranking is mercy toward what a body can do, not a verdict on what is finally real. Ramanuja, whose god is irreducibly a person, takes the verse at face value: the preference names the higher thing, and the face is the fact, not the concession. The poem hands both readers their sentence and moves on, because it is about to ground its preference where neither camp expects — not in glory but in strain. The personal is about to be called the better and, in the same breath, the easier.

The concession

A chapter with less honesty would stop at the preference. This one does what scripture rarely does: it prices the road it did not rank first, and states the price without flinching.

The cost is named twice over: struggle, and pain. Greater struggle for those attached to the unmanifest — not false, not barren, not second-class: harder. And a way the verse calls pain: the going itself hurts, before any arrival is reached. The reason is given in the body's currency. The way of the unmanifest is hard for the embodied to attain, and that clause reaches you, reading this in a body. The mind that must sustain the love runs on objects; the senses were trained by faces; attention reaches for a where. The unmanifest offers no landing place — no image, no name, no chariot to look at across a field — so the love must hold its object by thought alone, and thought, housed in flesh, keeps sliding back to what it can picture. Chapter 11 made the same point from the far side: even the benign form needed borrowed eyes. Here the limit runs to its end — no loan can help, because there is nothing to look at. Even the travelers carry a telling epithet: those attached to the unmanifest. Attachment, the habit the disciplines elsewhere train down, is on this road the only engine left.

What the concession costs, the chapter declines to resolve, and this book will not resolve it for the poem. Shankara's heirs hear the difficulty confirmed: the formless is the summit, and the strain — the verse's word runs closer to agony — is what a summit costs; they accept it as the discipline's true weight. The bhakti traditions hear a blessing on the easier door: the god, in kindness, pointing embodied lovers toward the love they can actually keep. Between those readings the verse sits with its price tag showing, and you, holding a body, read it yourself. One thing the concession does settle is the ground of the preference stated above. The face was ranked first not because it is truer but because it is livable. Better and easier arrive in a single breath, and best, in this chapter, carries both meanings at once.

The portrait

Then the chapter stops arguing and starts describing. The movement that fills its remainder is a portrait of the beloved, and the first surprise is who sits for it: not Krishna — the devotee. Asked which worship is better, the god answers at length by listing the qualities of the person he loves, with a refrain beating under the list: he is dear to me, he is dear to me. The Sanskrit's masculine is the grammar's default, not a restriction; the portrait is offered to whoever is listening. Theology, asked a question about God, answers with a description of how a person behaves.

Read the direction the list faces. It begins where social life begins, toward beings: free of ill-will toward all beings, friendly, compassionate. Then it digs to the roots of harm — without possessiveness, without ego: the mine and the I, the two habits that turn other people into furniture. Then evenness: level in pain and pleasure, patient. Nothing in the opening strokes concerns worship, ritual, or feeling toward the god. The first mark of the beloved is how he treats you.

Only here is devotion named outright, and notice where it sits: one item in a list of conduct. Content, self-possessed, firm of resolve, with mind and understanding offered to me — the god does not say he loves me constantly; he says the man's attention is placed in him, and then returns to behavior. My devotee, dear to me: devotion appears as an act of placement, surrounded on every side by traits a neighbor could verify without being told what the man believes.

Then the portrait's most quietly social stroke: he from whom the world is not disturbed, and who is not disturbed by the world — peace running in both directions, a person safe to have around. The four freedoms that follow — delight, envy, fear, dread — are the emotional weather of a battlefield, listed by a poem standing on one. Now lay this beside chapter 2's portrait of the settled one, the sthitaprajña, drawn the morning of the same conversation: the levelness in pain and pleasure is the same compound, word for word; the freedom from elation and fear is the same freedom. The person established in knowing and the person dear to the god are, to the eye, one person. The disciplines fork — knowledge, action, devotion — and the portrait they converge on does not.

The closing strokes are mostly absences. Asking nothing, pure, capable, indifferent, untroubled, letting go of every undertaking — no ill-will, no mine, no I, no expectations, no trembling, no projects clutched to the chest. The beloved is defined at least as much by what he has stopped doing to the world as by anything he does. And nowhere in the movement does a command appear: no order, no obligation, no punished alternative. Ethics arrives disguised as flattery — this is who I love — and the description doubles as a door. A reader who cannot yet feel devotion is handed a behavioral bridge: become what is described, and see which comes first, the love or the likeness.

One echo ties the hexad shut. The vision ended on its condition — bearing no enmity toward any being — and the portrait's first stroke is that same clause grown into character: free of ill-will toward all beings. The exit from the terror and the entrance of the beloved are the same door. The second hexad ends here, devotion's furniture set: a face to fix the mind on, a cost posted honestly, and a person-list standing where you expected a creed. The third hexad opens with the poem's most patient distinction — the field, and the one who knows it.

Takeaways

  1. The personal is called the better and the easier — one reading of "best".
  2. The formless path is conceded to be agony for embodied beings.
  3. The beloved is described by how he treats you — theology as a person-list.
