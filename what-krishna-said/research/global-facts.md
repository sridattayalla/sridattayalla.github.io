## Translation cascade (firsts)
- **1785 English** — Charles Wilkins, London (Nourse), with Warren Hastings's preface; pundit
  assistant **Kasinatha Bhattacharya**. First direct Sanskrit→European-language Gita.
  (Detail in FS-MOD; cite FS-MOD for Wilkins's specifics or FS-GLOBAL — keep one consistent
  choice: use FS-GLOBAL on the global-gita page, FS-MOD elsewhere.)
- **1787 French** — translated from Wilkins.
- **1802 German** — from Wilkins's English; this is the channel to Herder, the Schlegel circle,
  Hegel (who was critical of its "machine of thought"), and **Schopenhauer** (who revered it,
  keeping a Latin Gita — **August Wilhelm von Schlegel's 1823 Latin** version — on his table,
  calling it the consolation of his life; per Schopenhauer's own writings/letters as reported
  in standard accounts — attribute as "Schopenhauer wrote/kept...").
- **Wilhelm von Humboldt** lectured on the Gita (1825–26, Berlin) calling it "the most
  beautiful, perhaps the only true philosophical song existing in any known tongue" — widely
  quoted via his letters; verify wording at point of use (quote exists in several variants).

## The American transcendentalists
- **Emerson** and **Thoreau** read the Wilkins Gita; Thoreau took a copy to Walden Pond
  (Walden, 1854, mentions reading "the Bhagvat Geeta" in the mornings — primary: *Walden*,
  the "Reading" chapter). The Gita enters American literature as a pond-side companion.

## Oppenheimer (summary here, details in FS-MOD)
- Berkeley Sanskrit (from ~**1933**, teacher **Arthur Ryder**); Trinity **July 16, 1945**;
  the famous "Now I am become Death" from the **1965 NBC interview**, Ryder-flavored English
  of **11.32** (kāla = Time); "brighter than a thousand suns" is 11.12 via Jungk's 1958 book.
- For the global-gita page: Oppenheimer is the Gita's darkest global echo — the moment the
  chariot-field and the test-site fused in one man's memory.

## The Gita in global politics and movements
- **Gandhi's** anāsakti reading traveled with him (FS-MOD for specifics): civil-rights era
  figures read the Gita via Gandhi's interpretation of selfless action. Martin Luther King Jr.
  studied Gandhi deeply (Gandhi's Gita-reading is in the lineage; attribute carefully — MLK
  did not himself foreground the Gita; do NOT claim he did).
- **Theosophical Society** (Blavatsky, Besant) spread Gita interest in the late 19th c.;
  Besant published a translation.
- **Bhaktivedanta's As It Is** (1968/1972, FS-MOD) made the Gita a street-corner presence in
  the West and the best-distributed English Gita.
- **Vietnam-era and counterculture echoes** (Hesse's *Siddhartha* adjacent; the Gita cited in
  1960s-70s America alongside Eastern-texts paperback boom — Bantam's Prabhavananda/Isherwood
  translation 1944/1951 was the beat-era's Gita).

## The Gita in arts and science
- **Phillip Glass's opera "Satyagraha" (1979)** — libretto from the Gita (in Sanskrit), scenes
  from Gandhi's early life: the Gita set to music as the score of nonviolent resistance.
- **Robert Oppenheimer** (above).
- **T. S. Eliot's *Four Quartets*** ends "with the Gita's still point" scholarly claim —
  Eliot studied Sanskrit at Harvard and called the Gita "the greatest philosophical poem";
  the Three Daemons line and the *Dry Savages* opener ("the way of the Gita" reference) —
  verify exact quotes before use; cite Eliot primary where possible, else "as reported".

## India's state and law
- **1950s**: the Gita quoted in Constituent Assembly-era speeches; note that **Ambedkar's
  counter-reading** (FS-MOD) ran in the opposite direction at the same moment. The Gita has
  been proposed as "national scripture" (Rashtriya Sankriti Mahotsav debates 2015+) — contested
  in a secular republic; report as controversy, no verdict.
- **A court case worth citing** (optional, forks-2): the "Is the Gita a religious or
  philosophical text?" debate in Indian textbook politics (2012-2014 Haryana/Karnataka
  controversies — reported in press; cite press reporting as such).
