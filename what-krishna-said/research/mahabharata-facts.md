# FS-MAH — The Mahabharata frame: cast, battlefield, and the number 18

## The frame in one paragraph
The Gita is not a free-standing poem. It is a scene inside the Mahabharata epic: two armies are
drawn up at Kurukshetra; the blind king Dhritarashtra asks his reporter Sanjaya what is happening
on the field; Sanjaya, gifted divine sight, narrates — and within his narration, the warrior Arjuna
asks his charioteer Krishna to drive between the lines, collapses at the prospect of killing his
kin, and receives Krishna's teaching before re-entering the battle. The Gita ends with Arjuna's
consent and Sanjaya's closing verdict; the war itself resumes outside the poem.

## The cast (dramatis personae of the Gita itself)
- **Dhritarashtra** — the blind king, father of the Kaurava side; speaks exactly one verse (1.1).
- **Sanjaya** — his charioteer-reporter, granted divine distant vision by Vyasa; narrates 41 verses
  including the whole frame (1.1 framing and 18.73–78).
- **Arjuna** — Pandava archer, third of the five Pandava brothers; Krishna's friend, student, and
  interrogator across 84 verses.
- **Krishna** — avatar of Vishnu in the epic frame, but in the Gita increasingly the supreme lord
  (esp. ch. 9–11); speaks 574 verses.
- Mentioned but silent: Bhishma (the grandsire, Kaurava commander), Drona (Arjuna's teacher),
  Karna (Arjuna's secret half-brother), Yudhishthira, Bhima, Duryodhana.
Source: the Gita itself (1.1, 1.8–1.25 roll-call); bhagwadgita.info speaker counts; IGNCA intro.

## Kurukshetra
- "Kurukṣetra" = "field of the Kurus" — a plain north of modern Delhi, traditionally near
  Thanesar in Haryana.
- Verse 1.1's wordplay: **dharmakṣetre kurukṣetre** — "on the field of dharma, the field of the
  Kurus". Commentators from Sridhara Swami onward note the double reading.
Source: verse 1.1 (pinned text); Sridhara's Subodhinī gloss as reported in standard commentary
literature.

## The number 18
The Mahabharata is saturated with 18 — the Gita has 18 chapters and sits in an epic whose
architecture also runs on 18:
- **18 parvans** (books) of the Mahabharata.
- The war lasts **18 days**.
- **18 akṣauhiṇī armies** fight: **11 on the Kaurava side, 7 on the Pandava side**.
- The Gita itself: **18 chapters, ~700 verses**, and tradition also groups Krishna's teaching
  around 18 yogas (the chapter titles of the received text are all "...yoga" compounds).
Source for the 18-pattern: A. Bhattacharya / standard epic statistics; the fullest single source
we found is the Indologica vol. 30 article by S. Sarin
(asiainstitutetorino.it/indologica/volumes/vol30/19_Sarin.pdf), which tabulates the 18s.
- **Akṣauhiṇī composition** (one battle-unit), per Adi Parva (Ganguli translation):
  21,870 chariots, 21,870 elephants, 65,610 horses, 109,350 foot soldiers. Each ratio is
  1 : 1 : 3 : 5 per chariot. Note the digit-sum curiosity (2+1+8+7+0 = 18) — reported in the
  Sarin article; flag as tradition's own numerology, not a verified historical census.
Source: Kisari Mohan Ganguli's public-domain Mahabharata translation, Adi Parva, as cited by
Sarin (asiainstitutetorino.it).

## Where the Gita sits relative to the war
- The Gita occupies the moment **just before the first arrow** of the 18-day war.
- After 18.78 the narration returns to the battlefield: Bhishma blows his conch and the fighting
  begins (Bhishma Parvan continues).
Source: Mahabharata Bhishma Parvan sequence (IGNCA intro; Ganguli translation structure).

## Historical kernel? (for the-map page, keep to two sentences)
- The epic's war is unverified by archaeology; scholars treat the Kurukshetra war as legend
  layered over possible late-bronze-age conflict. The Gita's composition is centuries later than
  any putatable war (see FS-ACAD for dating).
