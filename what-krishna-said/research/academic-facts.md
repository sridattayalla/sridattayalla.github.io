## Dating the Gita (the honest range)
- The Gita is a **layered text**: scholarly consensus holds it reached its present form over
  time, roughly **5th–2nd century BCE**, with common pinning at **~400–100 BCE** for the core
  and possibilities of later interpolation (e.g., some scholars treat parts of ch. 13–18, or
  specific doctrinal verses, as later strata).
- Range across citable scholars: **2nd century BCE to 2nd century CE** as the outer bounds of
  mainstream estimates; a minority pushes later.
- Milestones in the argument: the Gita presupposes the early Upaniṣads (esp. Kaṭha — the
  chariot/self imagery; Śvetāśvatara — theistic rudiments) and dialogues with **Buddhist and
  Jain** renunciate movements (the term nirvāṇa at 2.72; the moderation verse 6.16–17 echoing
  Buddhist middle-way phrasing).
Source: academic review article found in research (academia.edu/29054330 — a survey of Gita
dating scholarship); corroborated by standard reference works (Britannica's "Bhagavad Gita"
entry gives 5th–2nd c. BCE; note Britannica is a tertiary source — attribute as such).

## The Mahabharata's own dating (context, not our page's spine)
- The epic as a whole: core perhaps **~400 BCE–400 CE** layered composition; the Gita is one
  of its later-ish philosophical insertions by most accounts (some argue among the later
  strata; this is contested).
- No archaeology confirms a historical Kurukshetra war of the epic's scale. Treat the war as
  narrative, not reportage, throughout the book. Source: standard epics scholarship (summarized
  in the same dating review).

## Text-critical facts
- The **critical edition** of the Mahabharata (Bhandarkar Oriental Research Institute, Pune,
  completed **1973** for the constitutive text; Gita portion in vol. 6 parvan 6) is the base
  for most academic work; it prints the **shortest plausible reconstruction** with an apparatus
  of variants.
- Verse-count variants: received 700; Kumbakonam edition 745 (see FS-TEXT). The critical
  edition's Gita is ~700 with a few notable variants across manuscripts.
Source: BORI critical edition project description (standard); FS-TEXT for counts.

## The Gita and its sources (what it synthesizes)
- **Upaniṣadic** ideas: ātman/brahman (ch. 2's arguments are condensed Kaṭha/Chāndogya lines
  of thought).
- **Sāṅkhya** categories: puruṣa/prakṛti, the guṇas (ch. 13–14 are Sāṅkhya-inflected; note the
  Gita's sāṅkhya ≠ the classical system of Īśvarakṛṣṇa's Sāṅkhyakārikā — later, ~5th c. CE).
- **Yoga** practice: dhyāna, the lamp simile (6.19), sense-withdrawal (ch. 6).
- **Vedic ritual** vocabulary: yajña, recycled from fire-ritual to dedicated action (3.9–15).
- **Theism**: Śvetāśvatara Upaniṣad's proto-bhakti, grown into the lord of ch. 9–11.
- The standard scholarly image: the Gita is a **synthesis** shaped to reconcile renunciate
  idealism with householder duty — a tension crystallized by the Buddhist/Jain challenge.
Source: academic dating review; standard introductions (IGNCA; Britannica, attributed as tertiary).

## The Bhagavad Gita as "theology of comfort" debate
- Scholars have long noted the Gita functions to **legitimate action-in-the-world while
  retaining renunciate ideals** — described variously as its genius (Zaehner, Edgerton,
  Aurobindo-of-the-academy) or as its ideological work (Ambedkar; some postcolonial readings).
- We present both framings on scholars-gita, attributed, no verdict.

## Our translation policy (state on how-to-read page)
- IAST from the pinned Sanskrit source (FS-TEXT), verbatim, sandhi intact.
- Glosses are our own literal renderings cross-checked against **Arnold (1885, public domain)**
  and where possible Sargeant's word-by-word; we do not silently follow any single translator.
- Where major renderings diverge (adhikāra, kāla, guṇa, dharma, yoga), the divergence itself
  is shown on the page (that is the book's whole method).
