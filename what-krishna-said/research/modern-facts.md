# FS-MOD — Modern readings: Tilak, Gandhi, Vivekananda, Aurobindo, Bhaktivedanta, Ambedkar, Oppenheimer, Wilkins

## Charles Wilkins (the global doorway)
- **1785**: first direct Sanskrit→English translation of the Gita, by **Charles Wilkins**,
  printed in **London by Nourse**, with a preface by **Warren Hastings** (East India Company).
- Wilkins worked at Benares assisted by the pundit **Kasinatha Bhattacharya**.
- Prompted the first wave of global fascination: French translation **1787**, German **1802**
  (the channel through which German Romanticism — Herder, the Schlegels, Hegel, Schopenhauer —
  received it).
Source: The Hindu books section retrospective on the first translation
(thehindu.com/books/first-translation-of-the-gita/article20104419.ece).

## Swami Vivekananda (1863–1902)
- Karma-yoga lectures delivered (in the West, 1895–96), collected in **Complete Works, Vol. 1**
  ("Karma Yoga", 1896).
- Key formulation, from "The Secret of Work": *"We may all be perfectly sure of this at least,
  that we are doing our duty, and what is right... 'Work incessantly, but be not attached to it.'*
  He reads 2.47 as the science of **non-attached work**, presenting Krishna as teaching
  strength-giving practical religion rather than world-denial.
Source: Complete Works Vol 1 (ramakrishnavivekananda.info); mkgandhi.org cross-references.

## Bal Gangadhar Tilak (1856–1920)
- **Gītā Rahasya** ("The Secret of the Gita"), written in **Mandalay prison** (drafted from
  **1908**, the year of his six-year sentence for sedition), published **1915** in Marathi.
- English translation by **Bhalchandra Sukthankar**, **1934/1936**.
- Thesis: the Gita's single thread is **karma-yoga — energetic action in the world**. Against the
  renunciate reading, Tilak insists the Gita teaches action as duty with the fruit renounced.
  Famous line of argument compressed as: "The Gita doesn't preach Renunciation. The Gita preaches
  Action!"
- Relies on 18.2's saṃnyāsa/tyāga distinction and 5.2's verdict ("karma-yoga excels
  renunciation-of-action").
- Context: written as an activist's manifesto — a justification that political work, not monkhood,
  is the Gita's true call, aimed at colonial India's freedom movement.
Source: kamat.com/database/articles/gita_rahasya.htm; Sukthankar translation bibliographic data.

## Mohandas K. Gandhi (1869–1948)
- Gandhi's reading: **Anāsakti Yoga** ("The Gospel of Selfless Action"), his Gujarati commentary
  (1926 discourses). Key texts at mkgandhi.org/swmgandhi/chap01.php.
- Core quotes (verified against mkgandhi.org):
  - *"The Gita is not an historical work... it represents the **duel that perpetually went on in
    the hearts of mankind**"* — the battle as allegory of the inner war.
  - The Gita's central teaching is **anāsakti** — non-attachment — the renunciation of the fruit
    of action, not of action itself.
  - On violence: Gandhi acknowledges the Gita's battlefield setting but argues Krishna's advice is
    about doing one's duty without attachment; he reads "fighting" as the soul's war against its
    own passions. He called the Gita his "spiritual reference book" / "mother".
- Influence: Gandhi's reading made 2.47 ("your right is to the action alone") a slogan of
  selfless public service worldwide, far beyond Hindu readers.
Source: mkgandhi.org/swmgandhi/chap01.php and adjacent chapters (primary, Gandhi's own text).

## Sri Aurobindo (1872–1950)
- **Essays on the Gita**: serialized in his journal **Arya** from **August 1916 to July 1920**;
  book editions **1922/1928**. Full text public at gacbe.ac.in (E books / EssaysontheGita.pdf).
- Core claim (from his first essay): the Gita is a **synthesis** — *"not pure Monism although it
  teaches the undivided unity of the existence of all that is... nor Mayavada... nor the
  theism of a personal God... nor Sankhya dualism..."* — he holds that Krishna deliberately
  gathers and reconciles the period's competing spiritualities into an **integral** teaching:
  action, devotion and knowledge together, world-affirming, evolution-directed.
Source: gacbe.ac.in/images/E%20books/EssaysontheGita.pdf (primary text, first essay
"The Divine Teacher").

## Swami Bhaktivedanta (A. C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupāda, 1896–1977)
- **Bhagavad-gītā As It Is**: first published in abridged form by **Macmillan in 1968**
  (~400 pages, 8 chapters + summary); **complete edition 1972**.
- Claims to transmit the reading of the **paramparā (disciplic succession)** from Chaitanya's
  line — "as it is", "without adulteration": the Gita's speaker is the Supreme Personality of
  Godhead, the supreme teaching is **surrender to Krishna** (18.66), and bhakti is the summit;
  karma-yoga and jñāna-yoga are subordinate rungs.
- Became the **best-known English Gita of the 20th century** by distribution (hundreds of
  millions of copies claimed by the publisher; exact sales numbers are publisher-reported, not
  independently audited — treat as claim, not fact).
Source: prabhupadagita.com/preface/ (the preface in his own words, primary).

## B. R. Ambedkar (1891–1956)
- Essay: **"Philosophic Defence of Counter-Revolution: Krishna and His Gita"**, part of
  *Riddles in Hinduism* (drafted 1950s, published posthumously). Full text at ambedkar.org
  (riddleinhinduism, PART II).
- Core claims (verified against ambedkar.org text):
  1. The Gita is a **philosophic defence of counter-revolution** — Krishna is marshaled to
     philosophically uphold the varna order against Buddhist-era egalitarianism.
  2. The **guna theory cannot ground 4 varnas**: there are 3 gunas but 4 varnas; Ambedkar calls
     this out as arithmetic and logical failure.
  3. Cites **3.26** (do not unsettle the minds of the ignorant) and **18.41–48** (duties by
     birth-nature) as the tell.
  4. Dates the Gita **after the rise of Buddhism**, reading it as a Brahmanical response to the
     Buddha's challenge.
  5. On 11.33's "be my instrument": Ambedkar judges that Krishna ends by making Arjuna a mere
     **nimitta** (instrument) in a pre-decided slaughter — in his reading, a moral evasion.
- Ambedkar's verdict matters for our book as the sharpest in-print counter-reading from within
  the modern Indian tradition — the strongest "lens of suspicion" included.
Source: ambedkar.org/riddleinhinduism/21B.Riddles%20in%20Hinduism%20PART%20II.htm (primary).

## J. Robert Oppenheimer (1904–1967)
- Read the Gita in Sanskrit (Berkeley, learned Sanskrit ~1933, taught by **Arthur Ryder** — the
  "Ryder" whose verse renderings Oppenheimer quoted).
- **Trinity test, July 16, 1945**: the explosion recalled to him the Gita; the famous phrase
  he later quoted is from **11.12** ("brighter than a thousand suns") and **11.32**.
- The "Now I am become Death, destroyer of worlds" phrasing comes from Oppenheimer's **1965
  NBC television interview** ("The Decision to Drop the Bomb"), **17 years after** the event —
  not from a 1945 utterance, and scholars (notably **James Hijiya**, "The Gita of J. Robert
  Oppenheimer", PDF at archive.org) note the English is Ryder-inflected.
- Translation note: **kāla = "time"**, not "death" (death = mṛtyu); "Now I am become Death" is
  Ryder's rendering; "Time" is the more literal reading of kālo'smi.
- **"Thousand suns"**: 11.12 — but the exact English phrase "brighter than a thousand suns"
  entered circulation via **Robert Jungk's 1958 book** (Brighter Than a Thousand Suns) — Oppenheimer
  did not say it at Trinity; it is a verse image he associated afterward.
Source: ia802907.us.archive.org/8/items/gitaandoppenheimer/Gita%20and%20Oppenheimer_text.pdf
(Hijiya paper, the standard scholarly account); 1965 NBC interview transcripts as quoted therein.
