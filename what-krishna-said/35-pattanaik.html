Pattanaik's My Gita — and Its Critics

In our own decade a mythologist rearranged the Gita by theme instead of chapter, called it My Gita, and told readers the poem makes meaning rather than gives messages; critics called the result shallow — and both are now part of the Gita.

The last page put the poem in the dock. Ambedkar's indictment came from the courthouse: a charge sheet read out against a text he believed had been built to defend an unjust order. This page changes rooms. The retelling you are about to meet comes from the marketplace, where the Gita is not cross-examined but bought, gifted, underlined and argued over at dinner — same poem, new room. And the man holding it there is not a monk, a jurist or a scholar. He is a mythologist.

The mythologist

Devdutt Pattanaik was born in 1970, trained as a medical doctor, and then did something a medical career does not usually permit: he became India's best-selling writer on mythology, a man whose books on gods and epics stack up at airport counters. His stated method is unusual, and he says it plainly. Where a philologist asks what the words meant in their own century, he retells through the beliefs of people — through what worshippers, grandmothers, village storytellers and festival crowds actually did and do with the stories. The beliefs, not the manuscripts, are his evidence base.

In 2015 he published My Gita, a retelling of the poem you have just walked, and the first thing to notice about it is physical. It is a book of about 250 pages, illustrated by the author himself in the line-drawing style his readers know on sight — Krishna and Arjuna sketched on facing pages, arrows, lotuses, thought-bubbles. The second thing to notice is the arrangement. The Gita's eighteen chapters are gone as a structure. In their place stand eighteen themes, and the number is his organizing conceit, a wink at the poem's own favorite number: eighteen chapters, eighteen days of war, and now eighteen topics, each one gathering verses from wherever in the poem they live. Action here, the self there, divinity further on. The book does not walk the poem. It rearranges the poem's furniture into rooms of its own.

And the title's first word is the whole method. Not The Gita, not Bhagavad Gita. My Gita.

His Gita

What Pattanaik announces with that pronoun is a claim about knowledge itself: that every reading of the poem is somebody's reading, made from where the reader stands. He reaches for the tradition's own word for this — truth as darśana, a view, a sightline — and takes it personally. You met that word in this part's toolkit: a darśana is where you have come to stand and what can be seen from there. Pattanaik's move is to say that there is no standing nowhere. The commentators had their sightlines; the saints had theirs; his book offers his, unabashedly, and invites you to notice that yours will be different again. On this telling, the Gita does not hand down messages. It makes meaning — and the making is done from a field.

His field, in fact. The frame for the whole book is the verse you met in the chapter on field and knower, and here is what it becomes in his hands.

Where the walk read this verse as the body and the self that watches it, Pattanaik widens the field. For him kṣetra is not only your body; it is your social body — the culture around you, the web of beliefs you were born into, the shared field humans plow together. And so the knower of the field is every reader standing inside a worldview, knowing the poem from within it. Knowledge, on this frame, is not a mirror held up to a fixed text. It is a crop — something a field grows, something the knower makes. Read the Gita this way and the poem stops being an instruction from above and becomes a mirror with a handle: you pick it up, and the face in it is partly yours. This is why the book foregrounds folklore the way a commentary foregrounds grammar — temple stories, regional Krishnas, the beliefs of people — and why its Sanskrit is glossed warmly, words introduced like characters at a door rather than terms in an index. Its Krishna is above all the empathetic friend, the divine companion counselling a man through a crisis, less war-room than counselling room. The vocabulary leans modern on purpose: ownership, ecosystem, potential, the institutional against the personal. The battlefield, the eighteen-day war, the blood — bracketed, held at the edge of the frame.

The critics

A book that sells the way Pattanaik's books sell does not go unreviewed, and the sharpest published response came from Nityanand Misra, whose 2017 review in Swarajya — titled My Gita: Hindered by His Truth — is a detailed philological counter-reading, objection by objection. The charge, at full strength, is that the book's warmth is not rigor: that a loving retelling has quietly become a loose one.

Misra's objections are specific. He argues that the book's etymologies are sometimes folk-etymology: its rendering of brahman as expanding-mind, for instance, is not the account the language supports. He objects to exactly the move you watched above — reading kṣetra as the social body — calling it a free rendering with no basis in the text. He notes coinages used loosely where the poem's own terms, paramātman and ātman, carry distinct doctrinal weight, and he objects to redefining the asuras as those who lack empathy — modern moralizing, he argues, laid over a word whose older semantics are entirely different. Behind the itemized objections stands the general one: a thematic retelling picks its verses, and the verses it does not pick go dark. The commentary tradition you met at the start of this part bound itself to coverage — every verse accounted for, including the ones that embarrass the system. The thematic reteller is free of that discipline, and the freedom, on this critique, is the flaw: hard verses softened, categories flattened, the poem's collisions filed off so the themes can sit side by side.

Notice what is not being claimed by anyone in this exchange. The book's admirers do not claim it replaces a commentary; its critics do not claim nobody should read it. And there is a final twist the critics would insist on, and this book records it without settling it: Pattanaik's subjectivity claim — all readings are my readings — is itself a reading, a modern one, with its own history and its own field. The claim to have no authority is not the absence of a lens. It is a lens.

The pattern

Step back and the room this retelling comes from joins a row of rooms you have already visited or passed by. Shankara read the poem from a monastery and found one reality wearing the appearance of a world. Ramanuja and Madhva read it from temple schools and found souls that never merge. Vivekananda read it from a lecture platform and found strength; Tilak wrote his reading in a prison cell and found a manifesto of action; Gandhi read it from an ashram and found a battle within; Ambedkar read it from the side of the oppressed and found a defence of the order that oppressed them; Bhaktivedanta carried it across the world as the one thing that must not be reinterpreted, and his edition became the best-known English Gita of the twentieth century by distribution. Every one of those readers remade the poem in the dialect of their room.

Pattanaik's illustrated, thematic, self-help-inflected Gita is not an anomaly in that row. It is evidence — evidence of what this era's room is. A marketplace, print-run and airport counter; a readership that meets the poem through popular culture and wants it to speak to a life, not a scholastic inheritance; a vocabulary in which ecosystem and human potential are as natural as karma once was. The courthouse had its Gita; the monastery had its; the marketplace has its. And the critique is part of the pattern too: every new Gita in this book's whole parade has drawn its counter-reading, from rival commentators to Ambedkar's charge sheet to Misra's review. The poem's history is not readings accumulating gently. It is readings and rebuttals, pair after pair, century after century.

That is where this part of the book is heading. Two pages remain in it — the poem's journeys abroad, and the scholars who date and dissect it — and then the book turns a corner it has been building toward all along: Part 4, where the lenses are laid down, four forked verses are set on the table, and the making of meaning is handed, at last, to you. Every era's Gita is somebody's Gita. The present's is no exception, and neither, the book will now ask, is yours.

Before that, one more room: the poem has been leaving India for over two centuries, carried in translations and in luggage. See what it wore abroad.

Takeaways

  1. The retelling reads by theme, not chapter — an arrangement that says arrangement itself is interpretation.
  2. His field-knower verse: knowledge is subjective making — the poem as mirror.
  3. The critics' charge: warmth is not rigor — categories flattened, hard verses softened.
  4. Every era's Gita is somebody's Gita — the present's is no exception.
