Atman: The Self and Its Background

Behind the battlefield conversation stands an older claim: the self is deathless, it belongs to an all, and the wheel it rides can be stopped.

You hold three disciplines now, action, knowledge, and devotion, and the open question of which one is supreme. They are paths. The last words of this vocabulary name who walks them, and the background behind those words names where the paths lead. Neither is the poem's invention: both were argued over for centuries before Krishna is made to speak, and the poem builds on them as on bedrock.

The self beneath the changes

The fourth word is ātman: the self beneath the body and the mind. Not the name you answer to, not the history you tell at dinner, not the body you occupied at seven or the one that answers the door now. The word points under all of that to whatever is aware of the changes, the one reading this sentence, the one who notices that the child's body is gone and is still here to notice the next thing. Cells are replaced. Thoughts arrive and leave. Moods color a week and drain. Character itself shifts across a lifetime, slowly enough to be deniable. Through all of it something registers the before and the after, and that registering thing, stripped of every attribute you could list on a form, is what the word names.

The claim, stated flat, is strange, and the poem states it flat: the self was never born and never dies. It is not killed when the body is killed. It does not age when the body ages. In the walk ahead this is the ground Krishna's first answer stands on; early in chapter two, before he says a word about dharma or fighting, he tells Arjuna that the thing doing the grieving in him was never born and cannot die, and the verse arrives there in full (2.20). Notice what the poem does not do. It does not argue the point. It asserts, then moves to what follows if you grant it. You are free to withhold belief; the verse is a claim, not a proof, and this book will say so again when it walks past. What this page needs is only the word itself, because everything the poem builds afterward leans on it.

Hold it against the field where the chariot has stopped. Arjuna's collapse, which begins on the next page, is grief for bodies: for kin and teachers across the line, and for the family order their deaths would break. Krishna's first reply will refuse the premise of the grief rather than console it. The one who truly exists in every one of those bodies, he will say, was not born and cannot be slain. That is not comfort. It is a change of subject, from what killing does to what killing cannot touch, and whether the change satisfies is a question the poem leaves you holding along with Arjuna.

The all

Now the background the self stands on. brahman is the Upaniṣads' name for the one reality behind all appearances: not a god among gods, not one more powerful resident of the pantheon, but the floor under the floor, what remains when everything nameable has been subtracted. The word grows from a root that means to swell, to grow great. Where the Vedas address a sky full of divine persons, the forest dialogues ask what those persons, and the sky, and the ritual itself, are made of, and this is their word for the answer. Brahman is not the creator in anyone's story. It is what the story, the creator, and the telling all rest on.

The poem files itself under this word. You have seen the receipt stamped at the end of every chapter, the colophon: each chapter of the Gita closes by seating the dialogue in the brahmavidyā, the knowledge of brahman, next to the scripture of yoga and the label of an Upaniṣad. Eighteen times, chapter by chapter, the poem describes itself as a teaching about this floor. Whatever else the battlefield speech turns out to be, it claims, by its own receipt, to be knowledge of brahman delivered as a conversation.

And the poem says the self and the floor are kin. Hold the relation at that: kinship, exact degree unstated. The Upaniṣads contain sayings that push the kinship all the way to identity, most famously you are that, and the Gita inherits the current. It also speaks, in its devotional stretches, as though the self and the Lord were two persons face to face. Both voices run through the poem. What the poem never does, in eighteen chapters, is settle the arithmetic between them, and that silence is not carelessness. It is the room where the deepest argument about this book will live, and the last section of this page walks into it.

The wheel and the stop

So the self is deathless. The next question is what it is doing here at all, and the vocabulary answers with a wheel. saṃsāra is the circuit of death and rebirth: the self passes through body after body, and each life is shaped by the traces the last one left behind. You have already met the engine. On the karma page you saw that the word names three things at once, the deed, its echo, and the cycle the echoes drive; this word is the cycle's own name. Nothing outside you sentences you to the ride. It is momentum, not verdict: consequences rolling forward, and what rolls them is acting, lifetime after lifetime. The wheel is not a place you are sent. It is a direction of travel.

The stop has a word too. mokṣa is release, the wheel's stopping, liberation from the circuit. The root means to set loose, to let free. Notice how the three disciplines now line up, each one an approach to this single exit. The discipline of action aims to act without planting new echoes. The discipline of knowledge aims at the seeing that, on its own account, ends the journey. The discipline of devotion aims at handing the ride over to the one being worshipped. The poem will not rank the three for good, and you already know that fork is open. It is far steadier about the destination: not a better seat on the wheel, but off the wheel.

What the stopped state is like, the poem approaches with care, and its own word for it arrives in the walk: brahmanirvāṇa, one compound, spoken at the close of chapter two. The word joins the name of the floor to a word for going out, the way a flame goes out, and it will be translated before your eyes when it arrives, with the translators disagreeing as they always do. Some read the state as identity achieved. Some read it as perfect nearness. The verse, characteristically, will not referee. Carry the pair as a pair: saṃsāra the problem, mokṣa the exit. Every path in the poem is a proposal about how the exit opens, and every argument between its readers is, at bottom, about what comes through it.

The deepest fork

Here is the fork the whole book turns on. Are the self and the floor one thing, two things, or something stranger in between? The poem says kin. How close the kinship runs is the question, and three great readers took the three positions. Their answers become the first three lenses of Part 3, so meet them here, briefly, with none of them crowned.

Shankara, traditionally dated 788 to 820 CE, says one: the ultimate is brahman, one without a second, and the self is that very reality appearing, for a while and wrongly, as an individual. For him the many-ness of the world is not the final fact about the world but a misreading of it, and release comes as knowledge: when the identity is seen, the mistake that divided the self from the all has nothing left to stand on. On his reading the knowledge path is the summit of the poem, and the other paths are its staircase.

Ramanuja, traditionally 1017 to 1137, says real parts: each self is a mode of brahman, the way a body belongs to the one whose body it is, never dissolving, never discardable. Release, on this reading, is not identity but eternal loving service, the self remaining itself in permanent relation to what it serves. Where Shankara's exit dissolves the divider, Ramanuja's perfects the relation.

Madhva, 1238 to 1317, says never: reality holds five eternal distinctions, God from souls, souls from matter, and the relations among them, and the soul's eternity is an eternity of dependence on a sovereign Lord, not of identity with him. On his reading the poem's devotional current is not a concession made to weaker readers; it is the point, and the separate, unmerged bliss of the self is not the lesser fate. It is the better one.

All three wrote commentaries on this same poem, and all three found their answers already speaking in it, which should tell you something about how the poem is built. You will get each case in full in Part 3, argued from the verses it leans on. This book will not rank them, and neither, quite, does the poem. Carry the fork open. Whether the self and the floor are identical runs deeper than the question of which path is supreme, because it decides what release means at all. If the self is the floor, release is the end of a mistake; if the selves are parts or dependents, release is the perfection of a relation. The poem gives you both voices and leaves you standing in the argument, which is where every serious reader of it has stood since.

That is the fourth word, and the four are carried: what holds, what moves and sticks, how to act, and who is acting, on what floor, bound to what wheel, promised what stop. The vocabulary is finished. The poem begins. A chariot stops between two armies.

Takeaways

  1. Ātman is the self beneath the changing body and mind — the Gita says it was never born and never dies.
  2. Brahman is the Upaniṣads' name for the reality behind all appearances.
  3. Saṃsāra is the wheel of rebirth karma drives; mokṣa is its stopping.
  4. Whether ātman and brahman are identical is the deepest fork in the book — three chapters of lenses will disagree.
