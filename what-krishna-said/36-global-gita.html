The Gita Goes Global

Since its first English translation the Gita has traveled light: Emerson pocketed it, Thoreau read it beside a pond, and the physicist who built the bomb quoted it at the flash. Every crossing since has left fingerprints on the gift.

The last page ended in the marketplace, holding the claim that every era's Gita is somebody's Gita. The journeys abroad prove it with suitcases: the same seven hundred verses riding in a governor's cargo, a New England knapsack, a physicist's memory, a swami's trunk. This page follows the poem out of India and watches what each crossing did — to the poem, and to the people who took it in.

The first crossing

The crossing was an empire's errand from the first day. In 1785 a London bookseller issued The Bhagvat-geeta, or Dialogues of Kreeshna and Arjoon, the first translation of the poem made directly from the Sanskrit into a European language, and the man who made it was Charles Wilkins — a printer by training, a writer in the East India Company's service by trade. Posted upriver to Benares, the old city of Sanskrit learning, Wilkins worked through the language with a pundit beside him, and with that help he carried the Gita into English verse by verse.

Look at the hands on the gift, because none of them were hidden. The work was championed by Warren Hastings, the Company's governor-general in Bengal, who urged his masters in London to publish it and signed his name to its preface. An empire busy consolidating its conquest of India paid to introduce India's most portable scripture to Europe: a company man did the translating, the empire's chief officer in Bengal did the recommending, the empire's capital did the printing. The gift was real, and the fingerprints were real too. This book states both and moves on.

There was a third pair of hands, quieter than the others. Kasinatha Bhattacharya, a pundit of Benares, checked the translation against the Sanskrit, and the title page does not carry his name. The book that taught Europe the Gita had a co-author its own preface never thanks.

Europe answered fast, and divided at first sight. A French version came in 1787 and a German one in 1802, both from Wilkins's English rather than the Sanskrit; Schlegel's Latin version of 1823 carried the poem into every learned study on the continent. Schopenhauer kept the Latin Gita on his table and called it the consolation of his life. Hegel read it and stayed cold, critical of what he saw as its machine of thought. Wilhelm von Humboldt lectured on it in Berlin and pronounced it — in a sentence that circulates in several wordings, all of them admiring — the most beautiful philosophical song in any known tongue. The poem arrived abroad the way it arrives everywhere: with devotees, with skeptics, and a large crowd in between that kept reading.

New England

The Wilkins Gita crossed the Atlantic and found its first American home in New England. Emerson read it and let it work on him for decades. Thoreau carried a copy to his cabin at Walden Pond, and his own Walden, published in 1854, records the habit: reading the Bhagvat Geeta — his spelling — in the mornings, between the pond and the woodpile.

Notice what the Americans kept and what they left. The poem arrived without its scaffolding — no caste frame, no ritual calendar, no temple, no commentators — and what New England took from it was conscience: the inner law set above inherited forms, duty heard as an inward command rather than a position in an order. In the transcendentalists' reading the Gita became an American book almost overnight, a scripture for people who trusted the still voice over the ceremony around it. That is the second making: not the empire's gift but the conscience's.

The current widened from there. In the late nineteenth century the Theosophical Society — Blavatsky's first, then Besant's — spread interest in the Gita across the West, and Besant published her own translation. Onto stages the era had built stepped the monk you met on the activists' page: his 1893 Chicago appearance carried Hindu vocabulary into American lecture halls, and the Gita rode the wave behind him. By the turn of the century the poem was no longer a curiosity in a governor's report. It was on shelves, and it stayed.

The flash

You have met this verse once before, on the field, in the walk through chapter 11 — and that page left the physicist's story untold, promising him to this part of the book. Here is the deferred story. A young professor of physics at Berkeley began studying Sanskrit around 1933, under a teacher named Arthur Ryder, whose English renderings of the poem he absorbed and carried. The professor's name was Robert Oppenheimer. A dozen years on, he directed the laboratory that built the first atomic bomb.

July 16, 1945: the Trinity test, in the New Mexico desert, the first detonation of an atomic weapon. The fireball climbed, the night turned to day, and the man who ran the laboratory stood and watched. By his own account, given long afterward in his own words, a line of Sanskrit surfaced in him at the flash. The line was this one.

Hold the timeline honestly, because it is usually told carelessly. The physicist did not cry out a translation in the desert. The English the whole world knows — Now I am become Death, the destroyer of worlds — was spoken to a television camera in 1965, two decades after Trinity, in an interview about the decision to drop the bomb. I remembered the line from the Hindu scripture, he said — remembered is his own verb — and the English he remembered it in was his teacher's. The translation note above the field said it plainly: kāla is time, not death; the death word, mṛtyu, sat unused in the Sanskrit; the darker rendering follows Ryder. In the interview he even retold the frame as duty — a god persuading a prince that he should do his duty — which is one reading of the verse, and a gentle one to the reteller.

The state was not finished with him. When his government later convened a closed hearing into his loyalty, the poem went into the record there as well: in the written reply read aloud at the opening of the proceedings, the physicist named the Sanskrit he had studied and the teacher he had read it with. The verse itself stayed unspoken in that room. The language it lived in did not.

And the argument over the quotation has never closed, because the verse does not close. Heard as consolation, it dissolves the doer's guilt: the dead are dead by time's hand, the instrument is an instrument. Heard as horror, it is a man telling you what he has just become. Oppenheimer's readers split along exactly that seam — absolution or dread, license or grief — and the poem, as you saw on the field, holds both readings and chooses neither. Even the companion phrase, brighter than a thousand suns, reached its famous English through a journalist's book in 1958 rather than the physicist's lips at the flash; the record keeps needing its flags.

The mass era

After the war the poem went into pockets. The beat era's Gita was the Prabhavananda and Isherwood translation, published in 1944 and reissued as a mass-market paperback in 1951 — the poem in drugstore racks, read by a generation that met India through paperbacks.

But the mass era belongs to a bigger engine, and you have already glimpsed its engineer. The previous page's parade of modern Gitas passed a swami, Bhaktivedanta, who carried the poem abroad as the one thing that must not be reinterpreted. His edition — Bhagavad-gītā As It Is — appeared in an abridged Macmillan printing in 1968 and in a complete edition in 1972. His followers pressed it into hands at airport terminals and street corners, and the publisher now claims copies in the hundreds of millions — a publisher's figure, unaudited, but large enough that nobody disputes the title: the most distributed Gita in English.

The edition carries two controversies, and this page states both and settles neither. The first is the translations. They did not stay fixed. The first printing cut the manuscript down; the complete edition restored it; and printings revised after the author's death altered the verse renderings again — changes the publisher presents as fidelity to the manuscript, and that critics, some of them from inside the movement, call tampering. Set the printings side by side and you can watch the text move under a title that says as it is. The second is the preface, which warns the reader. In his own words the author promises the poem 'without adulteration' and warns that the Gita read through the wrong commentary misleads — that the student who takes it up outside the devotional line of reading ends up bewildered on the path. A gift, with a gatekeeper standing at the gate.

So the journey ends where the marketplace said it would: everybody's own Gita, and now literally everybody's — a governor's, a pond-reader's, a physicist's, a swami's, and, by the hundreds of millions, anyone's at all. Each crossing edited the poem, and each edited its readers back. And alongside the readers, from the earliest decades, came the scholars — a colder set of eyes, asking not what the poem means but what it is made of, when it was made, and whether one poem made it at all. The next page takes up their lens: dating the poem, cutting its layers, doubting its unity.

Takeaways

  1. The first English Gita was an empire's gift — funded by a governor, translated by a company man.
  2. New England made it American: conscience over ritual.
  3. The time-verse outlived its chapter: quoted at the first bomb test, remembered at the hearing after.
  4. The most distributed English edition is also the most contested — an 'as it is' with edits.
