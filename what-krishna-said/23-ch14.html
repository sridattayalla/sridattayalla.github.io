Chapter 14 — The Three Strands

The field runs on three strands — light, drive, and dark — and each one binds you by the thing you enjoy most.

Chapter 13 left you with a clean line: the body is the field, the one who knows it is the field-knower, and the sight worth training is the one that finds the same lord in every being. But the field itself stayed whole — one unnamed territory of body, senses, and mind. Now the field gets dissected. The dissection finds three strands where you might have expected one stuff, and the chapter's quiet scandal is that none of the three is innocent.

Three strands, three snares

You have met the strands once before. In chapter 3, Krishna let the scheme out almost in passing: nature does the acting, he said there, and the deluded mind claims the doing for itself. The word this book has used since is guṇa — strand, cord, thread — and chapter 3 let it go at that. Chapter 14 gives the strands a stage of their own, deep in the jñāna hexad, chapters 13 to 18, the six chapters that work on the self. That six-chapter map is a readers' construction — Madhusudana Sarasvati drew it in the sixteenth century, and Yāmunācārya held a version of it centuries earlier (916–1041 CE) — but the placement fits: this chapter reads like anatomy.

The chapter counts before it explains.

Three claims, in an order that matters. The strands are born of prakṛti — nature, the field's own stuff — not of you. They bind. And what they bind is the embodied one, the imperishable, fast in the body: the very kṣetrajña chapter 13 taught you to find, watching. Sit with that grammar. The prison is not the body; the prison is the field's own ropes, and what they tie is the one inside. Chapter 13 distinguished the field from its knower precisely so this verse could say who gets tied.

Krishna names the three in his first breath, and the book keeps all three names. sattva is the strand of light — the glosses say lucidity. rajas is the strand of drive — passion. tamas is the strand of dark — inertia. Three materials, three tempers, one shared job description: binding.

The diagram's outline is the point. The strands are not visitors to the field; they are what the field is made of — prakṛti spun three ways, one loom working three threads. And the chapter does not stop at the count. The next three verses take the strands one at a time, and each gets the same sentence shape: a portrait, then the word binds.

Read the portrait first, because it flatters. Stainless, luminous, unailing — this is the strand you would vote for. When the mind is a clean window, when the body runs well, when a thing is understood without static, that is sattva at work; anyone who has had a good week has felt it. Then the sentence turns. It binds, the verse says — through attachment to happiness and to knowledge. Do not soften this, because the poem does not. The Gita is not saying that light is good, dark is bad, and drive sits between; it is saying that even the light is a rope, only a brighter one, and a golden rope holds as well as hemp. Knowledge is on the list. Held as a possession — I know, I understand — the clearest seeing becomes another tie. The pleasantness is real, and that is exactly why it holds you: a bond that hurts gets cut; a bond that feels like your best self gets defended.

Rajas gets the least flattering genealogy in the chapter: born of craving, attachment-bred, binding the embodied by attachment to action. Notice where that lands on the man in the chariot. The poem has spent its first half pushing Arjuna toward disciplined action — karma-yoga, the wager of chapters 2 and 3 — and now the strand of action is named a binder. The contradiction dissolves once you see what exactly is called a rope. Not action: the thirst riding the action, the demand that the act pay out. Karma-yoga's old instruction — act, and let the fruits go — was already training in action without this strand's traction. And rajas is, on any plain reading, the warrior's professional material: heat, velocity, the appetite to win. Arjuna is not being told it is evil. He is being told it is a rope.

Tamas needs the least argument. Born of ignorance, bewildering all embodied beings, binding through heedlessness, sloth and sleep — the strand nobody defends, the fog a mind defaults to when nothing pulls it. You can read chapter 1 backward from here: the trembling, the dry mouth, the bow sliding from the hand — a man in fog, mistaking the fog for conscience. But mark what the verse does not say. It does not call tamas rare, or foreign, or someone else's portion. It binds all embodied beings — the same rope on the same loom as the golden one.

Between these portraits and its close, the chapter watches the strands take turns — one rising as another sets, knowledge and error, restlessness and dullness traded like weather; it even says which strand prevails in a man at death, and where each one sends him. But the portraits have already made the point, and it is this page's hardest: there is no strand to shop for. Trade tamas for rajas, rajas for sattva, and you have changed cells, not left the prison. The exits, when the chapter finally offers them, are exits from the whole trio at once.

The exit through seeing

Two exits remain, and they differ in kind, not degree. The first is not a practice or a prayer. It is a way of looking, and the verse frames it as a condition: when the seer sees.

Look at what the seeing finds: no doer apart from the strands. This is chapter 13 paying out. The knower was stationed in the field as a witness; now the witness works. You go looking for the one who has been doing your life, and what you find is machinery — strands rotating, mood lifting and souring, energy rising and sinking, each state explaining itself as the last one's successor. The seer does not defeat the strands. The seer stops signing their work. And the verse names where this lands: knowing what is beyond the strands, that one reaches my being — Krishna's being, not a blank. The exit through seeing turns out to be a theology: past the machinery is not nothing. It is him.

The translation note flags the verse's edge, and the edge is real: if the strands do everything, where is free will? Chapter 14 is the sharpest determinism in the text, and readers split on it. Shankara takes the verse at full strength — for him the doer was never real, and seeing that is the seeing. A gentler reading keeps the verse aimed at a smaller target: what dissolves is not your capacity to choose but the conceit that says I am the one doing — the doer-claim, not the doing. The verse holds both doors open, and this book has promised you no verdict. What it can report is the verse's own next clause: the one who sees this way is carried into a being the strands never wove.

The exit through devotion

The second exit arrives without argument. One sentence, no condition of sight, one condition of loyalty.

The verb has changed. In the first exit you see; in this one you serve. Whoever serves me with unwavering devotion — the verse's own compound is bhakti-yoga — crosses beyond these strands and is made fit for brahman. Three words deserve the pause. Unwavering: devotion that does not turn aside, a bearing rather than a mood. Me: the same speaker who offered his being as the destination of sight now takes service as the road there. And made fit: the outcome is conferred, not performed. The devotee is never described as out-seeing the machine. The devotee is carried past it.

Set the two exits side by side and the chapter's shape appears. One is diagnostic — search for the doer and find strands. One is relational — serve, and what binds you lets go. Chapter 12, two pages back, described the beloved devotee by conduct; this verse says what such conduct does: it unbinds. Notice that the diagram draws no door out of the triangle, and that is honest. The exits are not structures inside the field. They are what the field's two relationships to its knower become when either one is taken all the way.

The strands do not vanish after this chapter; they grow. Chapter 15 stretches the three into a tree — the banyan, rooted above, branching downward through the worlds, its leaves the hymns. The machine you have just been shown becomes a forest you are living inside, and the tool changes to match: not a sight, not a service, but an axe.

Takeaways

  1. The strands are the field's machinery: sattva, rajas, tamas.
  2. Every strand is still a strand — even luminous clarity is a bond.
  3. Seeing no doer but the strands, the seer stands free of the doer-claim.
  4. Devotion is the other exit: the strand-machine cannot bind the devotee.
