The Other Classical Lenses

The famous three had rivals: between-and-both schools, a Kashmiri aesthetician who asked what the poem does to its reader, and a Marathi saint who made the Gita sing to farmers.

The last page gave you two refusals of the ladder: Ramanuja seating every soul in God's body, Madhva walling five eternal distinctions around each of them, and both reading the poem's final verse as a charter of surrender. With Shankara ahead of them they make the famous three — and the shelf was never only three. A single modern critical edition gathers eleven full Sanskrit commentaries on this poem, and even that count holds only the Sanskrit. The rest of the classical crowd takes this page: the between-and-both schools, a Kashmiri aesthetician, and a boy from Marathi farm country who moved the poem out of Sanskrit altogether.

Between and both

The three famous lenses answer one question — how far does the one reality go? Shankara's advaita takes it all the way; Madhva's dvaita stops it at the first distinction. Between those poles sat whole families of readers who refused to choose, and Bhāskara is their plainest early voice: a Vedāntin of the generations after Shankara who could not follow the great monist where he led. For Bhāskara the world was no veil. Brahman genuinely becomes what you see — cause turning into effect, the way cloth is nothing but its threads and still is not what anyone calls threads. His school's name for the position is bhedābheda, difference-and-non-difference, and it is less a compromise between the famous poles than a refusal of the question's shape. The world is really God, so nothing is scenery; the world is really other than God, so worship is not a mistake. Both halves carry weight, and neither is allowed to win.

Nimbarka comes later in the both-and line, and tradition seats his school near Mathura, in the cowherd country where Krishna was already at home. His lens folds the two answers into one word: dvaitādvaita, twoness-and-not-twoness. The soul stands to God as a ray stands to the sun — made of nothing else, going nowhere else, and never the thing itself. So with the whole world: nothing exists on its own, and nothing dissolves into what it depends on. Devotion, on this lens, is the natural posture of a ray that knows what it is made of. The line Nimbarka founded still runs, and its worship sets Radha beside Krishna, beloved and lord at once — which tells you where the both-and families put their weight. The metaphysics exists to make love possible, not to explain love away.

Vallabha, born 1479, dead 1531, gave the both-and crowd its sweetest reading. His lens is named śuddhādvaita, pure non-dualism — non-dualism with the māyā subtracted. The world is not God's concealment but God's expression, real at every level, and the souls within it are Krishna's own partial expressions. His Gita commentary is called the Jñānadīpa, the lamp of the Gita's meaning, and its light falls on grace: the soul does not climb to God, because the soul cannot walk that far. What cannot walk is carried. Vallabha's line is the Pushti Marg, the way of grace, and its worship centers on the child god of the cowherd country — Krishna as an infant to be fed and cared for, devotion running the way a parent's love runs, toward someone who needs you. The path was built for householders: no renunciation required, marriage no barrier, the farm and the kitchen fully inside the discipline.

The aesthetician

While the Vedāntins argued about what the poem says, Kashmir was running a different argument: what a poem is, and what it does to the reader when it works. Abhinavagupta, born around 950 and dead around 1016, is the summit of that conversation. The tradition credits him with the deepest account of rasa, the flavor a reader tastes; his work on how poetry and drama produce their effects in an audience still sets the terms of that field. And the same mind turned to this poem. His summary of it, the Gītārtha-saṅgraha — the gathering of the Gita's meaning — is one of the few classical commentaries on this shelf that is neither Vaishnava nor Advaita. It is a Shaiva reading, a reading from the tradition of Shiva.

On his reading, the poem teaches the identity of Shiva and Krishna as the self of all. When Krishna says me — as he says it again and again through the middle chapters — the word, on this lens, names what is looking out of every eye, yours included. But the reason Abhinavagupta sits on this page is less the doctrine than the angle. A reader trained to count what a poem does to its audience cannot read even a doctrine-poem as doctrine only. The Gita is a made thing: it opens on a collapse, it stages a correction, it moves a man from trembling to standing, and the movement it produces in the hearer is part of its meaning — not decoration on top of a teaching but the teaching's own delivery. That question — what does this poem do? — enters the commentarial world through Kashmir, and it never fully leaves.

The saint

Far to the south of Kashmir, and generations later, in Marathi farm country, the poem changed languages. Jñānadeva — born 1275, dead 1296 by the traditional count, a life of about twenty-one years — rendered the Gita into Marathi verse around 1290. The work is the Jñāneshwarī, and it is not a translation in the shy sense. Each Sanskrit verse opens into Marathi that walks, repeats, lingers, breaks into song, and every page of it is soaked in bhakti. The Sanskrit original was sealed property: to read it you needed years of training most people were never offered. Jñānadeva took the poem to the people who could not read Sanskrit — which was, in his country, nearly everyone.

What changes when a saint retells a teaching poem for villages is visible on every page. The doctrine stays — the deathless self, the discipline of works, the refuge at the end — but the register moves into the household and the field. God shows up as mother and friend: the one who feeds you, the one at the door, closer than close, scolded and petted and relied on the way villagers rely on each other. Abstract words grow faces. The poem had always been able to say his self absorbed in me; the Marathi makes you watch the absorption happen to someone you could meet. The retelling made the Gita a people's text in Maharashtra and became foundational for the devotional culture that grew from it — a culture that still treats the poem as something you sing where you live, not something you decode where you were trained.

The contest verse

Chapter 6 ended by moving a crown, and you have already watched it move. The meditation manual's page walked this verse: after the seat, the diet, the measured sleep, and the retrieval of the straying mind, the chapter's last word ranks the one who worships with faith above every adept — love ranked above mastery, the hinge toward the devotion chapters. That walk stands. The question this page asks of the same words is different. Not what the verse ranks, but who it crowns — because on this shelf, every lens claims the crown for its own kind of reader.

The most yoked of all. Every classical lens reads the crowned figure as a portrait of its own reader. For Shankara's line, the worshipper's absorption is knowledge in devotional dress: the crown goes to the one in whom the separate doer has gone quiet, and me names brahman. For Ramanuja's, the verse crowns the self doing what it was made for — his self absorbed in me is the mode turned toward its whole, service at full stretch. For Madhva's, faith is the act of a soul that knows it cannot free itself, and the crown rests on an eternal dependent's devotion. The both-and families read the verse as their charter in miniature: worship requires two, absorption makes one field of them, and the crowned reader holds difference and non-difference in a single act. For Abhinavagupta, me is the self of all, and the crown goes to the recognizer. For Jñānadeva, the crown lands on the villager singing in Marathi — the one who loves without a word of Sanskrit.

Six readings of one superlative, and this book referees none of them; the claims sit side by side and the table stays standing. But notice what the contest shows. Each lens looks at the top of the poem's ranking and finds a portrait of the reader it came from. Either the verse is hopelessly loose, or the superlative is doing what superlatives in scripture do: holding the door open wide enough that every discipline walks through believing itself crowned. You have now met enough lenses to suspect the second. You have not met enough to settle it — and neither did the classical world, which argues about this verse to this day.

One thing the whole classical crowd shares, from the coldest advaita to the warmest Marathi song: none of them asks what the poem does in time. For every lens so far, the Gita is eternal — addressed to Arjuna and to every soul at once, the war a setting, the history scenery. The vernacular saint is the first crack in that wall. His Jñāneshwarī was written in a language with an address, for villages with names, and centuries later it would be quoted alongside Tilak's Gita Rahasya. The moderns arrive with a question the classics never asked: what does the Gita do in history?

Takeaways

  1. Between non-dualism and dualism sat whole families of both-and schools.
  2. One Kashmiri reader counted the poem's beauty, not only its doctrine.
  3. A Marathi saint took the poem to people who could not read Sanskrit.
  4. Every lens wants the crown of the chapter that ranks the devotee highest.
