Chapter 5 — Renounce or Act

Chapter 5 opens as a courtroom. Renounce the world or act in it — that is the charge sheet — and the verdict, when it comes, is that the two descriptions fit one actor.

You left chapter 4 inside a fire. Nothing purifies like knowledge, Krishna said there — as fire turns fuel to ash, knowledge burns the residue of acts to ash — and the avatāra doctrine and the contested verse on the four varnas came with it. A fire that potent raises its own next question, and chapter 5 asks it at once: does the man burned clean have to leave the world to stay that way?

The referee verse

Arjuna opens the chapter by demanding a verdict. You praise the renunciation of action, he tells Krishna, and you praise the yoga of action too — tell me for certain which of the two. It is the question of a man who has been handed two teachings since chapter 2 forked the road in front of him, one line for the knower and one for the man of works, and who suspects he cannot walk both at once. He asks the court for a ruling.

Two rulings arrive in two lines. First: both carry one to the highest. Renunciation and karma-yoga are not rival destinations but two descriptions of one arrival. Second: of the two, karma-yoga excels. Notice how narrow the comparative is. The verse does not say renunciation fails; it does not say the leaving is for lesser souls. It sets one thing against one thing — giving up the act itself, or acting while giving up the inner claim to the act — and awards the round to action. And the verdict settles the quarrel by deferring the question, because nothing in it yet explains how a man can renounce while acting. The explanation is the rest of the chapter, and it is a relocation: renunciation moves from the limbs to somewhere behind the eyes.

One reader built a book on that comparative. Bal Gangadhar Tilak drafted his Gita Rahasya in the prison at Mandalay, begun in 1908 and published in 1915, to argue that the poem's single thread is karma-yoga — energetic action in the world, duty done with the fruit surrendered, not flight from society. For Tilak this half-line was a manifesto: political work, not monkhood, was the Gita's call. This book takes up his reading among the lens chapters to come. Here, notice only how much weight a comparative clause can carry — one half of one verse, and a freedom movement found its marching orders in it.

I do nothing

The chapter then tests its own verdict against ordinary life. What does the man of disciplined action actually experience, hour by hour, while he keeps working? The answer is the strangest sentence the poem has produced so far.

Read the middle of the verse slowly, because it is a grocery list of the mundane: seeing, hearing, touching, smelling, eating, walking, sleeping, breathing. The sage is not levitating above these events or refusing them. He does every one of them and thinks — the verse's own verb — I do nothing at all. Chapter 3 argued the point in the abstract: the strands act on the strands, and the 'I' that claims authorship is misreading the scene. Chapter 5 hands the argument to perception. This is not a doctrine to affirm on a festival day; it is a way of seeing, rehearsed against the most ordinary inventory the poem owns.

The next verse supplies the effort under the calm. Speaking, releasing, taking, opening and closing the eyes — the same ordinariness, then a different verb: he holds firmly. Even sight is not a mood that descends on the fortunate; it is a stance kept. The word behind that stance is yukta, yoked — the harness word you have carried since the Four Words pages; the verse's first line shows the sandhi-bent form yukto. And mark the setting. This is said on a battlefield, to an archer about to fight, not to a hermit in a hut. The sage does not stop acting; he stops signing his name to the acting. That is how the verdict at 5.2 could go the way it went. Renunciation, relocated behind the eyes, no longer competes with action — it rides inside it.

The leveling gaze

Then the chapter aims that even gaze outward, at other beings, and assembles the most quarreled-over catalog in the poem.

Look at the order of the list before anything else. It starts at the summit of regard: a brahmin with learning and courtesy. It steps down through beings valued but never consulted — a cow, an elephant, a dog. Then it steps off the ladder entirely: the dog-cooker, an outcaste occupation, work the order of the day treated as polluting. The catalog is built as a descent from the top of the social world to below its floor, and its verb is seeing. The learned — paṇḍitāḥ, the same word chapter 2 spent on those who grieve neither for the living nor the dead — see the same in all of them. Not are the same: see the same. The sameness lives in the seer's eye, and what that eye is aimed at is the ātman, which does not vary with the body it wears or the work the world assigns that body.

This verse has two futures, and this page flags both without arguing either. In Part 3, Gandhi will build his charter on it: his commentary on the poem, discourses delivered in Gujarati in 1926, makes the even gaze a public discipline — the same self looking out of every body, and no ranking of the vessels it looks through that can survive the verse. In the same part of the book, Ambedkar will cross-examine the poem that holds this verse, charging that its even metaphysics was marshaled to steady a social order rather than level one. Part 4 will press the colder question, whether a metaphysics of even sight is a sociology at all.

Set the objection beside the catalog and you can see what it holds. The verse levels what the seer sees and says nothing here about stations, duties, or who may touch whom. The same poem ties the four varnas to the strands at 4.13 and returns to each order's duties at 18.41, and the translation note above has already marked that coexistence. Seeing the same self in everyone and seating everyone in the same place are two different claims, and the verse does not make the second. The book keeps both futures open on purpose; the argument belongs to later pages.

The friend

The chapter could have ended on the catalog. Instead it ends with a signature — three titles in one sentence, and a strange quiet after the courtroom noise.

The order of the titles is a descent of a different kind. The enjoyer of sacrifices and austerities: the yajña of chapter 3, recycled — whatever the rite offered, the one speaking here is the one who receives it. The great lord of all worlds: the avatāra of chapter 4 grown to full size, authority now stated without genealogy. And third, the smallest and strangest of the titles: the friend of all beings. Suhṛt — well-wisher, the one who wishes you well, a word for a person who holds no office in your life and performs no function, only goodwill. The chapter that opened as a courtroom and ran a training in perception closes by introducing the judge as a friend.

Note the verb the verse hangs everything on: knowing. Knowing me thus — enjoyer, lord, friend — one attains peace. Not fasting, not leaving, not even acting: knowing. Peace is the fruit of a recognition, and the recognition is relational. The even gaze of the catalog needed something to be even toward; this verse says that something looks back. Śānti, peace, is the chapter's word for where the whole run of argument was headed, and it arrives not as retreat from the world but as the quiet of a man who knows he has a friend in it.

Chapter 6 now turns from argument to practice. The verdict has been handed down, the perception rehearsed, the judge introduced by name. What is still missing is method — how to sit, where, how long — and what to do about a mind that will not hold still. Arjuna is about to ask exactly that.

Takeaways

  1. Both paths reach the goal; disciplined action is the surer road.
  2. The sage's "I do nothing" is a way of seeing, not a way of sitting still.
  3. The same self stands in scholar, cow, elephant, dog, and outcaste — the verse Gandhi will build on and Ambedkar will cross-examine.
  4. The chapter closes with Krishna's self-introduction: enjoyer, lord, friend.
