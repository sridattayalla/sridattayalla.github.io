Chapter 15 — The Upside-Down Tree

The poem's strangest image: a banyan rooted in heaven, fruiting as your experience — and the only tool that cuts it is detachment's axe.

Chapter 14 counted the field's strands and found every one of them a bond, even luminous sattva, with two ways out: seeing no doer but the strands, or devotion. Chapter 15 adds no fourth strand and no third exit. It plants the machinery instead, and grows it into one tree — upside-down, rooted where you expected to find the fruit.

The tree described

Krishna has spent two chapters handing you parts — field and knower, then the strands that run the field — and chapter 15 opens with the assembled whole, delivered without preamble. There is a tree. It is an aśvattha, the banyan fig, the tree that shades every courtyard of this poem's world. This one is called imperishable, and it grows the wrong way up: roots above, branches below, its leaves the Vedic hymns. Then comes a claim shaped like a diploma — whoever knows this tree knows the Vedas.

Start with the name: the translation note above hands you a joke the tradition savors. Aśvattha parses as not-standing-the-same-till-tomorrow — the tree that stands for the endless cycle is named for lasting a day. The figure is older than the poem; the Katha Upaniṣad already hails the roots-upward fig as the imperishable brahman, and Krishna grafts his teaching onto it. The leaves are the hymns: on this tree the Vedas are foliage, part of the growth, not a report on it. And the diploma cuts both ways. Read one way, the verse honors the hymns as the living leaves of the world-tree. Read the other, it files them as decoration on something you are about to be told to cut down. Shankara, in his commentary, reads the figure outright as the tree of saṃsāra — transmigration drawn as vegetation. The verse does not settle which reading of its leaves is right.

The next verse sets the tree in motion, and the botany becomes exact. A real banyan does what no other tree does so well: it drops roots from its branches, and those roots become new trunks, and the tree walks. The upside-down banyan does the same, at the scale of a world.

Now match the parts to the picture. The branches spread down and up, grown fat on the strands — sattva, rajas, tamas, the three you learned to count. The shoots at the branch-tips, the fresh buds, are the sense-fields: the sights and sounds and tastes your senses feed on. And the secondary roots, the ones dropping toward the ground, are karmānubandhīni, bound to action — every attachment a root hardening into a trunk, another channel from the branch you sit on down into the soil of rebirth. That is what the inversion is for. The primal root is above, out of sight; what you meet are the tips. Experience is the tree's newest growth, and the oldest thing about you is the part you never see.

A tree described this exactly is not described for admiration. It is described the way a surgeon maps an organ before the incision — so that you will know what the cut is passing through.

The axe

First, the bad news about seeing. The tree's true form, this verse says, is not grasped here — no end, no beginning, no resting place. You cannot survey it, because you are on it. Every bud hangs from a branch that hangs from a branch, back to a root that starts above the sky. And then the verb arrives, and it is not see, or know, or meditate. It is cut.

The instrument is the argument. The axe is the verse's own word, asaṅga — non-attachment — and it must be dṛḍha, firm, hard enough to bite a trunk with no beginning. Chapter 14's exits were seeing and devotion; this is a third instrument, and notice what it is not: a way of knowing. Knowledge of the tree — even the full knowledge the first verse promises, the kind that knows the Vedas — leaves the tree standing. You can map a prison perfectly and still live in it. What fells the aśvattha is the release of grip: letting go of the buds you have been calling fruit. The verse does not say the tree is unreal; it says detach, and cut. Readers will argue for a long time about what exactly is being cut. The poem's own verb is carpentry.

The command also assumes something about you: that you are attached. No one cuts a tree he is not living in. The axe is aimed at the branches you call your life — the wants that budded, the roots you grew — and the chapter has already told you they are the tree's newest growth, not its oldest part.

The seat beyond the lights

Cut, and then what? The next verse gives the direction of the cut, and the direction is upstream, against the traffic, toward the root.

The destination is described as a seeking: that resting-place is to be sought, the one gone to without return. The Sanskrit calls it pada — a seat, a step, a foothold — and the verse's prayer takes refuge in the first person, the puruṣa from whom the ancient current set out. Follow the plumbing: the tree's whole traffic runs from that root downward, life spilling out along the branches into buds. The cut reverses the traffic. And the seat is given a single property, stated as a triple negative: neither sun nor moon nor fire lights it.

Sun, moon, fire: day, night, and the hearth — the lights that order every life and every altar. None of them reach the seat. The poem has used negation before, but rarely this starkly. The destination is not said to be bright; it is said to be unlit, past the reach of the very lights by which you are reading this sentence. Whatever else it is, it is not a place you could photograph — and the negation sharpens the tree as well. The tree is all surface, bud and color, sense-object upon sense-object; its beyond is a lightless point.

Then, before the chapter stacks its persons, the seat's occupant is named — by the one doing the teaching. I am seated in the hearts of all; from me come memory, knowledge, and their loss. The third item is the startling one: even forgetting runs through him. And the claim about the leaves comes home. By all the Vedas I alone am to be known; I am the maker of Vedanta, the knower of the Veda. The foliage was the hymns; the knower of the Veda now says he is what the hymns were reaching for. The seat no lights illumine is not vacant. The person sought at the root is already seated in your chest, running your memory.

Three persons stacked

The chapter's close is an inventory. There are two persons in this world, the verse says: the perishable and the imperishable. The perishable is all beings — everything that buds and drops, the whole population of the tree's tips. The imperishable is called the fixed one, kūṭastha — the anvil that does not move while the world pours past it, the witness the field-verses of chapter 13 circled without naming. Two tiers: what changes, and what watches.

Then the stack takes a third layer. Other than both is the highest person, called the highest self — entering the three worlds, upholding them, the imperishable lord. Not the beings; not the witness; the one inside and beneath the whole show. The chapter has stopped doing botany and started doing architecture: perishable beings, imperishable witness, and a highest who carries both.

Then the chapter signs itself. Because I am beyond the perishable, Krishna says, and higher even than the imperishable, in the world and in the Veda I am celebrated as the highest person. The translation note is exact: this is the puruṣottama verse, the three-tier figure that names the chapter — the text's own closing colophon calls chapter 15 the yoga of the highest person. The chapter began with a tree no one can see and ends with a self-identification: the root above, the occupant of the lightless seat, the third layer of the stack — the voice that has been talking says it is that one. Hear it as comfort or as the largest first-person claim in the poem; either way the verse does not soften it.

So the chapter's ledger reads: one tree, one axe, one seat with no lamps, and a stack of three persons with the teacher's name on the top tier. Behind the cut tree lies cleared ground. Chapter 16 walks onto it holding two lists — one for what is cut away, one for what remains — and it will say there is no middle ground between them.

Takeaways

  1. The banyan is the cycle of rebirth as vegetation: rooted above, fruiting as experience below.
  2. The tree's cut is not knowledge alone — the axe is detachment.
  3. The destination is a seat that no sun, moon, or fire lights.
  4. Three persons are stacked: perishable beings, the imperishable witness, the highest — and Krishna names himself the last.
