How to Read This Book

Every verse appears in three layers: the Sanskrit in Roman letters, a literal gloss, and the arguments readers have had about it. This book never picks a winner.

You are on the first page of forty, and the cover has already made you a promise: every reading attributed, and no verdicts, ever. This page is the machinery behind that promise. It shows you how each verse will be printed, what the marks above the letters mean, and the rules the book follows when readers disagree, so that every page after this one runs the same way.

Three layers

Every verse in this book arrives in the same small block, and the block never changes shape. A caption at the top gives the verse's address, chapter first and verse second: Bhagavad Gita 2.47, say. Beneath the caption come the three layers.

The first layer is the Sanskrit itself, one line of it, set in IAST: the Roman alphabet fitted to Sanskrit with a few extra marks. You don't have to be able to read it. It sits on the page so that every English word below it can be checked against the original, and so that nothing you are told about the poem has to be taken on faith.

The second layer is the gloss, one plain English line that says what the Sanskrit says, keeping the Sanskrit's word order wherever English allows it. The gloss is deliberately flat. It is the floor of the verse's meaning, not the ceiling, and it refuses what a famous translation is allowed to do: no added rhythm, no smuggled theology, no elegant smoothing of the awkward parts. Whatever else a verse means, the gloss tells you it means at least this much.

Expect the gloss to sound wooden in places. The woodenness is the price of checkability. A translator who reaches for the best English sentence has already decided questions the Sanskrit left open, and this book would rather show you the open places than hand you a finished line. When a gloss seems thinner than a verse you love, the note underneath usually says where the thickness went.

The third layer is the note, labeled Translation note, and it appears only where translators disagree, which is often. A note points at the fork in the line: the word that can carry two meanings, the phrase that can lean either way, the choice a translator had to make before interpretation even began. Some notes are a single sentence; others open onto the oldest arguments in the book.

How you use the layers is your business. If Sanskrit is new to you, read the gloss and let the Sanskrit sit overhead until it starts to look familiar, which it will. Slow down for the note whenever a verse you care about turns out to have one. And carry one thought from the start: any English line of this poem is already an interpretation. The gloss is this book's interpretation kept as thin as it can be made; the note shows you where the thicker ones fork off.

Sanskrit you can read

The marks of IAST look forbidding and are not. They do three small jobs. A bar above a vowel, as in ā, ī, ū, lengthens it: ā is held about twice as long as a, and the difference can turn one word into another. Beneath a consonant, a dot, as in ṭ, ḍ, ṇ, sends the tongue to a place English never asks it to go. And ś and ṣ record two distinct hushing sounds that English flattens into one sh. None of the marks is decoration; each one preserves a distinction the poem hears and this book does not want to blur.

You can say these words aloud if you want to, roughly, from the Roman letters alone, and roughly is enough for reading. The marks exist to make pronunciation exact for those who want exactness, not to fence you out. Read karmaṇi as if it were Italian and you will land close enough. Nobody is grading you, least of all this book.

What surprises new readers more than the marks is the joining. Sanskrit fuses neighboring words at their seams, and the grammarians gave the fusing a name: sandhi, "joining." When one word ends in a vowel and the next begins with one, the two vowels press together and come out as a single sound. Short words vanish into their neighbors this way, and a phrase you might expect to meet as three separate words arrives as one ribbon of letters.

Here is the worked example, from the poem's most translated line, the one the captions number 2.47. The Sanskrit opens with one ribbon: karmaṇyevādhikāraste. Unfused, the ribbon is three words. Karmaṇi means "in the action." Eva is an emphasizing particle; the gloss renders it as "alone." A short verb form at the end means "is your right." Now watch the seams close. The final i of karmaṇi and the opening e of eva press together and come out as ye, which gives karmaṇyeva. Then the final a of eva presses into the opening a of the verb, and the pair becomes a single long ā. Three words, two seams, one breath.

The gloss performs the reverse operation every time. "Your right is to the action alone": the action carries karmaṇi, alone carries eva, your right carries the verb. You are never asked to do the unfusing yourself. And this book leaves the Sanskrit joined exactly as the received editions print it, seams and all, because that is how the poem arrives. When a line looks like several words melted into one, that is not an error. It's the language's own habit, and the gloss is where it gets untangled.

The verse form

Nearly the whole poem is built from one verse form. A śloka is a couplet: two lines, each line split again into two halves, four units in all before the verse closes. The couplet runs on a counted rhythm of syllables, which you may half-hear as a pulse even in Roman letters, and its halves often answer or balance one another, a statement and its turn. In this book's verse blocks the couplet is printed as one band of letters with a vertical bar standing at the fold between its two halves, so you can always see where the verse turns. Read the bar as a breath mark and you will move at something like the poem's own pace.

The size of the poem is usually given as seven hundred ślokas. Treat that number as the first thing this book declines to settle. The Sanskrit edition these pages quote from parses to 701, because it splits one verse of the thirteenth chapter into two. An edition printed at Kumbakonam carries 745. This book follows the received numbering, the one the captions count by, and reports the variants instead of smoothing them away. If readers cannot even agree on how many verses they are reading, you can imagine what happens to the meanings.

One habit worth forming now: when the prose of this book cites a verse, the citation is an address. 2.47 is not a decimal; it is chapter two, verse forty-seven. Every claim you read from here on can be checked against that address, and the block you find there will always show you the same three layers.

How to argue with this book

Three rules govern everything this book says about meaning, and you should have them before page two. First: every interpretation carries a name. The sentence shapes never change: Shankara reads this as..., Gandhi called it..., Ambedkar charged that... When a monk, an activist, and a critic look at the same line and see three different poems, you will be shown all three, each with its reader's name attached. If you ever catch a claim about the poem's meaning floating free of any name, distrust it. Unattributed meaning is exactly what this book exists to prevent.

Second: disagreements are shown, not settled. When two readers split over a verse, you get both readings, side by side, in the same paragraph, each with its name on it. The book does not rank them, does not hint at a favorite. Its silence about winners is not an oversight; it is the design. A book that crowned one reading of this poem would only be one more contestant dressed up as a referee.

Third: the silence is not a shrug. To say that every reading is equally good would itself be a verdict, and verdicts are what this book has forsworn. What the silence means instead is that the judging is yours. You are being handed the poem, the gloss, the notes, the named readers and their quarrels, and at the end a quiet stretch of pages, the part called Your Reading, where the verdict you reach, if you reach one, will be yours rather than the book's.

The named readers arrive in Part Three: scholars of the classical schools, activists of the modern age, critics who put the poem itself on trial. Before them comes the poem. And before the poem, the frame: a blind king, a reporter with divine sight, and two armies drawn up on a field. The next page takes you there.

Takeaways

  1. Every verse appears in three layers: IAST text, a literal gloss, and a translation note where translators disagree.
  2. IAST is Sanskrit in Roman letters with marks that make pronunciation unambiguous.
  3. A śloka is a couplet of two lines, each split into two halves; the Gita runs to seven hundred of them.
  4. Interpretations are always attributed; this book never declares a winner.
