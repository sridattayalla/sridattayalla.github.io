Ambedkar's Indictment

Ambedkar read the same poem and saw the machine: a divine decree for the fourfold order, and a doctrine that tells the oppressed their station is deserved.

You left Gandhi with the war moved indoors — the armies as impulses, the poem his spiritual reference book, the allegory's cost named and paid. The interior war now meets its prosecutor. A reader born under the order the frame takes for granted was never going to allegorize it away: the social machinery was the whole point, and this poem stood accused of building it.

The jurist

Pin the man first, because the man is the reading. Bhimrao Ambedkar lived from 1891 to 1956, born into a community the order ranked as untouchable, and he fought that order in the languages it could not refuse him: law, economics, constitutions. He chaired the committee that drafted independent India's constitution, the document that outlawed untouchability outright. And he closed his life a Buddhist, taking the Buddha's vows in public before the following he had gathered. That last fact is the key to his Gita: the book he put on trial was, on his account, written to answer the teacher he ended up following.

The quarrel with Gandhi was the era's great argument, and it ran in print. Ambedkar wrote an address for a reform society that then withdrew its invitation rather than hear it; he published the speech anyway, as Annihilation of Caste, and Gandhi answered it in his journal; Ambedkar printed a reply to the reply. Mark the shape of that exchange, because it is the shape of this page: the two men were not disputing what a verse means. Gandhi held the scriptures authoritative and the caste order a corruption of them — a division of work misread into a division of birth. Ambedkar's position cut below interpretation altogether: the texts were the charter of the hierarchy, and a book that decrees the four orders forfeits the standing Gandhi granted it. The quarrel was over scripture's authority, not just its meaning.

His case against the Gita reached print after him. It sits in Riddles in Hinduism, drafted through the 1950s, as an essay titled like an indictment: a philosophic defence of counter-revolution, with Krishna and his Gita as the defendants. Of this book's modern readings it is the sharpest, written by a reader with nothing to protect, and it gets what every lens here gets: stated at full strength, answered from inside the poem, ranked by no one.

His Gita

The thesis is in his title. The Gita, Ambedkar charges, is a philosophic defence of counter-revolution: Krishna marshaled to uphold the varna order against Buddhist-era egalitarianism. He dates the poem after the rise of Buddhism and reads it as a Brahmanical response to the Buddha's challenge. On his account the birth order had lost the argument that mattered — a movement with no ranks in it had drawn the ranked world's best minds — and the Gita is the counterstroke: the era's most persuasive philosophy conscripted, its doctrines of karma, strand and self fitted onto the old machine like armor onto a cart.

Hold the word conscripted; it does the real work. The claim is not only that the poem is wrong; it is that the poem was built. The Krishna who teaches is, in Ambedkar's account, a retrofit: a divine voice installed into an older argument so that its conclusions arrive carrying a god's authority. The installation's capstone is the verse where Krishna tells Arjuna the slain are already slain — be my mere instrument, nimitta, in a slaughter decided before the first arrow flies. Ambedkar files that as moral evasion dressed in metaphysics: the warrior keeps his duty, the killing stays off his account, and both comforts serve the exact war the order needed fought. A poem that manages that, his reading implies, did not assemble itself by accident.

The verse on trial

At the center of the case stands the verse this book has already walked. The chapter 4 page spent its paragraphs on 4.13 — the order, the sorting phrase, the maker who disclaims making — and that walk stands; nothing here re-litigates it. Ambedkar's trial asks a different question: what the sentence does, read as written, in a world of graded rank. Here is the verse again, entered as evidence.

The plain reading is the prosecution's. Read the first half and stop. The four-varna order, made by me. A decree. The words that trail it — sorted according to strand and action — name a sorting method, not an exit, and a god who says he made the four orders has done everything a hierarchy requires: the order comes with a maker. The verse needed no commentary to do its work in the world; it did that work for centuries exactly as written.

The softening glosses are the defense's first exhibit; you have met them all. Shankara reads the orders as sorted by quality and, at the summit, not finally real at all; Ramanuja reads a real order sorted vocationally, by function; the devotional line reads the same two words as a door slammed on birth-right — the make and work of a soul fixing its station, never its parentage. On these readings the verse is a sieve, not a chain. The prosecution answers with arithmetic. The sorting phrase leans on the strands — the three guṇas you walked in chapter 14 — and the strands are three while the orders are four: three bins cannot shelve four crates, and no commentary, the charge runs, has ever repaired the arithmetic.

Then the corroborating witnesses, all from inside the poem. Krishna warns Arjuna not to unsettle the minds of the ignorant with knowledge they cannot bear (3.26): do not disturb the settled, the indictment hears. And the rule that installs the umpire takes the stand next: let scripture be your measure for what is to be done and not done (16.24), the verse the chapter 16 page promised to this trial — a rule that hands the final word to whoever controls the reading of the rule. And the closing settlement distributes the works of the four orders by the strands of each one's own nature (18.41 through 18.48), duties fixed by svabhāva, the nature a person arrives carrying. That word nature, on the plain reading, is where birth hides in this poem: who you were born to decides what you are made of, and what you are made of decides what you may do. The glosses say sieve; the indictment says chain; and the verse itself, as always, offers only the words it printed.

His deeper charge

Below the verse sits the doctrine, and the doctrine is the deeper charge. Ambedkar's case against karma is not that it is false; it is that it is exactly the theology an order of graded rank would commission. The act and its echo — the ledger Part 1 handed you — prices every birth as the settled verdict on a past life. On this doctrine the suffering are told their suffering is earned; the station you hold is a receipt for the person you were. No conqueror imposed the order on you. You built it, in an earlier body, before you were born into it.

And the doctrine's cruelest clause, on his reading, is its consolation. The ledger stays open, the next entry is yours to write, endure this life well and the next one improves. Under that accounting, revolt is not repair; it is bad bookkeeping — the disordering of an account that was settling itself toward balance. Set the charges side by side and the indictment stands complete: a god who decrees the order, a doctrine that prices the order's injuries as earned, and a teacher installed to carry both inside the era's most persuasive poem. Say it at that strength: he did, and this book does not soften prosecutors. Then hear the defense, from inside the poem.

The counter-case

The defense's first exhibit is the clause the plain reading skips. Sorted according to strand and action, read as criteria, names what a person is and does — never the parent they were born to. On that reading the order admits motion: souls find their level by make and function, and the verse is a sieve. The counter-case does not apologize for the ambiguity; it points straight at it. The fork is the battlefield, and you have been standing on it since the chapter 4 page.

The second exhibit is the open door. In the poem's most secret chapter, Krishna says that those who take refuge in him — even those born of ill wombs, women, vaishyas, shudras — even they go the highest way. You read the chapter 9 page and its two futures: the devotional line built its house on that even, birth barring no one; the indictment reads a concession — ill wombs named as a settled category, the exception priced against the rule. Both readings stand in the record. And so does the plain fact: the poem gathered the excluded by name and handed them its highest promise.

The third exhibit is the poem's own tensions. The same text that decrees the four orders teaches that the learned see the same self in a brahmin of learning and courtesy, a cow, an elephant, a dog — and in a dog-cooker. The same poem that warns against unsettling the ignorant spent chapter 2 telling a warrior he could hear the deepest truth. And you already hold the historians' third answer, that the fourfold verse is a later insertion into an older poem — a defendant's plea that the trial has charged the wrong document. None of these exhibits acquits; none convicts. This book shows both sides and decides nothing — the rule on Shankara's page, the rule on Gandhi's, and it holds hardest here, where the stakes are the realest: whose suffering the poem underwrites, and whose it unmakes.

A last reader waits outside, and he is not a judge. A mythologist will pick the poem up, walk it past the bench, and carry it into the folk pantheon — the country of stories where Krishna has been living all along, whatever the courts decide. That walk is next.

Takeaways

  1. The charge: the Gita is the theological defense of the social order — the four-varna verse read plainly.
  2. The deeper charge: karma tells the oppressed their station is deserved.
  3. The counter-reading exists inside the text itself: strands-and-actions as criteria, not birth — the ambiguity is the battlefield.
  4. The quarrel with Gandhi was over scripture's authority, not just its meaning — this book shows both and decides nothing.
