The Forks II: The Questions That Never Close

Three questions the poem opens and never closes: can a good person be an instrument of violence, does the machine-driver leave us free, and what does it mean to see a scholar and a dog as the same?

The last page set four verses side by side and found the readers to be the variable: the same line honestly read as devotion, knowledge, or action; the four-varna verse and the open-door verse pulling opposite directions inside one poem; the refuge verse meaning surrender to one school and clarity to another — and no verdict anywhere, because the verdict was never this book's to assign. This page asks a different question about the same forks. Not which road is right, but why the roads never close — why a fork walked for centuries keeps forking.

The instrument

The question about violence comes from one of the poem's strongest lines, spoken just after the vision has shown Arjuna everything:

Read the last clause on its plain face, because the plain face is where soldiers have lived with this verse for centuries. The deaths across the line are already authored — by me these have already been slain, Krishna says, before the first arrow flies. The archer's role shrinks to nimitta-mātra, a mere instrument, an occasion. To a fighter ordered to kill, that has been a working comfort: the author of the slaughter is not the hand doing the slaughtering, and the moral weight shifts off the bow arm. The verse says something stronger than comfort — the killing is finished, and you are late to a war already won — and solace of that size has been drawn from it ever since.

Gandhi refused exactly this. He kept the Gita nearer than any other book — his spiritual reference book, he called it, his mother — and he declined to let it counsel a war. His reading states its frame plainly: the Gita is not an historical work; the battle is the duel that perpetually went on in the hearts of mankind; the fighting worth doing is the soul's war against its own passions. On that reading the instrument verse licenses no arrow at any cousin. The comfort Gandhi took from the poem was real; the plain frame the verse arrives in, he set aside entire.

Ambedkar heard the same line and reached for the indictment. In the riddles he drafted in the 1950s, he judged that Krishna ends by making Arjuna a mere nimitta in a pre-decided slaughter — a moral evasion: a god who has already killed everyone conscripts a man to look away while it is done in his name. Where the soldier hears absolution, the historian hears a receipt. The translation note above carries his own phrase for this verse, and the phrase is not gentle.

And the verse does not choose among its readers. It offers the comfort and invites the evasion in one breath, and the poem sends it out without a footnote. The question it opens is the first one this page promised: can a good person be an instrument of violence — and remain, in the poem's own eyes, good? Readers have taken every available answer across the centuries; the verse has endorsed none. That is not the question failing. That is the question working.

The machine

The second question is planted near the poem's exit, and it is determinism's sharpest image in the whole text:

The Sanskrit for the machine is yantra, and the verse's grammar deserves a slow pass. The lord — īśvara — dwells in the hearts of all beings, at the controls of the very seat they steer by. All beings are mounted like passengers in a cart, and whirled, and the turning is done by his māyā. However that last word is read, the image on its face is blunt. You are ridden. You are turned. The driver is inside you.

Then the poem does something that should not work. Two verses after mounting every being on a machine, Krishna finishes the teaching and hands over the keys. His final counsel, in paraphrase: the knowledge has been taught to you; reflect on it fully, and do as you choose. That is verse 63, spoken on the poem's way out the door, and its grammar is not the machine's grammar. It does not say you will choose as you are turned to choose. It says do as you choose — and hands the whole poem, machine and all, to the man it has just finished mounting.

Readers have tried to file this as an accident of assembly, and the structure keeps refusing. The machine verse and the choosing verse stand a breath apart, in one voice — and the counsel that follows the machine immediately is refuge, the poem offering itself as the exit from the turning it has just described. The tension is not a seam some editor missed; it is load-bearing.

Every major lens walks through that exit, and no two agree on what the machine was. Shankara's advaita dissolves the turning into māyā, ignorance's cover over a self that was never mounted; the exit is knowledge. Ramanuja's viśiṣṭādvaita keeps the dependence real — the self is a mode of brahman, never dissolved — and reads the exit as prapatti, the surrender the carama śloka commands. Madhva's dvaita hardens the difference: the soul is God's dependent forever. Bhaktivedanta's Gita, traced through the paramparā, takes the machine at face value and the exit as surrender to the supreme person. Same door, four maps. Behind all of them stands the question the verse opens: does the driver leave us free? The poem needs both halves — a machine with no chooser is a text with no one to teach, and a chooser with no machine has nothing to be freed from.

The equal gaze

The third question is the quietest and does the most work in the world. It sits early, in the chapters on the discipline of action, and makes the boldest claim about seeing anywhere in the text:

Run the list slowly, because the order is the argument. A brahmin complete in learning and courtesy — the top of the poem's world. A cow, an elephant — the animals that world holds in reverence. A dog. And then the word the verse saves for last: a dog-cooker, śvapāka, an outcaste occupation, the bottom of the order the poem elsewhere takes for granted. The learned — paṇḍitāḥ, the poem's word for those who know — see the same across the whole drop. Not similar: the same, spent on all five without distinction.

Gandhi took the verse as a charter. On his reading — the same reading that moved the battle inside the chest — what is seen alike in the scholar and the dog is the self; the equality of souls is not a policy he happened to hold but a fact the poem states about what you are. From that fact he drew a life's practice: the gaze that sees the same must become, in the seer, the treatment that does the same. The verse was, to him, sufficient.

Ambedkar did not dispute that the verse says what it says. He disputed its reach, and his objection fits in one line: metaphysics is not sociology. The equal gaze levels what the seer sees; it does not touch the order the seer lives in — and the same book that commands the gaze also divides human beings into an order of four and settles each one's duties by inborn nature, the verses he put at the center of his charge. His arithmetic was as blunt: the strands are three and the orders are four, so the theory cannot ground the order it defends. On his reading the poem is a philosophic defence of counter-revolution, and the equal gaze is its most beautiful ornament — real, and inert. Seeing alike is not yet treating alike.

So the question stands where the poem left it. What does the equal gaze require? Is the seeing the whole instruction — or does it oblige the seer to remake the society the verse declines to remake? Gandhi answered with his life; Ambedkar, with his indictment. The poem answers with the verse and its neighbors, the gaze in one hand and the birth-settled duties in the other. Where you place them decides what your Gita is.

Three questions, then, none of them closed. Whether a good person can be an instrument of violence. Whether the driver of the machine leaves us free. What the equal gaze owes the dog-cooker. Each is still doing work: soldiers and pacifists, determinists and their refugees, reformers and indicters live inside these questions now, reading the lines you just read. A book that promised no verdicts kept the promise to its last fork. What the promise buys is not the comfort of an authority that would close the questions for you, but the questions themselves, which survive every answer they get. On the next page the loop closes — one walk through the whole poem, from the anxious king's first question back to verse one read differently — and then the door out.

Takeaways

  1. The instrument verse has comforted soldiers and indicted generals — it does not choose.
  2. Beings are mounted on a machine, then addressed as free choosers — the tension is structural.
  3. The equal gaze was a charter to one reader and insufficient to another — seeing alike is not yet treating alike.
  4. Questions that survive centuries are not failures of reading; they are the engine.
