Chapter 2 (Part 2) — The Fork in the Road

Verse 2.39 splits chapter 2 in two: knowledge or disciplined action — and the most quoted verse in the poem stands at the gate between them.

The first movement of chapter 2 closed on a stance rather than an argument: make the pairs the same — pleasure and pain, gain and loss, victory and defeat — then take up the fight. Equal-mindedness was named as the exit, never explained. Verse 2.39 now names the discipline that produces it, and the chapter's second half opens the teaching Arjuna will actually walk.

Two disciplines announced

Listen for the hinge. Verse 2.38 told Arjuna to equalize the pairs and gird for battle; verse 2.39 looks back over everything since the opening rebuke — the deathless self, the shed garments, the lawful battle — files it under one label, and announces a second teaching beside it. Two words sit at the fork, and the poem spends both.

The first is sāṅkhya: literally a reckoning, an enumeration; by extension, the discipline of knowledge, the work of seeing through appearances to what is. The translation note beneath the verse is exact — sāṅkhya here means analysis, and it names everything Krishna has just finished doing. The second is buddhi-yoga, the yoking of intelligence to action. Buddhi is the faculty that discriminates and decides; yoga you have carried since the four-words pages. Compounded, the two name a trained discernment applied not to knowing but to doing.

One more word belongs on the table before the verse arrives, because the most famous line in the poem leans on it. adhikāra is a right, a claim, a jurisdiction: the authority that attaches to an office or a role rather than to an outcome. It is not a feeling of entitlement. It is a property line, and the famous verse is about to draw one.

Read the verse as a table of contents. This much has been set out to you by way of sāṅkhya: the self that does not die, filed under knowledge. Now hear it in yoga — yoked by this understanding, Partha, you will cast off the bond of action. Notice how strange the promise is: not you will escape action, not you will act and be rewarded — the bond of action, karma's tether, the act and its echo, is to be cut while the acting continues. Knowledge can see what does not die; it cannot by itself move a man's hands to a bow. For that, a discipline.

The fork has an afterlife: later readers map the two disciplines onto jñāna-yoga and karma-yoga, the way of knowledge and the way of action, with devotion waiting downstream to claim the whole map. The verse's own framing is smaller — not two roads for two kinds of seeker, but two halves of one answer to one man in one chariot. Arjuna has been shown what does not die; what he needs now is a way to pick up the bow.

The verse everyone knows

Now the gate verse — the line people who know nothing else of the Gita know.

Read it clause by clause; the clauses do different work. Your right is to the action alone, never to its fruits: adhikāra draws a property line across the act. You hold the office of the doing; the results — victory, heaven, the restored order — stand on the other side of the fence. The middle clause tightens: do not let the fruits be your motive — the act is not to become the cause-in-the-fruit, the note's own phrase. Then the clause every quotation drops: neither let there be attachment to inaction. The verse forecloses the exit Arjuna is sitting in. Detachment from fruits may not curdle into detachment from the field.

The translation note marks the stakes: whether this counsels detachment from outcome, selfless service, devotional service, or psychological non-clutching is the fork the whole book turns on. On every reading, what it gives is a division of labor: the act is yours; the aftermath is not.

The next verse hands the rule its engine.

Established in yoga, do your work — even in success and failure; that evenness is called yoga. First, a definition: the yoke word is re-set, and what it names now is samatva, evenness, the very stance the first movement closed on. What chapter 2 had commanded as an exit is named as the discipline itself. Then an address with an edge: Dhanañjaya, conqueror of wealth — a man being told to surrender the takings. The verse does not say feel nothing; it says stop letting the scoreboard steer.

The settled one

A rule stated is not yet a rule visible, and Arjuna's next move is the reader's: he asks to see one. The question introduces the chapter's most influential coinage — sthitaprajña, the one whose wisdom stands settled: sthita, standing firm; prajña, the discernment that decides. The word does not name someone who knows much; it names someone whose knowing holds under load. And notice what Arjuna does not ask: not what the settled one believes. He asks how the man speaks, sits, walks about: a question about conduct, answerable from outside.

That is the question's shape. The sthitaprajña is to be recognized the way you recognize a craftsman — by carriage, not creed. Krishna's answer begins, though, where no observer can look.

Before any word of speech or gait, the criterion is interior: he casts off all the desires of the mind — all of them, the verse stresses — and is content in the self alone through the self. The settled one is defined by subtraction first. The answer to how does he sit turns out to be: the way a man sits who wants nothing. But the portrait has a shadow side: what happens, link by link, in a mind that is not settled.

The chain of ruin

The portrait's dark twin arrives as causation.

Read it as psychology; the note says this chain is quoted in management and psychology writing as much as in theology. The entry point is attention — brooding on the objects of the senses. Not touching them: dwelling on them. Dwelling ferments into attachment, as the objects begin to belong to you in imagination. Attachment hardens into desire, wanting as a standing state. And when wanting is blocked — when the desired thing is withheld, threatened, or lost — its heat turns to anger. Every link is ordinary. You have watched each run on a smaller stage: the second look, the fixation, the craving, the flash when it is taken away.

Then the chain crosses from feeling into cognition. From anger comes delusion, the smearing of what is seen. From delusion, loss of memory — not failed recall, but the lost grip on who you are and what you came to do. From lost memory, the ruin of discernment: buddhi, the very faculty buddhi-yoga trains, wrecked. With discernment ruined, he is destroyed. Now run chapter 1 back through it. Arjuna asked to see the men opposite: attention. He looked at his own people and broke: attachment. By his own report the mind whirled, the limbs failed, the judgment went — the far links of this exact chain, arriving in the body of the finest archer of the age. The grief Krishna refused to soothe is the end of a causal run that began with a request to position a chariot.

The portrait closes where the psychology ends: with water.

As rivers enter a sea that stands full and unmoving, so all desires enter such a one — and he attains peace; not the desire-chaser. The sea does not dam the rivers, does not fight them, does not run dry. It is already full, and because it is full, the inflow moves nothing. That is the sthitaprajña's relation to wanting: desires still arrive, from every side, all day long; what has changed is the capacity to be moved. Every link of the chain — brooding, attachment, desire, anger — is a vacancy asking to be filled. The full sea is not a different behavior. It is the absence of the vacancy — and with no vacancy, the chain has nowhere to start.

A borrowed word

The chapter's last verse reaches for a word the poem does not own. nirvāṇa — literally a blowing-out, as a flame — is the Buddha's word for his goal: the extinguishing of craving, release from the round of rebirth. The Gita does not coin the word; it borrows it, from rivals. Scholars read the borrowing as one thread of a running conversation: the text dialogues with the Buddhist and Jain renunciate movements of its time, and its composition is placed roughly between the 5th and 2nd centuries BCE. The same conversation surfaces again at 6.16–17, where the poem's phrasing on moderation echoes the Buddhist middle way. The Gita is in conversation, not in a vacuum — and at the moment of borrowing it does something characteristic.

Read the compound the verse actually uses: brahmanirvāṇa, nirvāṇa in brahman. Not the bare blowing-out of the rival vocabulary, but an extinguishing located inside the reality the upaniṣads name. The borrowing is an argument in miniature. The freedom renouncers sought by leaving the world, the Gita here promises to a fighter standing in the middle of one — established in brahman, held, the verse insists, even at the hour of death. A chapter that opened with a rebuke, you grieve for what deserves no grief, ends by handing its hero a borrowed word for peace.

So chapter 2 stands complete: a claim about what dies, a case about the work a warrior is made for, and now a discipline — buddhi-yoga, governed by the most translated verse in the poem and made visible in a portrait of the man it produces. But a rule this stark invites the obvious objection, and the poem knows it. If the fruits are not yours, why lift a finger at all? Chapter 3 takes that objection head-on, first in Arjuna's mouth, then in Krishna's answer.

Takeaways

  1. Verse 2.39 announces two paths — the fork every later reader maps onto.
  2. Your right is to the act alone, never to its fruits: the most translated line in the poem.
  3. The portrait of the settled one makes the teaching visible as conduct, not creed.
  4. The chain of ruin gives the mechanism: attachment hardens into delusion.
  5. The chapter's last word, nirvāṇa, is borrowed from the poem's Buddhist rivals — the Gita is in conversation, not in a vacuum.
