The Cast of the War

Two cousins lead armies of cousins; the charioteer is a god in disguise; and every fighter on the field carries a role the poem will name without explaining.

You know the listeners already: Sanjaya, the reporter granted distant sight, and Dhritarashtra, the blind king whose one spoken verse opens the poem and whose first word in it is "mine". This page introduces the people being reported on, two branches of a single family drawn up for a war of succession. What they are to each other is the engine of everything the Gita is about to say.

One family, two arrays

In one array stand the five Pandava brothers: Yudhishthira the eldest, Bhima the strongest, Arjuna the archer, and the twins Nakula and Sahadeva. In the other stand a hundred Kaurava cousins, the sons of the blind king, led by the eldest of them, Duryodhana. The quarrel between them is the quarrel of the whole Mahabharata: a kingdom, two branches of one family, and no way left of splitting it that does not involve armies. By the time the poem begins, the family has tried everything else.

Now the detail that matters most to this poem. Fighting for the Kaurava side are the two men who raised and trained the Pandavas: Bhishma, the grandsire of both lines and commander of the Kaurava armies, and Drona, the old master of arms who taught Arjuna the bow in the first place. There too is Karna, Arjuna's secret half brother. When Arjuna asks, in the poem's first chapter, to be placed where he can see who is actually out there, the answer is a roll-call of faces: the grandfather, the teacher, the cousins, the kinsmen, name after name for the better part of twenty verses (1.8 to 1.25). His collapse a few verses later is not about strangers. These are the people who raised him, taught him, and blessed his marriages, and the epic has spent whole books making its audience love them before asking it to watch him aim at them.

For all the crowd on the field, the Gita gives speaking parts to very few. The blind king speaks exactly one verse, the question that opens the poem. Sanjaya carries the narration, 41 verses in all, including the frame at both ends. Arjuna speaks 84, most of them questions, objections, and honest reports of his own condition. Krishna speaks 574, which is most of the poem. Bhishma, Drona, Karna, Duryodhana, the rest of the hundred: named, armed, present, and silent. The epic's battlefield teaching is a conversation between two people, reported live by a third, for the ears of a fourth.

The archer and his driver

Start with the archer. Arjuna is the third of the five brothers, the family's supreme archer, and the brother on whom the Pandavas' hopes ride. His brothers matter, but the poem does not distribute itself evenly: when the talking starts, it is Arjuna the teacher addresses, Arjuna who objects, Arjuna who must be convinced. The student's part he takes seriously: he asks how a settled person actually speaks, sits, and moves; he admits his own mind is as hard to hold as wind; he says so, out loud, whenever a teaching confuses him. The man doing the answering is holding his reins.

Now the driver. Krishna is a prince of the Vrishni clan, and divine by reputation: the epic frame presents him as an incarnation of Vishnu, and the Gita will go much further than the frame, letting him speak as the supreme lord and, in the poem's most famous scene, show a face that terrifies the friend who asked to see it. Before the fighting began, the epic says, he offered both sides a choice. His armies could fight for whoever wanted them, or he himself could stand with whoever wanted him, unarmed and outside the combat altogether. Both sides chose. Duryodhana took the troops; Arjuna took the man. The greatest archer of the age rides to war behind a noncombatant, and the poem you are reading is what the noncombatant says.

One fact completes the picture: Krishna has vowed not to fight. He drives, he advises, he answers, and he will not bear weapons on this field. Hold that in view as the poem unfolds, because it shapes everything said in it. The teacher carries no weapon; the student carries the best bow on the field; the conversation happens across exactly that gap.

The fourfold order

One more piece of background remains, and it is the piece the poem will lean on hardest. The world of the epic sorts human work into four large categories, and the Gita will invoke that sorting in several of its most consequential verses. The word for the sorting is varna: the fourfold social division of work. In the order the tradition itself counts them, the four are these. The brahmin is the priest and teacher, the keeper of ritual and learning. Next comes the kshatriya, the warrior and ruler, the one who fights and governs. Then the vaishya, the merchant, farmer, and herder, who produces and trades. Last in the counting, the shudra, the laborer and servant, whose work keeps everyone else standing.

Arjuna is a kshatriya; so is Duryodhana at the head of the opposite array; so is the blind king who set the war in motion. The poem knows this and uses it: one of the first arguments in Krishna's reply will be that a lawful war is the very battle a warrior should wish for. The field, though, is messier than the four categories. Drona is the standing complication, a teacher by calling who commands troops in his old age. A god holds the reins and carries no weapon. The sorting names kinds of work, and no life on this field, or anywhere else, sticks to exactly one.

Why does a battlefield conversation need this vocabulary at all? Because the cure the poem will offer for Arjuna's collapse runs straight through his role. It will tell him what a warrior is for, what work is his to do, and what happens to families and worlds when people abandon the work that is theirs, and a good part of what follows touches that nerve. So watch for the word. A verse in chapter four will say the four varnas were created by the divine himself. A verse in chapter nine will count women, merchants, and laborers, the vaishya and the shudra of the list, among those who can still take the highest way. The poem will rule, in its chapter of action and again in its final settlement, that your own work done imperfectly beats another's done well.

The fight over these verses is real, old, and unfinished. It runs from the classical commentators to B. R. Ambedkar, whose indictment of the poem gets its own chapter in Part 3 of this book. This page's job is smaller: you take delivery of the word here, so that the verses can arrive already introduced. What the verses say comes in Part 2; who has fought over them comes in Parts 3 and 4; no verdict comes at all.

The scale of the array

How large is the gathering? The epic counts its armies in a unit of its own, the akṣauhiṇī, a force reckoned in chariots, war elephants, horses, and foot soldiers held to a fixed ratio. Both sides field several such units apiece, and the epic is exact about the arithmetic, tabulating it down to the last horse and foot soldier. Those numbers are the next page's business. What matters here is the scale as a fact of feeling: not a skirmish between cousins but a mobilization, the sort of gathering that empties kingdoms, and the plain at Kurukshetra, a stretch of flat ground north of modern Delhi, holds all of it.

The Gita sits in the last quiet moment of that mobilization, just before the first arrow of an eighteen-day war. When the poem ends, the frame steps back in and the noise resumes: Bhishma sounds his conch, and the fighting begins. Everything the poem says, it says into the pause. Two armies stand at full strength; one archer is losing his grip; the driver speaks.

So the cast stands assembled on the field: a blind king listening in his palace, a reporter narrating what he alone can see, five brothers and a hundred cousins facing each other, a grandfather and a teacher on the far side of the line, a secret half brother with a bow, an archer whose arms are failing, and a god at the reins. What happens next is a conversation. It has a shape, eighteen chapters of it, and the next page of this book unfolds the map.

Takeaways

  1. The war is one family split in two: five Pandava brothers against a hundred Kaurava cousins.
  2. Arjuna is the Pandavas' finest archer; Krishna has vowed not to fight and drives Arjuna's chariot.
  3. Varna is the fourfold social order of work: brahmin, kshatriya, vaishya, shudra.
  4. The Gita will lean on varna in verses that later readers fight over — meet the word now, judge it later.
