Gandhi's Gita: The Battle Within

Mohandas Gandhi read the battlefield as the human breast: the armies are impulses, the war is daily. He built that reading into a politics while conceding the plain fact underneath, that the poem, on its face, is about a war.

Vivekananda, Tilak and Aurobindo pulled the Gita into history: work as worship, the prison manifesto, the world-affirming synthesis. Mohandas Gandhi kept what they kept, the poem's call to act, and then he moved the field. Where the action-moderns made the Gita historical, he made it interior without losing the action.

The daily reader

Start with how he read, because the how is half the lens. Mohandas Gandhi lived from 1869 to 1948, and across the decades that matter he treated the Gita less as a book he had finished than as one he lived beside. He read it daily, in prison and out, in ashrams and on sea voyages, consulting it the way other people consult a dictionary, one passage pulled for a present need. The book earned two names from him, and both are exact: his spiritual reference book, and his mother. A reference book serves the word you lack at the moment; a mother serves before any question is asked. That was his order of reading, practice before theory, living before explaining, with theory dug out of the practice afterward.

The practice left a record with a date on it. In 1926, at his ashram, he worked through the poem in Gujarati with his fellow residents, verse by verse and talk by talk, and the talks were gathered as Anāsakti Yoga, The Gospel of Selfless Action, his translation and his commentary in one. His summary of the poem from those talks is among the most quoted sentences in the whole Gita literature: the Gita is not an historical work, he said; it represents the duel that perpetually went on in the hearts of mankind. Read that twice. Not the battle on the plain, then, but the duel inside a person, running before the book opens and after it closes, with the poem as its field manual.

The allegory

Now the lens itself. Take the poem's first verse, the blind king's question: on the field of dharma, the field of the Kurus, gathered and eager to fight. You watched that opening compound split two ways on the walk, a place name and a pun. Gandhi takes the pun all the way down. The field of dharma is the body, the field you carry around, the ground on which what holds and what unravels contest your hours.

You have met this field before. Chapter 13 named the body the kṣetra, the field, and set the knower of the field inside it, and the walk left the two facing each other. Gandhi stages the entire war there. One army is drawn up from the base impulses, lust, anger, greed, delusion, arrogance, jealousy, everything in a person that wants and grabs; the other is everything that holds. Between the lines stands the chariot of the body, and in it Arjuna, who is the soul in crisis, and at the reins the charioteer who has vowed not to fight, the one seated in the heart of every being, who never touches the weapons he steers. The collapse of chapter 1 becomes, on this reading, the despair that comes over anyone about to move against their own habits: the mind produces excellent reasons for standing down, and the reasoning is fluent, frightened, and bent on keeping the impulse army exactly where it stands.

For the teaching at the center he reached for a word of his own: anāsakti, non-attachment, the renunciation of the fruit of action, not of action itself. Notice what he does not renounce. Shankara treated the discipline of works as a rung that knowledge would eventually dissolve; Gandhi keeps the climber, the ladder, and every rung, and the actor is never retired. The walk met the poem's own closing distinction between saṃnyāsa, laying down the act, and tyāga, laying down the claim on results, and Gandhi read the whole poem as a brief for the second, performed in the middle of ordinary life. A man can march, fast, and lift salt from the sea, and none of it requires him to own what comes of it.

His verse

The verse stands here a third time. It walked the chapter 2 pages clause by clause, and it stood on Shankara's page as a rung, and neither walk is re-litigated here. Set Gandhi's lens against the same words and watch what they become. Not a rung: an engine.

Read the first clause as a resister has to. Your right is to the action alone, never to its fruits. A campaign of nonviolent resistance is an act with the fruit lifted out of the actor's hands: you make the appeal, you take the blow, you accept the cell, and the one thing you cannot own is whether the appeal works. The opponent must convert. The law must bend. The empire must leave. None of that is yours, on this reading; what is yours is the next right act, and then the next.

This is why the verse could power a movement instead of consoling one. A resister attached to results has a deadline, and when the deadline passes, despair sets in, and despair curdles toward harsher means, since if the fruit is everything, harsher means promise it sooner. The resister who has released the fruit has no expiry date. Gandhi's word for the stance, satyagraha, holding to truth, names the posture exactly: the act held, the fruit released. His reading carried the verse far beyond Hindu readers and made it a slogan of selfless public service worldwide.

And he did not drop the clause that every quotation drops. Neither let there be attachment to inaction. A politics of the released fruit could have collapsed into withdrawal, because if outcomes are not mine, acting at all looks pointless; the verse forecloses that exit in its closing breath, and Gandhi's life reads as a commentary on the foreclosure. Here he stands nearest the activists of the previous page, and the engine is the difference between them. Tilak read the verse as a license for energetic action in history. Gandhi kept the energy and changed what an action is, from a lever pulled for an outcome to an offering made. The lever-puller is spent by failure. The offerer cannot be, because nothing was kept that could be lost.

The honest problem

Now the cost, and it has been sitting in view since the page's first sentence. The poem stages a war: named kings, a genealogy, conches, bows, a driver, a plain. The command is issued to a man holding weapons, and the arguments that carry it, a self that killing cannot touch, a warrior's duty with no better stage, are addressed to a fighter on a field. Gandhi never denied the letter of any of this. He conceded it. The poem, on its face, is about a war.

His answer is analogy. The Gita is not an historical work, he said; it is the perpetual duel in the human heart, wearing the largest war its hearers knew as its picture. As for the command to kill, his reply was that a teaching which ends in non-attachment cannot be walked to that end by way of slaughter, because the way and the end were, for him, one thing: killing cannot be the way to nonviolence. So the fighting is the soul's war on its own passions, and the warrior's discipline is the discipline of anyone holding a line against themselves.

The charge against the answer is that it is not one. The frame does not read like a picture. The blind king asks about real sons, the epic around the poem counts its dead at the end, and Krishna's arguments have bodies in view. On this account Gandhi has not interpreted the frame so much as declined it: he settled in advance what the poem may teach, moved the battlefield to fit, and loved a Gita he had partly built. His critics said so, and they were not all his enemies. He carried the cost more openly than most allegorists carry theirs, conceding the letter, keeping the spirit, and saying plainly which one he chose when the two pulled apart.

This book does not referee the exchange; Part 4 returns to it, where the forks that never close stand side by side and this one among them. And the page stays open here for a reason: the fiercest reader of the era read the same poem, refused nothing about its frame, and saw in it the machine of caste. That reading is next.

Takeaways

  1. Gandhi's Gita is interior: the war is the self's civil war, fought daily.
  2. The act-without-fruits verse became the engine of nonviolent resistance.
  3. He called the book his spiritual reference book and read it as a mother reads — practice before theory.
  4. The allegory's cost: it must overrule the poem's own frame — Gandhi accepted the cost.
